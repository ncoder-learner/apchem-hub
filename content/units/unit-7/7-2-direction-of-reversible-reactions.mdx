A reversible reaction can move toward products or toward reactants depending on the mixture's current composition. The direction is not determined by which side has more particles; it depends on whether the current mixture is at equilibrium.

<TopicLab unitId={7} topicNumber="7.2" title="Reaction Direction" />

<LessonSection title="1. Read net change, not a one-way arrow" description="The dominant direction depends on the current state" defaultOpen>

Before equilibrium, one direction has a greater rate than the other. If the forward rate is greater, products form faster than they are consumed. If the reverse rate is greater, reactants form faster than they are consumed. At equilibrium, the rates match.

A balanced equation describes stoichiometry, not which direction is currently faster. To predict direction quantitatively, compare the reaction quotient Q with the equilibrium constant K.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="2. Avoid the 'more particles' shortcut" description="Amount alone does not identify the net direction">

A mixture with a large product concentration can still shift forward if its current composition is below the equilibrium product-to-reactant ratio. Likewise, a mixture dominated by reactants can shift in reverse if its composition is above the equilibrium ratio. The reaction quotient provides the comparison.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: qualitative reasoning">
Suppose a reversible reaction has mostly reactants, but the current reaction quotient is smaller than K. Because Q < K, the mixture has too little product relative to reactants compared with equilibrium. The net reaction proceeds forward until Q reaches K.
</WorkedExample>

Their comparison tells whether the mixture has too much or too little product relative to equilibrium." />

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="4. Predict which direction is favored at a given moment" description="The current mixture and the reaction's tendency are separate ideas">

A reversible reaction can proceed in either direction. The net direction at a particular moment depends on the relative forward and reverse rates, which depend on the current concentrations or pressures. If reactants are added, the forward rate may temporarily increase; the system then changes composition until the rates match again. A reaction can be product-favored at equilibrium yet initially move in the reverse direction if the starting mixture contains too much product.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: distinguish direction from equilibrium amount">
Suppose \(A\rightleftharpoons B\) has an equilibrium constant greater than 1, so products are favored under the stated conditions. If a prepared mixture initially contains a very large amount of B and almost no A, the net reaction can still proceed left until Q falls to K. “Products favored” does not mean the reaction always moves right from every possible starting mixture.

</WorkedExample>

Use Q and K for precise direction predictions once those quantities are introduced: Q describes the current ratio; K describes the equilibrium ratio at that temperature.
