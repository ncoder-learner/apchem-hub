K is tied to a particular balanced equation and temperature. Algebraic changes to the reaction must be matched by corresponding changes to K.

<TopicLab unitId={7} topicNumber="7.6" title="Transform Equilibrium Constants" />

<LessonSection title="1. Reverse or scale a reaction" description="Make the same operation to the equation and K" defaultOpen>

- Reverse the reaction: K' = 1/K.
- Multiply every coefficient by n: K' = Kⁿ.
- Divide every coefficient by n: K' = K^(1/n).

The temperature must remain the same for these transformations. Changing temperature can change K itself.

<QuickCheck question="For A ⇌ B, K = 0.25. What is K for 2B ⇌ 2A?" options={["0.25","2","4","16"]} correctIndex={3} explanation="Reversing gives 1/0.25 = 4. Doubling the reversed equation squares the constant: 4² = 16." />

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="2. Add reactions by multiplying constants" description="Hess-like algebra for equilibrium expressions">

If two reactions are added to obtain a target reaction, multiply their equilibrium constants. Intermediate species cancel from the net equation and their factors cancel in the product of expressions.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: combine two reactions">
Reaction 1 has K₁ = 2 and reaction 2 has K₂ = 5. If the target is their sum as written, Ktarget = K₁K₂ = 10. If either reaction must be reversed or scaled first, transform its K before multiplying.
</WorkedExample>

<QuickCheck question="Two reactions are added as written. Their equilibrium constants are 0.40 and 3.0. What is K for the summed reaction?" options={["0.13","1.2","3.4","7.5"]} correctIndex={1} explanation="When reactions are added, their equilibrium expressions multiply: K = 0.40 × 3.0 = 1.2." />

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="4. Transform K when the equation changes" description="The balanced equation defines the numerical expression">

K depends on how the reaction is written. Reversing a reaction changes K to \(1/K\). Multiplying every coefficient by n raises K to the nth power. Adding reactions multiplies their equilibrium constants. These rules follow directly from the algebra of equilibrium expressions.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: combine two equilibria">
If reaction 1 has K₁ = 4.0 and reaction 2 has K₂ = 3.0, and their equations are added to form a target reaction, then \(K_{target}=K_1K_2=12\), provided the reactions are written in the directions used in the sum. If reaction 2 is reversed, use \(1/K_2=1/3.0\), so the combined K is \(4.0/3.0\).

</WorkedExample>

K changes with temperature because the relative favorability of reactants and products changes with thermal conditions. Changing concentration or pressure at constant temperature changes Q and the equilibrium composition, but not K.

<QuickCheck question="A balanced equation is doubled and its original K is 0.20. What is the new equilibrium constant?" options={["0.10","0.20","0.40","0.040"]} correctIndex={3} explanation="Doubling the equation squares the equilibrium expression, so Knew = (0.20)² = 0.040." />

</LessonSection>

## Readiness check

Reverse means reciprocal, scaling means exponentiation, and adding reactions means multiplying the adjusted constants.
