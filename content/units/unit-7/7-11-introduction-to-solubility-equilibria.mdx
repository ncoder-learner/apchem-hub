A sparingly soluble ionic solid can dissolve while dissolved ions recombine to form solid. In a saturated solution at equilibrium, these opposing processes occur at equal rates. The solubility product Ksp describes the equilibrium ion concentrations.

<TopicLab unitId={7} topicNumber="7.11" title="Solubility Product" />

<LessonSection title="1. Write Ksp from the dissolution equation" description="Omit the pure solid and exponentiate ion concentrations" defaultOpen>

For AgCl(s) ⇌ Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq), Ksp = [Ag⁺][Cl⁻]. For CaF₂(s) ⇌ Ca²⁺(aq) + 2F⁻(aq), Ksp = [Ca²⁺][F⁻]². The solid is omitted from the expression.

Ksp values are compared only with expressions for the same dissolution reaction and at the same temperature. A smaller Ksp often indicates lower solubility, but direct comparisons require attention to stoichiometry.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="2. Connect Ksp to molar solubility" description="Let x represent the amount dissolved">

For a 1:1 salt MX(s) ⇌ M⁺ + X⁻ in pure water, if molar solubility is s, then [M⁺] = s and [X⁻] = s, so Ksp = s² and s = √Ksp. For salts with other ratios, ion concentrations are multiples of s and the expression changes accordingly.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: 1:1 salt">
For AgCl in pure water, let s be the molar solubility. At equilibrium [Ag⁺] = [Cl⁻] = s, so Ksp = s². Therefore s = √Ksp. This shortcut is valid for the 1:1 stoichiometry and the stated pure-water conditions.
</WorkedExample>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="4. Connect Ksp to ion concentrations" description="The dissolution equation determines the powers in Ksp">

For a sparingly soluble salt MₐXᵦ(s)⇌ aMᵇ⁺ (aq) + bXᵃ⁻ (aq), the pure solid is omitted and Ksp = [Mᵇ⁺ ]^a[Xᵃ⁻ ]^b at a given temperature. The exponents come from the balanced dissolution equation. Ksp is not generally equal to molar solubility because one mole of salt may produce more than one mole of an ion.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: convert solubility to Ksp">
For CaF₂(s)⇌ Ca²⁺ (aq) + 2F⁻ (aq), let the molar solubility in pure water be s. Then [Ca²⁺] = s and [F⁻] = 2s, so Ksp = s(2s)^2 = 4s^3. The factor of 4 comes from the ion stoichiometry; using Ksp = s² would be incorrect.

</WorkedExample>

Compare the ion product Qsp with Ksp to predict precipitation. If Qsp > Ksp, the solution is supersaturated and precipitation is favored. If Qsp < Ksp, more solid can dissolve without reaching saturation. If Qsp = Ksp, the solution is at saturation equilibrium.
