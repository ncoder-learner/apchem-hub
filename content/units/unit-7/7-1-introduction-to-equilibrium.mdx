A sealed container of water reaches a point where the water level appears constant. Molecules still evaporate and condense, but the two processes occur at equal rates. Chemical equilibrium is dynamic in the same way: reactions continue at the particle level even when macroscopic concentrations stop changing.

<TopicLab unitId={7} topicNumber="7.1" title="Dynamic Equilibrium" />

<LessonSection title="1. Equilibrium means equal rates, not equal amounts" description="Forward and reverse reactions continue" defaultOpen>

For a reversible reaction, reactants form products and products can reform reactants. In a closed system, equilibrium is reached when the **forward reaction rate equals the reverse reaction rate**. Concentrations then remain constant over time.

Equilibrium does not require equal concentrations. A reaction may have mostly products or mostly reactants at equilibrium. The relative amounts depend on the reaction's equilibrium constant and conditions.

<LessonSection title="2. Identify what must remain constant" description="Stable concentrations are evidence of equilibrium">

In a concentration-versus-time graph, reactant concentrations may fall and product concentrations may rise before leveling off. A flat concentration curve indicates no net concentration change; it does not mean particles have stopped reacting.

Equilibrium requires the system's conditions to be stable and the reaction to be reversible. A continuously open system that loses products may not establish the same equilibrium state as a sealed system.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: interpret a concentration graph">
A graph shows [A] decreasing and [B] increasing. After 40 seconds, both curves become horizontal. The correct interpretation is that the forward and reverse reaction rates have become equal, so neither concentration has a net change. It is not valid to conclude that [A] = [B] unless the graph actually shows equal values.
</WorkedExample>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="3. Read equilibrium as a balance of opposing processes" description="A plateau in concentration is a net result, not molecular inactivity">

For A⇌ B, the forward rate may initially be large when A is abundant and the reverse rate small when little B is present. As B accumulates, the reverse rate increases; as A is consumed, the forward rate may decrease. At equilibrium the rates become equal. Both directions continue, and the concentrations remain constant because the two opposing changes balance.

The concentrations at equilibrium depend on the initial mixture and the reaction's equilibrium constant. A system can reach equilibrium with mostly reactants or mostly products. “Equal rates” is the defining condition; “equal concentrations” is not.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: interpret a concentration-time trace">
A closed mixture starts with 1.0 M A and no B. Later, [A] = 0.35 M and [B] = 0.65 M, and both curves flatten. The data are consistent with equilibrium, but the concentrations are unequal. The flat curves indicate no net change; they do not show that molecular reactions stopped.

</WorkedExample>

<LessonSection title="4. Connect concentration graphs to particle-level equilibrium" description="Flat concentration curves do not mean molecular motion has stopped">

Imagine the concentration curves for A and B becoming horizontal after an initial period. The graph shows that their **macroscopic concentrations are no longer changing**. In a closed reversible system, the microscopic forward and reverse reactions continue, but their rates are equal. A forward event that consumes A is balanced, on average, by reverse events that regenerate A.

For `A ⇌ B`, equilibrium could occur with `[A] = 0.10 M` and `[B] = 0.90 M`. The concentrations are not equal, yet they can remain constant because the forward and reverse rates are equal at those concentrations. The ratio is related to K for this reaction at that temperature, but the ratio alone does not tell you how quickly equilibrium was reached.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: interpret two flat curves">

A graph shows [A] decreasing from 1.0 M to 0.2 M while [B] increases from 0.1 M to 0.9 M; both curves then flatten. The correct conclusion is that the system has reached equilibrium under the stated closed-system conditions. It is not correct to conclude that A and B have equal concentrations, that both concentrations are zero, or that the reaction has stopped.

</WorkedExample>
