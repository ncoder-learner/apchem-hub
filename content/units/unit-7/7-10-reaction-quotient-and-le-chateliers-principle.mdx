Le Châtelier's principle predicts the direction of response, while Q provides the quantitative test. A disturbance changes the current reaction quotient; the net reaction proceeds until Q equals K again.

<TopicLab unitId={7} topicNumber="7.10" title="Disturbance and Q vs K" />

<LessonSection title="1. Recalculate Q after a change" description="K stays fixed unless temperature changes" defaultOpen>

At constant temperature, K does not change when concentration or volume is disturbed. Instead, the disturbance changes Q. If Q becomes smaller than K, the net reaction proceeds forward; if Q becomes larger than K, it proceeds in reverse.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: remove product">
For A(g) ⇌ B(g), suppose the mixture is at equilibrium and some B is removed. The numerator of Q decreases, so Q < K. Net forward reaction forms B until Q returns to K. The equilibrium constant did not change.
</WorkedExample>

<LessonSection title="2. Temperature is different" description="Heat can be treated as a reactant or product">

Changing temperature can change K because it changes the relative favorability of the forward and reverse processes. For an endothermic forward reaction, adding heat favors products and increases K; for an exothermic forward reaction, adding heat favors reactants and decreases K. This is the key distinction from concentration or pressure changes.

<QuickCheck question="Which disturbance can change the equilibrium constant K for a reaction?" options={["Adding more reactant at constant temperature","Removing product at constant temperature","Adding an inert gas at constant volume","Changing the temperature"]} correctIndex={3} explanation="K depends on temperature. Other disturbances can change Q and shift composition, but they do not change K when temperature remains constant." />

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="4. Use Q to verify a predicted shift" description="Le Châtelier's principle becomes quantitative when you calculate the ratio">

For concentration or pressure disturbances, the most reliable approach is to calculate the new Q immediately after the disturbance while holding the other species fixed as appropriate, then compare it with the unchanged K at the same temperature. If Q > K, net reaction proceeds left; if Q < K, net reaction proceeds right. This prevents memorized “shift toward the side you add” rules from being misapplied to complicated changes.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: add product">
For \(A\rightleftharpoons B\), K = 2.0. At equilibrium [A] = 0.50 M and [B] = 1.0 M. Adding B raises it to 1.5 M before reaction occurs, so the immediate quotient is Q = 1.5/0.50 = 3.0. Since Q > K, the system proceeds left, consuming B and forming A until Q returns to 2.0. The added B is not necessarily all consumed; it is the direction of net change that matters.

</WorkedExample>

At constant temperature, changing concentration or volume changes Q but not K. Changing temperature changes K, so the old equilibrium ratio is no longer the target.
