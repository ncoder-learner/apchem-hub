Equilibrium can be represented by a particle-level picture, a concentration-versus-time graph, or a mathematical expression. Each representation shows different evidence; none should be read as if it were another.

<TopicLab unitId={7} topicNumber="7.8" title="Represent Equilibrium" />

<LessonSection title="1. Read particle diagrams as counts" description="Count species and account for stoichiometry" defaultOpen>

A particle diagram represents relative numbers of particles in a chosen sample. At equilibrium, forward and reverse reaction events occur at equal rates, so the average species counts remain stable. A single snapshot does not show rates directly; repeated observations or a model is needed to infer dynamic equilibrium.

Do not assume that a diagram with equal numbers of reactant and product particles means K = 1. The balanced equation and the correct expression matter.

<LessonSection title="2. Translate a graph into a chemical claim" description="Plateaus mean concentrations are constant">

On a concentration-time graph, equilibrium is indicated by concentrations reaching plateaus. The plateaus may occur at different heights. If a system is disturbed, concentrations can change again before settling at a new equilibrium.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: interpret a disturbance">
A product concentration is constant, then suddenly decreases when some product is removed. The system is no longer at equilibrium. The mixture typically responds by forming more product until it reaches a new equilibrium, provided the temperature and reaction system remain suitable.
</WorkedExample>

<QuickCheck question="Which feature on a concentration-time graph most directly indicates equilibrium?" options={["Reactant and product curves cross","Every curve has the same height","Species concentrations become constant over time","The product curve must be above the reactant curve"]} correctIndex={2} explanation="Equilibrium corresponds to constant macroscopic concentrations. The concentrations need not be equal or ordered in any particular way." />

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="4. Translate between equations, tables, particles, and graphs" description="Every representation has strengths and limits">

A concentration-time graph can show when the system reaches a plateau and how the composition changes, but it does not by itself reveal the microscopic mechanism. An ICE table quantifies the stoichiometric change from initial to equilibrium values. A particle diagram shows relative numbers of particles but must be read carefully: equal numbers of drawn particles may represent equal concentrations only if the volumes and scale are comparable.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: reconcile a graph with an ICE table">
For A⇌ B, a graph begins with [A] = 0.80 M and [B] = 0.20 M and levels off at [A] = 0.50 M and [B] = 0.50 M. The change is −0.30 M A and +0.30 M B, consistent with a 1:1 reaction. Then K_c = [B] / [A] = 1.0 for those equilibrium values. The flat region identifies equilibrium; the ratio gives K.

</WorkedExample>

When interpreting an experiment, state what the representation directly shows and what conclusion follows from a chemical model. Avoid claiming that a diagram proves a mechanism or that a flat line means reaction has stopped.
