When an equilibrium mixture is disturbed, its composition changes as the system approaches equilibrium again. Le Châtelier's principle is a useful qualitative summary, but the reaction quotient and the balanced equation explain why the response occurs.

<TopicLab unitId={7} topicNumber="7.9" title="Le Châtelier's Principle" />

<LessonSection title="1. Predict the response to concentration changes" description="The system consumes part of an added species" defaultOpen>

Adding a reactant often drives net reaction toward products; removing a reactant often drives it toward reactants. Adding product often drives net reaction in reverse; removing product often drives net reaction forward. These predictions assume the temperature is unchanged, so K remains constant.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="2. Treat pressure changes through gas moles" description="Only certain volume changes change the equilibrium composition">

For a gas equilibrium, decreasing volume raises partial pressures. At constant temperature, the system tends to shift toward the side with fewer moles of gas if the two sides have different gas coefficients. If both sides have the same total gas stoichiometric amount, a volume change does not favor either side by this rule.

Adding an inert gas at constant volume does not change the reacting gases' partial pressures, so it does not shift the equilibrium under ideal-gas assumptions.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: volume decrease">
For N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g), the reactant side has four moles of gas per stoichiometric reaction and the product side has two. Compressing the mixture at constant temperature favors the product side, which has fewer gas moles.
</WorkedExample>

<LessonSection title="4. Predict shifts by identifying the disturbance" description="The system responds in a direction that reduces the imposed change">

Le Châtelier's principle is a qualitative summary of how an equilibrium system responds to a disturbance. Adding a reactant often increases Q relative to K, causing net reaction toward products; removing a product often has the same directional effect. Removing a reactant or adding product usually drives net reaction toward reactants. These predictions describe the shift, not necessarily the final concentration compared with its original value.

For gas equilibria, changing volume at constant temperature changes partial pressures. Compression favors the side with fewer moles of gas; expansion favors the side with more gas, when the numbers differ. If both sides have equal gas stoichiometric totals, changing volume does not favor either side through that mole-count effect.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: pressure change">
For N₂(g) + 3H₂(g)⇌2NH₃(g), the left side has four moles of gas and the right side has two. Compressing the container at constant temperature increases pressure, so the system shifts right toward fewer gas particles. K remains constant because temperature is unchanged.

</WorkedExample>

A catalyst is not an equilibrium disturbance: it speeds both directions and does not shift the equilibrium composition. Temperature is different because it changes K.
