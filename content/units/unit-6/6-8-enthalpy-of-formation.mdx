The standard enthalpy of formation, ΔH°f, is the enthalpy change when one mole of a compound forms from its constituent elements in their standard states. An element in its standard state has ΔH°f = 0 by convention. This does not mean every allotrope or physical form of an element has zero enthalpy of formation; the standard state matters.

<LessonSection title="1. Use the products-minus-reactants structure" description="Coefficients multiply formation enthalpies" defaultOpen>

```text
ΔH°rxn = Σ nΔH°f(products) − Σ nΔH°f(reactants)
```

Multiply each substance's ΔH°f by its balanced-equation coefficient. Add the product terms, add the reactant terms, then subtract the reactant total from the product total. Keep units consistent, usually kJ/mol of reaction.

<LessonSection title="2. Calculate a reaction enthalpy" description="A reliable sign convention prevents reversed results">

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: formation enthalpies">

For a hypothetical reaction A(g) + 2B(g) → C(g), suppose ΔH°f values are A = −20, B = +10, and C = −100 kJ/mol. Products total = −100 kJ/mol. Reactants total = (−20) + 2(+10) = 0 kJ/mol. Thus ΔH°rxn = −100 − 0 = **−100 kJ/mol**.

</WorkedExample>

<LessonSection title="3. Check states and coefficients" description="Thermodynamic tables are state-specific">

Use formation enthalpies for the phases shown in the equation, such as H₂O(l) versus H₂O(g). Do not omit a coefficient or assume a compound's formation enthalpy is zero. If a reaction equation is scaled, the calculated ΔH for that equation scales too.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="4. Use standard formation data with coefficients and states" description="Formation enthalpy makes one mole of a substance from elements in standard states">

A standard enthalpy of formation, ΔHf°, is the enthalpy change when exactly one mole of a substance forms from its constituent elements in their standard states. The standard formation enthalpy of an element in its standard state is zero by convention. For a reaction, ΔHrxn° = Σ nΔHf°(products) - Σ nΔHf°(reactants). Multiply each value by its coefficient and retain the table's signs.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: ammonia formation">
For N₂(g) + 3H₂(g)→2NH₃(g), suppose ΔHf°[NH₃(g)] = - 46.1 kJ·mol⁻¹ and elemental values are zero. Then ΔH° = 2( - 46.1) - [0 + 3(0)] = - 92.2 kJ for the equation as written. It forms two moles of NH₃, so per mole formed the enthalpy is −46.1 kJ·mol⁻¹.

</WorkedExample>

Check phase labels: H₂O(l) and H₂O(g) have different formation enthalpies because vaporization carries energy. Using the wrong state can shift the result substantially.

<LessonSection title="5. Build and scale a standard formation reaction" description="Formation data are defined for exactly one mole of compound">

A standard enthalpy of formation, `ΔHf°`, is the enthalpy change for forming exactly one mole of a compound from its constituent elements in their standard states. The formation equation must produce one mole of the compound, even if fractional coefficients are required. The standard formation enthalpy of an element in its standard state is zero by convention; this does not mean that every allotrope or physical state has zero enthalpy relative to every other form.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: formation of ammonia">

The formation equation is `½N₂(g) + 3/2 H₂(g) → NH₃(g)`. If `ΔHf°[NH₃(g)] = −46.1 kJ·mol⁻¹`, this value applies to one mole of NH₃ formed. For `N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) → 2NH₃(g)`, the reaction enthalpy is `2(−46.1) = −92.2 kJ`. The standard-state elements N₂(g) and H₂(g) each have `ΔHf° = 0`.

</WorkedExample>

In a reaction calculation, multiply each substance's `ΔHf°` by its stoichiometric coefficient, sum products, and subtract the corresponding reactant sum. Always use the specified physical states: `H₂O(l)` and `H₂O(g)` have different formation enthalpies because they are different thermodynamic states.

<QuickCheck question="If ΔHf°[CO₂(g)] = −393.5 kJ·mol⁻¹, what is ΔH° for 2C(graphite) + 2O₂(g) → 2CO₂(g)?" options={["−393.5 kJ","−196.8 kJ","−787.0 kJ","+787.0 kJ"]} correctIndex={2} explanation="The reaction forms two moles of CO₂ from elements in their standard states, so ΔH° = 2(−393.5) = −787.0 kJ." />

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="6. Build a formation-enthalpy calculation from a balanced equation" description="The standard state and coefficient for every species matter">

For a balanced reaction, calculate ΔH°rxn = ΣνΔH°f(products) − ΣνΔH°f(reactants). The coefficient ν multiplies each species' formation enthalpy. Elements in their standard states have ΔH°f = 0, but this rule does not apply to every form of an element: for example, O₂(g) is the standard state of oxygen, while atomic O(g) is not. Physical-state labels also matter because H₂O(l) and H₂O(g) have different formation enthalpies.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: calculate a simple reaction enthalpy">

For `CH₄(g) + 2O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(l)`, use illustrative tabulated values ΔH°f[CH₄(g)] = −74.8 kJ·mol⁻¹, ΔH°f[CO₂(g)] = −393.5 kJ·mol⁻¹, and ΔH°f[H₂O(l)] = −285.8 kJ·mol⁻¹; ΔH°f[O₂(g)] = 0. Products sum to −393.5 + 2(−285.8) = −965.1 kJ·mol⁻¹. Reactants sum to −74.8 + 2(0) = −74.8 kJ·mol⁻¹. Thus ΔH°rxn = −965.1 − (−74.8) = −890.3 kJ·mol⁻¹ for the reaction as written.

</WorkedExample>
