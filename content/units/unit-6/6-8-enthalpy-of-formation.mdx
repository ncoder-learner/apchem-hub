The standard enthalpy of formation, ΔH°f, is the enthalpy change when one mole of a compound forms from its constituent elements in their standard states. An element in its standard state has ΔH°f = 0 by convention. This does not mean every allotrope or physical form of an element has zero enthalpy of formation; the standard state matters.

<LessonSection title="1. Use the products-minus-reactants structure" description="Coefficients multiply formation enthalpies" defaultOpen>

```text
ΔH°rxn = Σ nΔH°f(products) − Σ nΔH°f(reactants)
```

Multiply each substance's ΔH°f by its balanced-equation coefficient. Add the product terms, add the reactant terms, then subtract the reactant total from the product total. Keep units consistent, usually kJ/mol of reaction.

<LessonSection title="2. Calculate a reaction enthalpy" description="A reliable sign convention prevents reversed results">

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: formation enthalpies">

For a hypothetical reaction A(g) + 2B(g) → C(g), suppose ΔH°f values are A = −20, B = +10, and C = −100 kJ/mol. Products total = −100 kJ/mol. Reactants total = (−20) + 2(+10) = 0 kJ/mol. Thus ΔH°rxn = −100 − 0 = **−100 kJ/mol**.

</WorkedExample>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="3. Check states and coefficients" description="Thermodynamic tables are state-specific">

Use formation enthalpies for the phases shown in the equation, such as H₂O(l) versus H₂O(g). Do not omit a coefficient or assume a compound's formation enthalpy is zero. If a reaction equation is scaled, the calculated ΔH for that equation scales too.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="4. Use standard formation data with coefficients and states" description="Formation enthalpy makes one mole of a substance from elements in standard states">

A standard enthalpy of formation, Δ H_f^°, is the enthalpy change when exactly one mole of a substance forms from its constituent elements in their standard states. The standard formation enthalpy of an element in its standard state is zero by convention. For a reaction, Δ H_rxn^° = Σ nΔ H_f^°(products) - Σ nΔ H_f^°(reactants). Multiply each value by its coefficient and retain the table's signs.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: ammonia formation">
For N_2(g) + 3H_2(g)→2NH_3(g), suppose Δ H_f^°[NH_3(g)] = - 46.1 kJ·mol⁻¹ and elemental values are zero. Then Δ H^° = 2( - 46.1) - [0 + 3(0)] = - 92.2 kJ for the equation as written. It forms two moles of NH₃, so per mole formed the enthalpy is −46.1 kJ·mol⁻¹.

</WorkedExample>

Check phase labels: H₂O(l) and H₂O(g) have different formation enthalpies because vaporization carries energy. Using the wrong state can shift the result substantially.
