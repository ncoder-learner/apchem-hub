Entropy, S, is a state function related to the number of accessible microscopic arrangements and the dispersal of energy. At the AP Chemistry level, you often predict the sign of ΔS by comparing the initial and final states rather than calculating an absolute entropy.

<LessonSection title="1. Compare accessible arrangements" description="Use particle-level evidence, not the word 'disorder' alone" defaultOpen>

General patterns:
- For the same substance, S(g) > S(l) > S(s), because gas particles have more accessible positions and arrangements.
- More independently moving gas particles often means greater entropy, all else comparable.
- Dissolving or mixing can increase or decrease the entropy of a particular system depending on the interactions and species involved; do not apply a universal rule without evidence.

<QuickCheck question="For the phase change H₂O(l) → H₂O(g), what is the likely sign of ΔS for the water?" options={["Negative, because gas particles are closer together","Positive, because the gas has more accessible arrangements","Zero, because the substance is still H₂O","Impossible to predict qualitatively"]} correctIndex={1} explanation="Gas particles have much greater freedom of position and more accessible arrangements than liquid particles, so vaporization increases entropy for the substance." />

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="2. Predict entropy changes in reactions" description="Count gaseous particles and inspect phase changes">

A reaction that produces more moles of gas than it consumes often has ΔS > 0; a reaction that consumes gas and forms fewer gas particles often has ΔS < 0. This is a useful trend, not a substitute for checking phases and molecular structures. The coefficients matter because they describe the amount of each species.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: gas particle count">

For N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) → 2NH₃(g), the reactants contain 4 mol of gas per reaction as written and the products contain 2 mol of gas. Fewer gas particles are present in the products, so a negative ΔS is a reasonable qualitative prediction, assuming other effects do not dominate.

</WorkedExample>

<QuickCheck question="Which process most clearly has a positive entropy change for the system?" options={["Gas condenses to liquid","Liquid freezes to solid","A solid sublimes to gas","Two gas molecules combine to form one gas molecule"]} correctIndex={2} explanation="Sublimation changes a solid to a gas, greatly increasing particle freedom and accessible arrangements. Condensation, freezing, and gas-particle combination generally reduce entropy." />

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="3. Keep system entropy separate from surroundings" description="Spontaneity depends on the universe, not just the system">

A process may decrease the system's entropy while increasing the surroundings' entropy by a larger amount. The total entropy change is ΔS_universe = ΔS_system + ΔS_surroundings. This distinction sets up Gibbs free energy for processes at constant temperature and pressure.

</LessonSection>
