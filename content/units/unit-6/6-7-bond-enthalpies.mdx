# Bond enthalpies: estimating reaction energy

Bond enthalpy estimates the enthalpy change for a reaction by comparing the energy needed to break reactant bonds with the energy released when product bonds form. The method is an estimate because tabulated bond enthalpies are averages, usually for gaseous molecules.

## 1. The accounting rule

**ΔH_rxn ≈ ΣD(bonds broken) − ΣD(bonds formed)**

- Breaking a bond requires energy, so its contribution is positive.
- Forming a bond releases energy, so its contribution is subtracted.
- Count every bond using the balanced equation's coefficients.

Do not reverse the subtraction. A negative estimate means the energy released by forming product bonds exceeds the energy needed to break reactant bonds.

### Quick check

Breaking a C–H bond requires 413 kJ/mol. What sign does this contribution have?

- A. Negative, because energy is released
- B. Positive, because energy is required
- C. Zero, because bonds are not substances
- D. It depends on the overall reaction enthalpy

**Answer: B.** Breaking a bond requires energy, so it contributes a positive value to the sum for bonds broken.

## 2. Work a complete estimate

A reaction breaks one X–X bond with D = 200 kJ/mol and one Y–Y bond with D = 150 kJ/mol. It forms two X–Y bonds, each with D = 180 kJ/mol.

1. Energy required to break bonds: 200 + 150 = 350 kJ/mol.
2. Energy released when bonds form: 2(180) = 360 kJ/mol.
3. Estimate: ΔH ≈ 350 − 360 = **−10 kJ/mol**.

The estimate is exothermic because forming the product bonds releases slightly more energy than breaking the reactant bonds requires.

### Quick check

A reaction breaks bonds requiring 900 kJ/mol total and forms bonds releasing 1,150 kJ/mol total. What is ΔH?

- A. +2,050 kJ/mol
- B. +250 kJ/mol
- C. −250 kJ/mol
- D. −2,050 kJ/mol

**Answer: C.** ΔH ≈ 900 − 1,150 = −250 kJ/mol.

## 3. Count bond changes from structures

For the hydrogenation reaction C₂H₄(g) + H₂(g) → C₂H₆(g), the reactants contain one C=C bond, four C–H bonds, and one H–H bond. The product contains one C–C bond and six C–H bonds.

Four C–H bonds appear on both sides and cancel from the accounting. The net changes are:

- Bonds broken: one C=C and one H–H.
- Bonds formed: one C–C and two C–H.

Using illustrative average values of 614, 436, 347, and 413 kJ/mol, respectively:

ΔH ≈ (614 + 436) − (347 + 2 × 413) = **−123 kJ/mol**.

This is an estimate, not an exact experimental value. Count bonds from molecular connectivity, not merely from the number of atoms. Double bonds and single bonds have different enthalpies.

## 4. Know the method's limits

Average bond enthalpies are most useful for estimating gas-phase reactions involving molecular bonds. Their values vary with molecular environment, so they do not give an exact enthalpy for every reaction.

Standard enthalpies of formation are usually more specific when reliable values are available for each substance in its stated physical state. For example, forming H₂O(l) has a different enthalpy change from forming H₂O(g), because condensation releases additional energy. A gas-phase bond-enthalpy estimate does not automatically include that phase change.

### Quick check

Why can a bond-enthalpy estimate differ from a calculation using standard enthalpies of formation?

- A. Bond enthalpies are averages and usually refer to gas-phase bonds, while formation values are state-specific.
- B. Bond enthalpies violate energy conservation.
- C. Formation enthalpies cannot be used for reactions.
- D. Bond enthalpies change the balanced equation.

**Answer: A.** The two methods use different data and assumptions; formation enthalpies account for specified substances and states.
