Bond enthalpy estimates ΔH by tracking bonds broken and bonds formed in the gas phase. Breaking a bond requires energy, so it contributes a positive term. Forming a bond releases energy, so it contributes a negative term.

<LessonSection title="1. Use the bond-energy accounting rule" description="Break old bonds, then form new bonds" defaultOpen>

```text
ΔH_rxn ≈ Σ D(bonds broken) − Σ D(bonds formed)
```

Count every bond according to the balanced reaction, including coefficients. First list the bonds in the reactants that must be broken; then list the bonds present in the products that form. The subtraction already accounts for energy released during bond formation.

<LessonSection title="2. Work a complete estimate" description="The result is approximate because tabulated values are averages">

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: bond accounting">

Suppose a reaction breaks one X–X bond with D = 200 kJ/mol and one Y–Y bond with D = 150 kJ/mol, then forms two X–Y bonds each with D = 180 kJ/mol. Bonds broken require 200 + 150 = 350 kJ/mol. Bonds formed release 2(180) = 360 kJ/mol. Therefore ΔH ≈ 350 − 360 = **−10 kJ/mol**, an exothermic estimate.

</WorkedExample>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="3. Know the model's limits" description="Bond enthalpies are averages, not exact molecular measurements">

Bond enthalpies are average values for bonds in gaseous molecules. Their use gives an estimate, while standard enthalpies of formation are often more accurate for a specified substance and state. A common error is subtracting in the opposite order or forgetting that a product coefficient means multiple bonds form.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="4. Count bond changes from structures" description="The formula is only as accurate as your bond inventory">

The bond-enthalpy estimate compares bonds in reactants with bonds in products. Draw or inspect structures, list each bond type, and multiply its tabulated average energy by the number of bonds. Count every bond in the balanced reaction; a coefficient of 2 means counting that molecule's bonds twice.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: hydrogenation of ethene">
For C₂H₄ + H₂→ C₂H₆, reactants contain one C=C, four C–H, and one H–H bond. The product contains one C–C and six C–H bonds. Cancel the four C–H bonds common to both sides, leaving one C=C and one H–H broken and one C–C and two C–H formed. Using illustrative average values 614, 436, 347, and 413 kJ·mol⁻¹ gives ΔH≈(614 + 436) - (347 + 2(413)) = - 123 kJ·mol⁻¹. Treat this as an estimate because bond values depend on environment.

</WorkedExample>

A bond is not “broken” merely because its molecule is on the reactant side. Track which bonds disappear and which appear when connectivity changes.
