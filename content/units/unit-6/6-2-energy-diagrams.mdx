An energy diagram shows potential energy along a reaction pathway. The vertical axis is energy; the horizontal axis is reaction progress, not time. The curve describes a pathway model, not a movie of individual molecules.

<TopicLab unitId={6} topicNumber="6.2" title="Energy Diagrams" />

<LessonSection title="1. Read the landmarks" description="Each vertical difference answers a different question" defaultOpen>

- **Reactants:** the starting energy level.
- **Products:** the ending energy level.
- **Transition state:** the high-energy arrangement near the top of a barrier.
- **Forward activation energy, Eₐ:** energy difference from reactants to the transition state.
- **Enthalpy change, ΔH:** energy difference from reactants to products, ΔH = Hₚᵣₒdᵤcₜₛ − Hᵣₑₐcₜₐₙₜₛ.

The peak is not the product energy. A reaction can have a high activation barrier and still be exothermic.

<QuickCheck question="On an energy diagram, which difference represents ΔH?" options={["Reactants to the transition state","Products to the transition state","Products minus reactants","The horizontal distance from reactants to products"]} correctIndex={2} explanation="ΔH is the final product energy minus the initial reactant energy. The reactant-to-peak difference is the forward activation energy." />

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="2. Compare forward and reverse pathways" description="The same transition state gives different activation energies">

For a single-step pathway, the transition state is the same whichever direction the reaction is considered. If the forward reaction is exothermic, the products lie below the reactants. The reverse reaction must climb from those lower-energy products to the same transition state, so its activation energy is larger by the magnitude of the forward ΔH.

For the same pathway, Eₐ,reverse − Eₐ,forward = −ΔH. Equivalently, Eₐ,forward − Eₐ,reverse = ΔH. This is a relationship between energy differences, not a rule that the two reaction rates are equal.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: compare activation energies">

A forward reaction has Eₐ = 48 kJ/mol and ΔH = −20 kJ/mol. The transition state is 48 kJ/mol above the reactants; the products are 20 kJ/mol below the reactants. Therefore the reverse barrier is 48 + 20 = **68 kJ/mol**.

</WorkedExample>

<LessonSection title="3. Multi-step diagrams" description="Count barriers and intermediates carefully">

A multi-step diagram has a local maximum for each transition state and a local minimum between steps for an intermediate. Each elementary step has its own activation barrier. The overall ΔH is still the difference between the final products and the initial reactants; it is not the sum of the heights of the peaks.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="4. Calculate energy differences from a profile" description="Measure vertical differences from the correct baseline">

On an energy diagram, the vertical axis is potential energy or enthalpy and the horizontal axis is reaction progress, not time. The peak is the transition state. Forward activation energy is peak minus reactants; ΔH is products minus reactants. A diagram does not directly provide a numerical rate.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: read three energy quantities">
Reactants are at 15 kJ·mol⁻¹, the peak is at 68 kJ·mol⁻¹, and products are at −5 kJ·mol⁻¹. The forward activation energy is 53 kJ·mol⁻¹; ΔH = - 5 - 15 = - 20 kJ·mol⁻¹; the reverse activation energy is 73 kJ·mol⁻¹. Products are lower, so the reaction is exothermic despite the barrier.

</WorkedExample>

A common error is measuring ΔH from reactants to the peak. That is activation energy. Another is using the sign of an arrow without checking whether it represents products minus reactants.

</LessonSection>


<LessonSection title="5. Relate forward and reverse activation energies" description="Activation barriers are measured from their own starting levels">

The transition state is the highest-energy point along a reaction pathway. The forward activation energy is the energy difference between the transition state and reactants; the reverse activation energy is measured from products to that same transition state. They are not generally equal because reactants and products may have different energies.

The relationship follows from the definitions: `Ea,forward = E‡ − Ereactants`, `Ea,reverse = E‡ − Eproducts`, and `ΔH = Eproducts − Ereactants`. Therefore `Ea,reverse = Ea,forward − ΔH`. For an exothermic reaction, ΔH is negative, so the reverse barrier is larger than the forward barrier.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: infer the reverse barrier">

A reaction has `Ea,forward = 55 kJ·mol⁻¹` and `ΔH = −30 kJ·mol⁻¹`. Then `Ea,reverse = 55 − (−30) = 85 kJ·mol⁻¹`. The reverse barrier is larger because the products lie 30 kJ·mol⁻¹ below the reactants, so the transition state is farther above the products.

</WorkedExample>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="6. Infer what changes when a catalyst is added to an energy profile" description="A catalyst changes the pathway's barrier, not the endpoint energies">

On a potential-energy diagram, the reactant and product energy levels define the reaction's overall enthalpy change. A catalyst creates an alternative pathway with a lower activation barrier. Because the initial and final states are unchanged, ΔH is unchanged. For the same catalyzed pathway, both forward and reverse activation energies are lower, and the difference between them still equals the same ΔH.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: compare two pathways">

For a reaction, reactants are at 20 kJ·mol⁻¹ and products at −10 kJ·mol⁻¹. The uncatalyzed transition state is at 90 kJ·mol⁻¹; a catalyzed route has a transition state at 55 kJ·mol⁻¹. Both routes have ΔH = −10 − 20 = −30 kJ·mol⁻¹. The forward activation energies are 70 and 35 kJ·mol⁻¹, respectively. The reverse barriers are 100 and 65 kJ·mol⁻¹. The catalyst lowers both barriers by 35 kJ·mol⁻¹ without changing the energy difference between endpoints.

</WorkedExample>
