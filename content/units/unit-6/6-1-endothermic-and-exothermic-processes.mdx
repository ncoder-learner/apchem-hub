A thermochemical explanation starts by naming the **system** (the reaction or process being studied) and the **surroundings** (everything exchanging energy with it). Heat, q, is energy transferred because of a temperature difference. Enthalpy change, ΔH, describes the system's enthalpy change; for a process at constant pressure, the heat transferred to the system is qₚ = ΔH.

<LessonSection title="1. Read the sign from the system's point of view" description="Heat flow and temperature change are related, but they refer to different things" defaultOpen>

- **Exothermic:** the system transfers heat to the surroundings. The system loses energy as heat, so ΔH < 0.
- **Endothermic:** the system absorbs heat from the surroundings. The system gains energy as heat, so ΔH > 0.

A solution getting warmer usually means the reaction released heat to the solution. The solution is the surroundings; do not assign the solution's positive heat gain to the reaction.

<LessonSection title="2. Connect heat flow to energy diagrams" description="The sign describes the difference between products and reactants">

For a reaction energy diagram, compare the products' energy with the reactants' energy. If products are lower, energy has been released and ΔH is negative. If products are higher, energy has been absorbed and ΔH is positive. The activation-energy barrier is a different quantity: it describes the energy needed to reach the transition state, not the net enthalpy change.

| Evidence | Conclusion |
|---|---|
| Products lower in energy than reactants | ΔH < 0, exothermic |
| Products higher in energy than reactants | ΔH > 0, endothermic |
| Larger activation barrier | Slower reaction is often expected, but this alone does not determine ΔH |

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="3. Keep the system boundary consistent" description="The same process has opposite heat signs for system and surroundings">

For a well-insulated calorimetry setup, energy conservation gives qₛᵧₛₜₑₘ + qₛᵤᵣᵣₒᵤₙdᵢₙgₛ ≈ 0. Therefore, qₛᵧₛₜₑₘ ≈ −qₛᵤᵣᵣₒᵤₙdᵢₙgₛ. The approximation assumes energy exchange with the outside is negligible.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: sign from the solution">

A solution gains 2.4 kJ as a reaction occurs at constant pressure. The surroundings gain heat, so the reaction system loses approximately 2.4 kJ: qᵣₑₐcₜᵢₒₙ ≈ −2.4 kJ. If this is the reaction's constant-pressure heat, ΔH ≈ −2.4 kJ for the amount reacted.

</WorkedExample>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="4. Explain what the sign does and does not tell you" description="Enthalpy is a state-function difference, not a rate or spontaneity test">

For a process at constant pressure, Δ H = H_products - H_reactants = q_p for the system. The sign depends on the system boundary: if a reaction warms its surroundings, the reaction's q and ΔH are negative. The magnitude refers to the reaction as written and the amount represented by its coefficients. Doubling the equation doubles ΔH; reversing it reverses the sign.

Do not infer speed or spontaneity from ΔH alone. An exothermic reaction can be slow because its activation barrier is large. Spontaneity depends on Gibbs free energy, which also includes entropy and temperature.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: scale and reverse">
If A→ B has Δ H = - 42 kJ, then 2A→2B has Δ H = - 84 kJ. Reversing the doubled equation, 2B→2A, has Δ H = + 84 kJ. The energy change is tied to the direction and amount of the process.

</WorkedExample>
