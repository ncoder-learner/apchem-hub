When objects at different temperatures exchange energy, heat flows spontaneously from the warmer object to the cooler one until they reach thermal equilibrium. At equilibrium, they have the same temperature; they do not necessarily have the same mass, heat capacity, or amount of energy.

<LessonSection title="1. Use the direction of heat flow" description="Energy leaves one part and enters another" defaultOpen>

For a simple isolated setup, energy conservation gives:

```text
q_hot + q_cold = 0
```

Thus q_hot = −q_cold. The hot object has a negative q as it cools; the cold object has a positive q as it warms. This relation assumes negligible heat transfer to the cup or outside environment.

<LessonSection title="2. Predict the final temperature qualitatively" description="The amount of temperature change depends on heat capacity">

A material with a larger total heat capacity requires more energy to change its temperature by one degree. In a hot-metal/cool-water problem, the final temperature lies between the initial temperatures when no phase change occurs and no energy enters or leaves the setup. It is not generally the arithmetic mean; mass and specific heat matter.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: qualitative check">

A small hot metal piece is dropped into a much larger mass of cool water. If the setup is insulated and neither material changes phase, the final temperature must be between the two starting temperatures. Because the water has a large total heat capacity, its temperature may change only a little. A calculated final temperature outside the initial range signals a sign, unit, or setup error.

</WorkedExample>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="3. Know the assumptions" description="Real calorimeters are not perfectly isolated">

A typical introductory calculation assumes the system is insulated, no material escapes, no phase change occurs unless stated, and heat capacities remain constant over the measured interval. In a real experiment, the cup and thermometer can absorb energy too. If that contribution is not negligible, it must be included in the energy balance.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="4. Distinguish heat transfer from temperature" description="Energy flows until the objects reach a shared thermal equilibrium">

Heat is energy transferred because of a temperature difference; temperature is not an amount of energy. In an isolated setup, the warmer object loses energy and the cooler object gains it until they reach a common final temperature. The amount exchanged depends on mass, specific heat capacity, and temperature change.

For two objects, q_1 + q_2 = 0, so m_1c_1(T_f - T_i,1) + m_2c_2(T_f - T_i,2) = 0. The final temperature is not generally the arithmetic mean: the object with larger total heat capacity mc changes temperature less for the same energy transfer.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: metal placed in water">
A 50.0 g metal sample with c = 0.40 J g⁻ 1 °C⁻ 1 cools from 90.0°C to 30.0°C in 100.0 g of water initially at 20.0°C. Metal heat is q_m = (50.0)(0.40)(30.0 - 90.0) = - 1200 J. Assuming no heat escapes, water gains +1200 J, so 1200 = (100.0)(4.184)(T_f - 20.0), giving T_f≈22.9°C.

</WorkedExample>

The ideal balance can fail when the cup, thermometer, or outside environment absorbs heat. State that assumption when interpreting experimental data.

<LessonSection title="5. Calculate a final temperature from energy conservation" description="The final temperature is shared, but each material transfers a different amount of heat">

When two objects exchange heat in an insulated container, the hot object loses energy and the cold object gains it until both reach the same final temperature. For each object, use `q = mc(Tf − Ti)`; the signs emerge from the temperature difference. The energy balance is `q₁ + q₂ = 0` if no calorimeter or outside environment absorbs heat.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: hot copper placed in water">

A 50.0 g copper sample at 90.0°C is placed in 100.0 g water at 20.0°C. Use `cCu = 0.385 J·g⁻¹·°C⁻¹` and `cwater = 4.184 J·g⁻¹·°C⁻¹`, and assume no heat loss.

`50.0(0.385)(Tf − 90.0) + 100.0(4.184)(Tf − 20.0) = 0`

Solving gives `Tf ≈ 23.1°C`. The final temperature is much closer to the water's starting temperature because the water's total heat capacity, mc, is far larger than the copper sample's. Equal heat magnitudes do not require equal temperature changes.

</WorkedExample>

<LessonSection title="6. Solve a two-object mixing problem using energy conservation" description="The heat lost by the warmer object equals the heat gained by the cooler object">

For two objects in an insulated system, energy conservation gives qhot + qcold = 0. With no phase change and constant specific heats, this becomes mhot chot(Tf − Thot) + mcold ccold(Tf − Tcold) = 0. The signs come from the temperature changes: the warmer object has Tf below its initial temperature, while the cooler object has Tf above its initial temperature. Do not set the magnitudes equal until you have accounted for the masses and specific heats.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: hot metal in water">

A 50.0 g metal sample with specific heat 0.400 J·g⁻¹·°C⁻¹ at 100.0°C is placed in 100.0 g water at 20.0°C. Assume the metal and water exchange heat only with each other and use cwater = 4.184 J·g⁻¹·°C⁻¹. Then 20.0( Tf − 100.0) + 418.4( Tf − 20.0) = 0. Combining terms gives 438.4Tf = 10,368, so Tf ≈ 23.6°C. The water's much larger total heat capacity makes the final temperature much closer to its starting temperature.

</WorkedExample>

A final temperature must lie between the initial temperatures if no phase change or external heating occurs. A result outside that interval usually indicates a sign, unit, or arithmetic error. If the calorimeter absorbs heat, add its heat term to the energy balance rather than silently assuming it is negligible.
