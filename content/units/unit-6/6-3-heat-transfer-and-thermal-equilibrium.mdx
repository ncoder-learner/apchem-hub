When objects at different temperatures exchange energy, heat flows spontaneously from the warmer object to the cooler one until they reach thermal equilibrium. At equilibrium, they have the same temperature; they do not necessarily have the same mass, heat capacity, or amount of energy.

<LessonSection title="1. Use the direction of heat flow" description="Energy leaves one part and enters another" defaultOpen>

For a simple isolated setup, energy conservation gives:

```text
q_hot + q_cold = 0
```

Thus q_hot = −q_cold. The hot object has a negative q as it cools; the cold object has a positive q as it warms. This relation assumes negligible heat transfer to the cup or outside environment.

<LessonSection title="2. Predict the final temperature qualitatively" description="The amount of temperature change depends on heat capacity">

A material with a larger total heat capacity requires more energy to change its temperature by one degree. In a hot-metal/cool-water problem, the final temperature lies between the initial temperatures when no phase change occurs and no energy enters or leaves the setup. It is not generally the arithmetic mean; mass and specific heat matter.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: qualitative check">

A small hot metal piece is dropped into a much larger mass of cool water. If the setup is insulated and neither material changes phase, the final temperature must be between the two starting temperatures. Because the water has a large total heat capacity, its temperature may change only a little. A calculated final temperature outside the initial range signals a sign, unit, or setup error.

</WorkedExample>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="3. Know the assumptions" description="Real calorimeters are not perfectly isolated">

A typical introductory calculation assumes the system is insulated, no material escapes, no phase change occurs unless stated, and heat capacities remain constant over the measured interval. In a real experiment, the cup and thermometer can absorb energy too. If that contribution is not negligible, it must be included in the energy balance.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="4. Distinguish heat transfer from temperature" description="Energy flows until the objects reach a shared thermal equilibrium">

Heat is energy transferred because of a temperature difference; temperature is not an amount of energy. In an isolated setup, the warmer object loses energy and the cooler object gains it until they reach a common final temperature. The amount exchanged depends on mass, specific heat capacity, and temperature change.

For two objects, \(q_1+q_2=0\), so \(m_1c_1(T_f-T_{i,1})+m_2c_2(T_f-T_{i,2})=0\). The final temperature is not generally the arithmetic mean: the object with larger total heat capacity \(mc\) changes temperature less for the same energy transfer.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: metal placed in water">
A 50.0 g metal sample with \(c=0.40\ \mathrm{J\,g^{-1}\,°C^{-1}}\) cools from 90.0°C to 30.0°C in 100.0 g of water initially at 20.0°C. Metal heat is \(q_m=(50.0)(0.40)(30.0-90.0)=-1200\) J. Assuming no heat escapes, water gains +1200 J, so \(1200=(100.0)(4.184)(T_f-20.0)\), giving \(T_f\approx22.9°C\).

</WorkedExample>

The ideal balance can fail when the cup, thermometer, or outside environment absorbs heat. State that assumption when interpreting experimental data.
