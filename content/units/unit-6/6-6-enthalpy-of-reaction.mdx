The enthalpy change ΔH is a state-function difference for a process as written. Its sign describes whether the system's enthalpy increases or decreases. A thermochemical equation must be read with its coefficients: ΔH refers to the reaction extent represented by those coefficients.

<LessonSection title="1. Treat ΔH as part of the equation" description="Changing the equation changes the enthalpy value" defaultOpen>

If a reaction equation is reversed, ΔH changes sign. If all coefficients are multiplied by a factor, ΔH is multiplied by the same factor. If coefficients are divided by a factor, ΔH is divided by that factor. The physical molar enthalpy remains consistent when the amount of reaction is defined consistently.

<QuickCheck question="A thermochemical equation has ΔH = −120 kJ. What is ΔH if the equation is reversed and all coefficients are unchanged in magnitude?" options={["−240 kJ","+120 kJ","−120 kJ","0 kJ"]} correctIndex={1} explanation="Reversing a process reverses the direction of enthalpy change, so the sign changes from −120 kJ to +120 kJ." />

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="2. Connect calorimetry to reaction enthalpy" description="Measure heat, then normalize by reaction amount">

For a constant-pressure reaction, q_p = ΔH for the amount of reaction that occurred. In a coffee-cup calorimeter, q_rxn ≈ −q_solution if heat exchange with the cup and outside is negligible. To compare experiments or use a tabulated molar enthalpy, divide by the number of moles corresponding to the balanced reaction's extent.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: scaling a reaction">

A reaction releases 18 kJ when 0.30 mol of reaction occurs. The molar enthalpy is (−18 kJ)/(0.30 mol) = **−60 kJ per mole of reaction**. If the reaction extent doubles, the heat released doubles, but the molar enthalpy remains −60 kJ/mol.

</WorkedExample>

<QuickCheck question="If the balanced equation is multiplied by 2, what happens to its ΔH value?" options={["It is divided by 2","Its sign reverses","Its magnitude doubles","It stays unchanged in all cases"]} correctIndex={2} explanation="Enthalpy is extensive with respect to the amount of reaction. Doubling every coefficient doubles the enthalpy change for the equation as written." />

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="3. Avoid sign shortcuts" description="Determine the system and process before assigning a sign">

Do not assign signs from words such as “warm” or “cold” without identifying what is warming. A warming solution often signals an exothermic reaction because the solution is the surroundings. A thermochemical equation's ΔH, however, is always written from the reaction system's perspective.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="4. Choose an enthalpy calculation that matches the data" description="Bond enthalpies estimate energy; formation enthalpies use a state-function sum">

Bond-enthalpy estimates use ΔH_rxn≈Σ D(bonds broken) - Σ D(bonds formed). Breaking bonds requires energy; forming bonds releases energy. Count every bond using the balanced equation and molecular structures. Because tabulated bond enthalpies are averages, this method estimates ΔH.

Standard enthalpies of formation use ΔH°_rxn = Σ nΔHf°(products) - Σ nΔHf°(reactants). Multiply each value by its stoichiometric coefficient. An element in its standard state has ΔHf° = 0.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: bond bookkeeping">
For H₂ + Cl_2→2HCl, one H–H bond and one Cl–Cl bond are broken, and two H–Cl bonds form. Using representative values 436, 243, and 431 kJ·mol⁻¹, ΔH≈436 + 243 - 2(431) = - 183 kJ per reaction as written. More energy is released forming product bonds than is required to break reactant bonds.

</WorkedExample>

Do not mix the two methods in one sum. One uses average bond energies and broken-minus-formed; the other uses formation enthalpies and products-minus-reactants.

<QuickCheck question="For a bond-enthalpy estimate, which operation gives the correct sign convention?" options={["Bonds formed minus bonds broken","Bonds broken minus bonds formed","Sum all bond energies without signs","Products' coefficients minus reactants' coefficients"]} correctIndex={1} explanation="Energy is required to break bonds and released when bonds form, so ΔH is estimated as broken minus formed." />

</LessonSection>


<LessonSection title="5. Scale enthalpy to the amount that actually reacts" description="A thermochemical equation defines ΔH for the reaction amount written">

A thermochemical equation associates an enthalpy change with its stoichiometric coefficients. If the equation is multiplied by a factor, its ΔH is multiplied by the same factor. For a reaction written with `ΔH = −572 kJ` per reaction event, that value corresponds to the full stoichiometric equation, not to one mole of every substance in the equation.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: use the reaction extent">

For `2H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2H₂O(l)`, suppose `ΔH = −572 kJ` as written. If 0.500 mol H₂ reacts completely with excess O₂, the reaction extent is `0.500/2 = 0.250` of the equation as written. Therefore `q = 0.250(−572) = −143 kJ`. The same amount forms 0.500 mol H₂O. Dividing −143 kJ by 0.500 mol H₂O gives −286 kJ per mole of water formed, consistent with the reaction equation scaled to one mole of H₂O.

</WorkedExample>

Do not divide by the number of moles of all reactants combined. Use the balanced equation to determine the amount of reaction that occurred, then scale ΔH accordingly. If a problem instead gives calorimetry data, first calculate the heat for the sample and then divide by the moles of reaction as defined by the balanced equation.

</LessonSection>
