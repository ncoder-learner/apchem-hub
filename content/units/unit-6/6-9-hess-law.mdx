Hess's law says the enthalpy change for an overall reaction depends only on the initial and final states. If a target reaction can be written as a sum of known reactions, its ΔH is the same sum of the corresponding enthalpy changes.

<TopicLab unitId={6} topicNumber="6.9" title="Hess’s Law" />

<LessonSection title="1. Transform equations, not just numbers" description="Every equation operation has a matching ΔH operation" defaultOpen>

- Reverse an equation: reverse the sign of ΔH.
- Multiply every coefficient by a factor: multiply ΔH by that factor.
- Add equations: add their ΔH values.
- Cancel species that appear on opposite sides; the final net equation must match the target.

<LessonSection title="2. Build the target reaction systematically" description="Use species cancellation as your check">

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: combine two equations">

Given:
A → B, ΔH₁ = +25 kJ
B → C, ΔH₂ = −70 kJ

Adding the equations cancels B and gives A → C. Add the enthalpy changes: ΔH = +25 + (−70) = **−45 kJ**. The intermediate B disappears from the net equation, just as it should.

</WorkedExample>

<LessonSection title="3. Use the net equation as an audit" description="Do not stop when the ΔH arithmetic looks plausible">

Write the transformed equations, label each adjusted ΔH, add the equations line by line, and verify every unwanted species cancels. Only then add the enthalpy values. Enthalpy is a state function, but a correct number attached to the wrong net reaction is not a valid answer.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="4. Solve a Hess's-law cycle with multiple source equations" description="Use cancellation to isolate the target formation reaction">

Hess's law can combine several reactions, not just one reversal and one addition. The reliable method is to write the target equation, select source equations that contain its species, transform each equation until unwanted substances cancel, and apply every transformation to ΔH. A source equation may need to be reversed even if only one of its species is visibly part of the target, because intermediates must cancel across the full sum.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: derive the formation of methane">

Use these data:

- `C(s) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g)`, `ΔH = −393.5 kJ`
- `2H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2H₂O(l)`, `ΔH = −571.6 kJ`
- `CH₄(g) + 2O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(l)`, `ΔH = −890.3 kJ`

The target is `C(s) + 2H₂(g) → CH₄(g)`. Reverse the methane-combustion equation so CH₄ appears as a product: its `ΔH` becomes +890.3 kJ. Add the first two equations to that reversed equation. CO₂, 2H₂O, and 2O₂ cancel, leaving exactly the target reaction. Therefore `ΔH = −393.5 − 571.6 + 890.3 = −74.8 kJ`.

</WorkedExample>

Do not cancel species that appear on the same side of every equation, and do not cancel substances in different physical states as though they were identical. The final audit is always the net balanced equation plus the sum of the adjusted enthalpies.

<LessonSection title="5. Use Hess's law as a linear combination of reactions" description="Every source equation must cancel to the target equation">

Hess's law works because enthalpy is a state function: the net enthalpy change depends on the initial and final states, not the path. To solve a problem, choose multipliers for the supplied equations so unwanted species cancel and the target coefficients remain. Apply each multiplier to both the equation and its ΔH, reverse the sign when reversing a reaction, and add the resulting enthalpy changes only after the chemical equations have been combined.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: combine two equations">

Given `C(s) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g)`, ΔH₁ = −393.5 kJ, and `CO(g) + ½O₂(g) → CO₂(g)`, ΔH₂ = −283.0 kJ, find ΔH for `C(s) + ½O₂(g) → CO(g)`. Reverse the second equation to obtain `CO₂(g) → CO(g) + ½O₂(g)`, ΔH = +283.0 kJ. Add it to the first equation; CO₂ cancels, leaving the target equation. The total is −393.5 + 283.0 = −110.5 kJ.

</WorkedExample>

Before accepting the number, verify that the summed chemical equation exactly matches the target, including physical states and coefficients. A numerically plausible ΔH with the wrong net equation is not a valid Hess's-law solution.
