Acid–base chemistry is fundamentally about proton transfer in aqueous solution. The safest way to analyze a reaction is to identify which species donates H⁺, which accepts H⁺, and how each species changes after transfer.

<LessonSection title="1. Two definitions of acids and bases" description="Use the definition that matches the prompt" defaultOpen>

An **Arrhenius acid** increases the concentration of H₃O⁺ in water; an **Arrhenius base** increases OH⁻ in water. The **Brønsted–Lowry** definition is broader: an acid donates a proton, and a base accepts a proton.

For example, HCl transfers a proton to water:

HCl + H₂O → H₃O⁺ + Cl⁻

HCl is the acid because it donates H⁺. H₂O is the base because it accepts H⁺. The product Cl⁻ is the conjugate base of HCl, and H₃O⁺ is the conjugate acid of H₂O.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="2. Find conjugate acid–base pairs" description="A conjugate pair differs by exactly one H⁺">

When an acid donates H⁺, the remaining species is its conjugate base. When a base accepts H⁺, the product is its conjugate acid. Conjugate partners differ by one proton, and their charges differ by one unit.

Examples:
- H₂CO₃ / HCO₃⁻: acid / conjugate base
- NH₃ / NH₄⁺: base / conjugate acid
- H₂O / OH⁻: acid / conjugate base when water donates H⁺
- H₂O / H₃O⁺: base / conjugate acid when water accepts H⁺

Water can act as either an acid or a base. This behavior is called amphiprotic.

<WorkedExample title="Identify every role in a proton-transfer reaction">
For HCO₃⁻ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ + OH⁻, HCO₃⁻ accepts a proton and is the base; H₂CO₃ is its conjugate acid. Water donates a proton and is the acid; OH⁻ is its conjugate base. Check the pairs: HCO₃⁻/H₂CO₃ and H₂O/OH⁻ each differ by one H⁺.
</WorkedExample>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="3. Read equations before memorizing labels" description="Follow the proton, not the name">

A species is not always an acid or base in every reaction. Its role depends on what it does in that particular equation. Compare the reactant and product formulas, track the H⁺, and then assign the roles.

Common error: calling OH⁻ the base in every equation where it appears. In a forward neutralization it often accepts H⁺, but always verify the direction and species changes shown.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="4. Track proton transfer and charge together" description="Conjugate partners differ by exactly one H⁺">

When a Brønsted–Lowry acid donates H⁺, its conjugate base has one fewer hydrogen and a charge one unit more negative. When a base accepts H⁺, its conjugate acid has one more hydrogen and a charge one unit more positive. These checks help catch incorrect pairings in polyprotic systems.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: identify both pairs">
For H_2PO_4^ - + H_2O⇌ H_3O^ + + HPO_4^2 -, dihydrogen phosphate donates H⁺, so it is the acid and HPO₄²⁻ its conjugate base. Water accepts H⁺, so it is the base and H₃O⁺ its conjugate acid. Check total charge: −1 on each side.

</WorkedExample>

Some species are amphiprotic and can either donate or accept a proton depending on the reaction partner. Do not assign a permanent acid/base role from the formula alone; use the proton transfer shown in the equation.
