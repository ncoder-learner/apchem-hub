Buffer capacity is the amount of added strong acid or base a buffer can absorb before its pH changes substantially. Capacity is about the amount of material available to react, not just the pH value.

<LessonSection title="1. Separate capacity from pH" description="Same pH does not mean same capacity" defaultOpen>

Two buffers can have the same ratio [A⁻]/[HA] and therefore approximately the same pH, while having very different total concentrations. A buffer containing 1.0 M HA and 1.0 M A⁻ has much greater capacity than one containing 0.010 M of each because it has far more material available to consume added reagent.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="2. Compare resistance to acid and base" description="The component that reacts sets the direction of capacity">

For HA/A⁻, A⁻ consumes added acid and HA consumes added base. A buffer with much more A⁻ than HA has relatively greater capacity against added acid, but less capacity against added base. A buffer with much more HA than A⁻ has the opposite balance.

<WorkedExample title="Compare two buffers">
Buffer A contains 0.20 mol HA and 0.20 mol A⁻. Buffer B contains 0.020 mol HA and 0.020 mol A⁻. Their equal ratios mean similar initial pH values if the same acid is used, but Buffer A has ten times as many moles of each component and can neutralize more added acid or base before one component is depleted.
</WorkedExample>

<LessonSection title="3. Identify buffer exhaustion" description="A buffer is finite">

If the amount of added OH⁻ approaches or exceeds the available HA, the weak acid component is depleted and the buffer can no longer resist the addition effectively. Calculate stoichiometric amounts first; if a component becomes zero or negative in the bookkeeping, the excess strong reagent must be used to determine pH.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="4. Quantify capacity by following the limiting buffer component" description="The ratio predicts pH; the total amounts determine how much disturbance can be absorbed">

A buffer's capacity is not determined by pH alone. The pH depends largely on the ratio of conjugate base to weak acid, while capacity depends on the available moles of both forms. To evaluate a proposed addition, write the strong-acid or strong-base neutralization equation and subtract moles from the consumed buffer component while adding them to the conjugate partner.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: identify the capacity limit">
A buffer contains 0.020 mol HA and 0.015 mol A⁻. Adding 0.018 mol OH⁻ consumes 0.018 mol HA, but only 0.020 mol HA is available. The remaining HA is 0.002 mol and A⁻ becomes 0.033 mol. The buffer still contains both forms, but the ratio has changed from 0.75 to 16.5, so the pH rises sharply. If more than 0.020 mol OH⁻ were added, excess strong base would remain and control the pH.

</WorkedExample>

For equal pH, a more concentrated buffer generally has greater capacity. Dilution often leaves the ratio and pH approximately unchanged but lowers capacity because the same added amount represents a larger fraction of each component.

<LessonSection title="5. Estimate how much strong base a buffer can absorb" description="The limiting buffer component sets the approximate neutralization capacity">

Buffer capacity depends on the amounts of both members of a conjugate pair. When strong base is added, HA is consumed; when strong acid is added, A⁻ is consumed. The buffer resists a large pH change only while both forms remain in appreciable amounts. The pH ratio alone does not tell you how much acid or base can be neutralized, so use moles and stoichiometry to estimate the capacity of a particular sample.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: find the limiting buffer component">

A sample contains 0.012 mol HA and 0.008 mol A⁻. Ignoring other reactions, it can neutralize at most about 0.012 mol added OH⁻ before HA is consumed, and at most about 0.008 mol added H₃O⁺ before A⁻ is consumed. In practice, buffering becomes poor before a component reaches exactly zero, because the acid/base ratio becomes extreme and pH changes sharply. A larger volume of the same buffer contains proportionally more moles and therefore has greater total capacity.

</WorkedExample>

Dilution usually leaves the HA:A⁻ ratio and approximate pH nearly unchanged, but lowers the concentration and total moles in a fixed volume. This is why two solutions can start at the same pH yet respond very differently to the same addition of strong acid or base.
