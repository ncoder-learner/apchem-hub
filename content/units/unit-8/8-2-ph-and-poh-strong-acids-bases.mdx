pH expresses hydronium concentration on a logarithmic scale. For strong acids and bases, the central step is usually to determine the concentration of the ions produced by essentially complete dissociation.

<LessonSection title="1. Use the logarithmic definitions" description="Keep the concentration and logarithm signs straight" defaultOpen>

pH = −log[H₃O⁺]

pOH = −log[OH⁻]

At 25 °C, pH + pOH = 14.00 and Kᵥ = [H₃O⁺][OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴. These relationships depend on temperature; use the stated temperature if a problem provides a different value.

To reverse the logarithm, [H₃O⁺] = 10⁻ᵖᴴ and [OH⁻] = 10⁻ᵖᴼᴴ.

<WorkedExample title="Find the pH of 0.0032 M HCl">
HCl is a strong monoprotic acid, so [H₃O⁺] ≈ 0.0032 M. Then pH = −log(0.0032) = 2.49485…, so pH = 2.49. The two significant figures in the concentration correspond to two digits after the decimal in pH.
</WorkedExample>

<LessonSection title="2. Account for the formula and stoichiometry" description="Do not assume acid concentration always equals hydronium concentration">

For a strong monoprotic acid such as HNO₃, one mole produces approximately one mole of H₃O⁺. For a strong base such as Ba(OH)₂, one mole produces two moles of OH⁻, so an ideal 0.010 M Ba(OH)₂ solution gives [OH⁻] ≈ 0.020 M before calculating pOH.

<WorkedExample title="Calculate pH for a strong base">
For 0.020 M Ca(OH)₂, assume complete dissociation: [OH⁻] = 2(0.020 M) = 0.040 M. pOH = −log(0.040) = 1.40. At 25 °C, pH = 14.00 − 1.40 = 12.60.
</WorkedExample>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="3. What the logarithmic scale means" description="A one-unit pH change is a tenfold concentration change">

A solution at pH 3 has ten times the hydronium concentration of a solution at pH 4. A two-unit difference corresponds to a factor of 100. Do not describe pH as a linear measure of acidity.

Common errors include dropping the negative sign, using pH = −log(acid molarity) without checking ion stoichiometry, and reporting too many decimal places.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="4. Build pH calculations from concentration" description="Logarithms compress a concentration scale; they do not replace chemistry">

At 25°C, pH = - log[H₃O⁺ ], pOH = - log[OH⁻ ], and pH + pOH = 14.00 for dilute aqueous solutions under the standard AP assumptions. Strong acids and bases are treated as essentially fully dissociated. Account for stoichiometry before calculating concentration: one mole of HCl gives one mole of H₃O⁺, while one mole of Ba(OH)₂ gives two moles of OH⁻.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: a strong base with two hydroxides">
A 0.0150 M Ba(OH)₂ solution gives [OH⁻ ] = 2(0.0150) = 0.0300 M. Then pOH = - log(0.0300) = 1.523, and pH = 14.000 - 1.523 = 12.477. The factor of two comes from the balanced dissociation, not from a pH shortcut.

</WorkedExample>

Dilution changes concentration but not the moles of solute added, so use M₁V₁ = M₂V₂ only when the same solute is simply diluted and no reaction occurs. For a mixture of acid and base, first perform neutralization stoichiometry, then calculate the concentration of the excess species.

</LessonSection>


<LessonSection title="5. Neutralize mixtures before calculating pH" description="Strong acids and bases react essentially completely before the remaining solution is analyzed">

When a strong acid and strong base are mixed, do not average their pH values or calculate pH from the starting acid concentration alone. First calculate moles of H₃O⁺ and OH⁻, use the neutralization stoichiometry, identify any excess reagent, divide its remaining moles by the total volume, and only then calculate pH or pOH.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: strong acid mixed with strong base">

Mix 25.0 mL of 0.100 M HCl with 15.0 mL of 0.100 M NaOH. The initial amounts are `n(H₃O⁺) = 0.0250(0.100) = 0.00250 mol` and `n(OH⁻) = 0.0150(0.100) = 0.00150 mol`. Neutralization leaves 0.00100 mol excess H₃O⁺. The total volume is 0.0400 L, so `[H₃O⁺] = 0.00100/0.0400 = 0.0250 M` and `pH = −log(0.0250) = 1.60`.

</WorkedExample>

If the strong acid and base amounts are exactly equal, the excess strong acid/base calculation ends with neither in excess; at 25°C, a strong-acid/strong-base solution is approximately neutral at equivalence. If one reagent is in excess, its remaining concentration controls pH, assuming dilute-solution ideal behavior.
