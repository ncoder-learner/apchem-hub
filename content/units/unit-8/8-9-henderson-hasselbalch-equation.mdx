For a weak acid HA and its conjugate base A⁻, the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation is:

pH = pKa + log([A⁻]/[HA])

It is derived from the weak-acid equilibrium expression and is most useful when both conjugate components are present in appreciable amounts.

<LessonSection title="1. Choose the ratio correctly" description="Conjugate base over weak acid" defaultOpen>

The numerator is the conjugate base A⁻ and the denominator is the weak acid HA. If both are in the same final solution, you can use concentrations or moles in the ratio because the common volume cancels.

<WorkedExample title="Calculate a buffer pH">
A buffer contains 0.30 M HA and 0.10 M A⁻, with pKa = 4.80. pH = 4.80 + log(0.10/0.30) = 4.80 + log(0.333) = 4.32. Since base/acid is less than 1, pH is below pKa.
</WorkedExample>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="2. For titration questions, neutralize first" description="Do not use initial amounts if strong titrant has reacted">

When strong acid or base is added to a buffer, first use the balanced neutralization equation to determine the new moles of HA and A⁻. Then apply Henderson–Hasselbalch to the post-reaction amounts. This avoids treating a strong reagent as though it simply joined the buffer without reacting.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="3. Know the limits" description="The equation is not a universal pH formula">

Henderson–Hasselbalch is not the first choice for a solution containing only a strong acid, only a strong base, or a weak acid before any appreciable conjugate base exists. For those situations, calculate pH from the controlling equilibrium or excess strong reagent.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="4. Use Henderson–Hasselbalch only after identifying the buffer pair" description="The equation summarizes an equilibrium ratio, not a neutralization reaction">

The Henderson–Hasselbalch equation, pH = pKa + log([A⁻ ] / [HA]), follows from the acid equilibrium expression when both members of a conjugate pair are present. In the same final solution volume, the concentration ratio equals the mole ratio, so moles can be used directly after strong-acid/base stoichiometry has been completed.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: add strong base then calculate pH">
A mixture contains 0.040 mol HA and 0.030 mol A⁻. Add 0.010 mol OH⁻. Neutralization consumes 0.010 mol HA and forms 0.010 mol A⁻, leaving 0.030 mol HA and 0.040 mol A⁻. If pKa = 4.80, pH = 4.80 + log(0.040 / 0.030) = 4.92. Calculating from the original ratio would ignore the chemical reaction and give the wrong pH.

</WorkedExample>

If the added strong base exceeds the available HA, the buffer calculation no longer applies directly: first determine the excess OH⁻ after stoichiometry, then calculate pOH and pH. A formula should never replace identifying the species actually present.

<LessonSection title="5. Infer a buffer ratio from pH data" description="Use logarithms carefully and distinguish a ratio from an amount">

The equation can be rearranged to find the composition ratio: `[A⁻]/[HA] = 10^(pH − pKa)`. The exponent is the difference between pH and pKa. Do not interpret that difference itself as the ratio: a difference of 1.00 means a ratio of 10, not 1; a difference of 0.30 means a ratio near 2.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: infer the ratio and then the amounts">

A buffer has pH = 5.20 and pKa = 4.80, so `[A⁻]/[HA] = 10^0.40 ≈ 2.51`. If the final volume is 0.500 L, the same ratio can be written as a concentration ratio. If total buffer concentration `[HA] + [A⁻] = 0.100 M`, then `[HA] = 0.100/(1 + 2.51) = 0.0285 M` and `[A⁻] = 0.0715 M`. The ratio and the total concentration are both needed to determine the two concentrations.

</WorkedExample>

The equation is useful only when appreciable amounts of both conjugate partners are present. If a strong acid or base has been added, update the amounts by stoichiometry first. If one partner has been essentially consumed, calculate pH from the excess strong reagent or the relevant weak-acid/base equilibrium instead.
