Acid–base reactions are proton transfers. A buffer is a mixture containing meaningful amounts of a weak acid and its conjugate base, or a weak base and its conjugate acid. The pair reacts with small additions of strong acid or base, limiting the pH change.

<LessonSection title="1. Predict proton transfer" description="Compare acid–base partners and identify products" defaultOpen>

For a generic acid HA and base B, proton transfer can be represented as HA + B ⇌ A⁻ + BH⁺. The equilibrium favors the side with the weaker acid and weaker base. A useful comparison is the pKa values of HA and BH⁺: equilibrium favors the side with the higher-pKa acid.

<WorkedExample title="Recognize a conjugate pair">
In HF + H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + F⁻, HF donates H⁺ and becomes F⁻; water accepts H⁺ and becomes H₃O⁺. The two conjugate pairs are HF/F⁻ and H₂O/H₃O⁺.
</WorkedExample>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="2. Identify a buffer" description="Both members of the conjugate pair must be present">

A mixture of CH₃COOH and CH₃COO⁻ can act as a buffer. A solution containing only CH₃COOH is not an effective buffer simply because it is a weak acid; it needs a substantial amount of its conjugate base as well. Similarly, NH₃ and NH₄⁺ form a buffer pair.

<QuickCheck question="Which mixture is a buffer?" options={["0.10 M HCl only","0.10 M NaOH only","0.10 M CH₃COOH and 0.10 M CH₃COONa","0.10 M NaCl only"]} correctIndex={2} explanation="The mixture contains a weak acid and its conjugate base in appreciable amounts." />

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="3. What happens when strong acid or base is added?" description="Neutralization consumes one member of the buffer pair">

Added H⁺ is consumed by the conjugate base: A⁻ + H₃O⁺ → HA + H₂O. Added OH⁻ is consumed by the weak acid: HA + OH⁻ → A⁻ + H₂O. The buffer composition changes, but the ratio changes much less than the hydronium concentration would in unbuffered water.

For stoichiometric buffer problems, first calculate moles consumed in the neutralization reaction. Only after that reaction should you calculate the new acid/base ratio. Do not apply an equilibrium formula before accounting for the strong acid or base reaction.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="4. Do neutralization stoichiometry before buffer equilibrium" description="The reaction changes the amounts of the weak acid and its conjugate base">

A buffer often contains a weak acid HA and its conjugate base A⁻. When strong base is added, OH⁻ reacts essentially completely with HA: HA + OH⁻ → A⁻ + H₂O. When strong acid is added, H₃O⁺ reacts with A⁻: A⁻ + H₃O⁺ → HA + H₂O. Only after this stoichiometric reaction should you calculate the remaining HA and A⁻ amounts.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: base added to a buffer">
A buffer starts with 0.030 mol HA and 0.020 mol A⁻. Adding 0.005 mol OH⁻ consumes 0.005 mol HA and forms 0.005 mol A⁻, leaving 0.025 mol HA and 0.025 mol A⁻. For the same final volume, their ratio is 1, so Henderson–Hasselbalch gives pH = pKa. Do not add OH⁻ directly into Ka's equilibrium expression as if it had not reacted.

</WorkedExample>

Buffer capacity depends on the total amounts of the conjugate pair as well as their ratio. Two buffers can have the same pH but very different resistance to added acid or base if one contains much less material. A buffer is effective only while enough of both components remain.

Adding a small amount of strong acid primarily..." options={["converts HA into A⁻","converts A⁻ into HA","changes Ka immediately","removes both buffer components equally"]} correctIndex={1} explanation="Added H₃O⁺ reacts with the conjugate base A⁻, converting it into HA and limiting the pH change." />

</LessonSection>


<LessonSection title="5. Buffer capacity is finite" description="The ratio controls pH; the total amount controls how much acid or base the buffer can absorb">

Buffer **pH** and buffer **capacity** are related but not identical. For the same HA/A⁻ ratio, two buffers have the same approximate pH even if one contains far fewer moles. The more concentrated buffer has more material available to consume added H₃O⁺ or OH⁻ and therefore resists a given addition more effectively.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: same starting pH, different capacity">

Buffer A contains 0.020 mol HA and 0.020 mol A⁻. Adding 0.015 mol OH⁻ leaves 0.005 mol HA and 0.035 mol A⁻. The ratio becomes 7, so the pH rises by `log(7) = 0.845` units above pKa.

Buffer B contains 0.100 mol HA and 0.100 mol A⁻. The same 0.015 mol OH⁻ leaves 0.085 mol HA and 0.115 mol A⁻. Its ratio becomes 1.35, so pH rises by only `log(0.115/0.085) ≈ 0.131` units. Both buffers began at pH = pKa, but Buffer B has much greater capacity.

</WorkedExample>

Capacity is not unlimited. If added OH⁻ consumes all HA, the solution no longer contains both buffer components in meaningful amounts; excess OH⁻ then controls pH. Likewise, adding enough strong acid to consume essentially all A⁻ ends the effective buffer region. Always compare the added amount with the available component moles.

<LessonSection title="6. Calculate buffer pH after a strong-base addition" description="Stoichiometry changes the mole amounts before the buffer equilibrium is evaluated">

When a strong base is added to a buffer, OH⁻ reacts essentially completely with HA: `HA + OH⁻ → A⁻ + H₂O`. Subtract the moles of OH⁻ from HA and add the same amount to A⁻. Only after that neutralization step should you use `pH = pKa + log(nA⁻/nHA)`. Since both species occupy the same final volume, the volume cancels in their concentration ratio, provided both remain in appreciable amounts.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: buffer after added base">

A buffer contains 0.030 mol HA and 0.020 mol A⁻. Add 0.005 mol NaOH. The reaction leaves 0.025 mol HA and produces 0.025 mol A⁻. The new ratio is 1, so pH ≈ pKa. The original ratio was 0.020/0.030, so the initial pH was about pKa − 0.176. Adding base moves the composition toward equal acid/base amounts and raises the pH.

</WorkedExample>

If added OH⁻ equals or exceeds the initial HA amount, do not continue using the buffer equation as though HA remains. First identify any excess strong base, or recognize that one buffer component has been essentially consumed and a different equilibrium calculation is needed.
