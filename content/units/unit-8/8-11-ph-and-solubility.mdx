Solubility can depend on pH when a dissolved ion reacts with H₃O⁺ or OH⁻. Acid often increases the solubility of salts containing basic anions because H₃O⁺ consumes the anion, shifting the dissolution equilibrium toward more dissolved solid.

<LessonSection title="1. Connect dissolution and acid–base reactions" description="Look for an anion that can accept H⁺" defaultOpen>

For a sparingly soluble salt MX(s) ⇌ M⁺(aq) + X⁻(aq), adding acid may protonate X⁻ if X⁻ is a basic anion. Removing X⁻ from solution shifts dissolution to the right. Carbonate, sulfide, and hydroxide are common examples of anions that react with acid.

For a salt with an anion that is the conjugate base of a strong acid, such as Cl⁻, protonation is negligible in ordinary aqueous conditions, so lowering pH may have little direct effect on its solubility.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="2. Apply Le Châtelier's principle" description="Predict direction, then explain the chemical cause">

Consider a metal hydroxide: M(OH)₂(s) ⇌ M²⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq). Adding acid consumes OH⁻ through H₃O⁺ + OH⁻ → 2H₂O. Because a product is removed, more solid dissolves to restore equilibrium. The explanation needs both the acid–base reaction and the equilibrium shift.

<LessonSection title="3. Write a complete justification" description="Name the ion, reaction, and shift">

An AP-style response should identify the ion consumed by acid, show or describe its protonation/neutralization, and state the direction of the dissolution equilibrium. Avoid saying only “the pH changes, so solubility increases”; the mechanistic connection is what supports the prediction.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="4. Predict how pH changes solubility" description="Acid-base reactions can remove one of the ions produced by dissolution">

The solubility of a salt can depend on pH when one of its ions reacts with H₃O⁺ or OH⁻. For a salt containing a basic anion, added acid can consume that anion, lowering its free concentration. The dissolution equilibrium then shifts right to replace it, often increasing the salt's solubility. Salts containing anions that are conjugate bases of weak acids are common examples.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: a carbonate solid in acid">
For CaCO_3(s)⇌ Ca^2 + + CO_3^2 -, added H₃O⁺ reacts with carbonate through proton-transfer steps to form bicarbonate and ultimately carbonic acid/CO₂(aq). Removing free CO₃²⁻ lowers Qsp, so additional CaCO₃ can dissolve. This explains why many carbonates dissolve more readily in acidic solution than in pure water.

</WorkedExample>

Use the coupled equilibria rather than saying “acid always increases solubility.” If the dissolved ion does not react with acid or base, pH may have little direct effect. Common ions can decrease solubility, while acid consumption of a basic anion can increase it; the actual direction depends on the chemical species.
