Acid strength is not determined by the number of hydrogen atoms in a formula. It depends on how readily a species donates H⁺ and how stable the conjugate base is after that proton leaves.

<LessonSection title="1. Compare oxyacids with the same central atom" description="More oxygen atoms can stabilize the conjugate base" defaultOpen>

For oxyacids with the same central atom, additional oxygen atoms often increase acid strength. Electronegative oxygen atoms withdraw electron density, and the conjugate base can distribute negative charge over a larger oxygen framework. For example, HClO₄ is stronger than HClO₂.

This trend is a comparison rule, not a universal shortcut across every acid family. Check whether the acids share the same central atom and structural family before applying it.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="2. Compare binary acids" description="Bond strength and bond polarity both matter">

For binary acids in the same group, acid strength often increases down the group because the H–X bond becomes weaker and is easier to break. For example, HI is a stronger acid than HF in water, despite fluorine's greater electronegativity. Bond strength dominates this down-group comparison.

Across a period, electronegativity and conjugate-base stability are often important, but use the comparison context provided in the question rather than treating one trend as absolute.

<LessonSection title="3. Write an AP-style justification" description="State the structural feature and connect it to stability">

A strong explanation identifies the relevant bond or conjugate-base feature, then connects it to proton donation. For example: “HClO₄ is stronger than HClO₂ because its conjugate base has greater charge delocalization across oxygen atoms, stabilizing the conjugate base and favoring proton transfer.”

Avoid unsupported statements such as “it has more oxygen so it is stronger” without explaining the electronic or structural reason.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="4. Connect molecular structure to acid strength" description="Stability of the conjugate base helps explain proton donation">

For related acids, stronger acidity often corresponds to a more stable conjugate base. Important structural factors include electronegativity, atom size, resonance, and inductive effects. If negative charge can be delocalized over equivalent atoms by resonance, the conjugate base is often stabilized. Electron-withdrawing groups can also stabilize negative charge through the sigma-bond framework.

For oxyacids with the same central atom, adding oxygen atoms often increases acidity because the additional electronegative oxygen atoms withdraw electron density and can stabilize the conjugate base. Comparisons across different central atoms also depend on bond strength and electronegativity, so do not apply a single rule without identifying what changes.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: compare two oxyacids">
For a pair such as HClO and HClO₃, the central atom is the same but the number of oxygen atoms differs. HClO₃ is the stronger acid because its conjugate base has more resonance delocalization and the additional oxygen atoms withdraw electron density, stabilizing the negative charge. The conclusion is structural; it is not based simply on counting hydrogen atoms.

</WorkedExample>

A Lewis structure does not provide a numerical Ka by itself. Structural reasoning predicts a relative trend, while Ka data quantify the equilibrium. Avoid claiming that a resonance structure means atoms physically alternate between drawings; the real electron distribution is delocalized.

<LessonSection title="5. Explain acid strength through conjugate-base stability" description="Structure changes how strongly a conjugate base holds onto a proton">

Acid strength depends on the relative stability of the conjugate base in water. For oxyacids with the same central atom, additional oxygen atoms often withdraw electron density and allow the negative charge in the conjugate base to be distributed over more atoms by resonance. These effects generally stabilize the conjugate base and strengthen the acid. For example, HClO₄ is much stronger than HClO.

For binary acids HX in the same group, bond strength is an important factor. Down the group, the H–X bond generally becomes longer and weaker, making proton transfer easier; this is why HI is a stronger acid than HBr, HCl, and HF in water. This trend differs from the same-central-atom oxyacid trend, so identify which structural comparison the prompt is asking about.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: explain rather than memorize">

To compare HClO₃ and HClO₂, note that both contain the same central atom, Cl, but HClO₃ has one additional oxygen. The additional oxygen withdraws electron density and the conjugate base ClO₃⁻ has more resonance delocalization than ClO₂⁻. These effects stabilize the conjugate base and support stronger acidity for HClO₃.

</WorkedExample>
