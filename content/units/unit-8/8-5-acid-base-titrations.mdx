A titration tracks a solution as measured titrant is added. The equivalence point occurs when stoichiometric amounts have reacted; it is not automatically the point where pH equals 7. The equivalence-point pH depends on the acid and base strengths.

<LessonSection title="1. Solve the neutralization first" description="Use moles and the balanced equation" defaultOpen>

For a monoprotic acid titrated with a strong base, the reaction is HA + OH⁻ → A⁻ + H₂O. Calculate initial moles, calculate added OH⁻ moles, and compare them before doing any pH equilibrium calculation.

Before equivalence, excess weak acid and generated conjugate base may form a buffer. At equivalence, the original weak acid has been converted to its conjugate base, which may react with water. After equivalence, excess strong base controls pH.

<WorkedExample title="Find the equivalence volume">
A 25.0 mL sample of 0.100 M monoprotic acid is titrated with 0.100 M NaOH. Initial acid moles = 0.0250 L × 0.100 mol/L = 0.00250 mol. At equivalence, 0.00250 mol NaOH is required, so volume = 0.00250 mol / 0.100 mol/L = 0.0250 L = 25.0 mL.
</WorkedExample>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="2. Read the curve by region" description="Each region has a different controlling species">

- **Initial solution:** the acid or base alone determines pH; for a weak acid, use Ka.
- **Before equivalence:** both weak acid and conjugate base are present; use their mole ratio after neutralization.
- **Half-equivalence:** for a weak monoprotic acid titrated by strong base, [HA] = [A⁻], so pH = pKa.
- **Equivalence:** only the conjugate species remains in the idealized stoichiometric picture; account for its hydrolysis when weak acid/strong base or weak base/strong acid is involved.
- **After equivalence:** excess strong titrant determines the pH after accounting for total volume.

<LessonSection title="3. Explain curve features chemically" description="Do not infer strength from volume alone">

A weak acid titrated with strong base generally has a buffer region and an equivalence point above pH 7 because its conjugate base reacts with water to make OH⁻. A weak base titrated with strong acid generally has an equivalence point below pH 7. A strong acid–strong base titration has an equivalence point near pH 7 at 25 °C.

The steep region signals that a small volume addition causes a large pH change. An indicator is useful when its color-change range overlaps the steep region.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="4. Read each region of a titration curve" description="The dominant chemistry changes before, at, and after equivalence">

A titration curve is not one continuous formula. Before equivalence, a weak acid titrated by strong base forms a buffer; after neutralization stoichiometry, both HA and A⁻ are present. At half-equivalence, their amounts are equal and pH = pKa under the usual assumptions. At equivalence, the original acid has been stoichiometrically consumed; for a weak acid/strong base titration, A⁻ hydrolyzes water, so the equivalence pH is greater than 7 at 25°C. After equivalence, excess strong base controls pH.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: equivalence volume">
A 25.0 mL sample of 0.120 M monoprotic acid is titrated with 0.100 M NaOH. Initial acid amount is (0.0250)(0.120) = 0.00300 mol. At equivalence, the same moles of NaOH are required, so V = 0.00300 / 0.100 = 0.0300 L = 30.0 mL. At 15.0 mL, the titration is at half-equivalence and pH = pKa if it is a weak-acid/strong-base titration.

</WorkedExample>

For diprotic acids, there may be two equivalence points if the proton-loss steps are sufficiently separated. Always use the balanced reaction and stoichiometric mole ratios rather than assuming every titration has a 1:1 relationship.

<LessonSection title="5. Follow one weak-acid titration through every region" description="The correct calculation changes as the dominant species change">

For 25.00 mL of 0.1000 M HA with `Ka = 1.8×10⁻⁵`, titrated with 0.1000 M NaOH, the initial acid amount is 0.002500 mol and equivalence occurs at 25.00 mL of base. The same curve requires different reasoning at different volumes.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: four regions of one curve">

**Initially (0.00 mL NaOH):** `[H₃O⁺] ≈ √(KaC) = √(1.8×10⁻⁶) = 1.34×10⁻³ M`, so pH ≈ 2.87. Percent ionization is 1.34%, supporting the small-x estimate.

**Half-equivalence (12.50 mL):** Half the HA has reacted, so `n(HA) = n(A⁻)`. Henderson–Hasselbalch gives `pH = pKa = −log(1.8×10⁻⁵) = 4.74`.

**Equivalence (25.00 mL):** All HA has been converted to A⁻. The total volume is 50.00 mL and `[A⁻] = 0.002500/0.05000 = 0.0500 M`. Since `Kb = Kw/Ka = 5.56×10⁻¹⁰`, `[OH⁻] ≈ √(KbC) = 5.27×10⁻⁶ M`, giving pH ≈ 8.72. The pH is above 7 because A⁻ hydrolyzes water.

**After equivalence (30.00 mL):** Added OH⁻ = 0.003000 mol, leaving 0.000500 mol excess in 0.05500 L. Thus `[OH⁻] = 0.00909 M`, pOH ≈ 2.04, and pH ≈ 11.96. Excess strong base, not the buffer ratio, controls pH.

</WorkedExample>

This region-by-region method prevents a common error: using one equation for the whole curve. Stoichiometry determines what remains; equilibrium determines pH only when the controlling species require it.

<LessonSection title="6. Explain why a weak-acid titration has a basic equivalence point" description="At equivalence, the conjugate base remains and reacts with water">

When a weak acid HA is titrated with a strong base, the equivalence point occurs after stoichiometric neutralization of HA. The solution contains A⁻, the conjugate base of the weak acid. A⁻ reacts with water to form HA and OH⁻, so the equivalence-point solution is basic at 25°C. The pH is not automatically 7 simply because moles of acid and base matched.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: determine the chemistry at equivalence">

A weak acid is titrated with NaOH. Before equivalence, added OH⁻ converts some HA into A⁻, producing a buffer. At the half-equivalence point, the remaining HA and formed A⁻ amounts are equal, so pH = pKa. At equivalence, HA has been converted mainly to A⁻; calculate its concentration from the moles of acid originally present and the total mixed volume, then use `Kb = Kw/Ka` to find the OH⁻ generated by A⁻ hydrolysis. After equivalence, excess strong OH⁻ controls pH.

</WorkedExample>

The titration curve encodes changing dominant chemistry. Do not use the same formula for every region: use excess strong acid/base before or after stoichiometric equivalence, buffer relationships in the buffer region, and the conjugate-base equilibrium at the equivalence point for a weak-acid/strong-base titration.
