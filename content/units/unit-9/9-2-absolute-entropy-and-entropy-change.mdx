Unlike standard enthalpies of formation, standard molar entropies S° are not set to zero for elements in their standard states. At a given temperature, each listed substance has a tabulated absolute molar entropy. Use the balanced equation to calculate the entropy change for the reaction as written.

<LessonSection title="1. Use products minus reactants" description="The equation coefficients multiply each molar entropy" defaultOpen>

```text
ΔS°rxn = Σ nS°(products) − Σ nS°(reactants)
```

Multiply each substance's S° by its balanced-equation coefficient. Add product terms, add reactant terms, then subtract. Entropy is often reported in J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, so check units before combining it with ΔH in a Gibbs calculation.

<LessonSection title="2. Calculate and interpret a result" description="Keep coefficients and signs attached to the correct side">

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: standard reaction entropy">

For A(g) → 2B(g), suppose S°(A) = 190 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ and S°(B) = 140 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. Then ΔS°rxn = 2(140) − 190 = **+90 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹**. The positive result is consistent with producing more moles of gas, though the tabulated values determine the numerical answer.

</WorkedExample>

It does not by itself determine enthalpy, rate, or the value of K." />

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="3. Do not use entropy alone to decide spontaneity" description="The surroundings and temperature also matter">

A positive ΔS_system does not guarantee a spontaneous process. The surroundings may lose entropy, especially when the process absorbs heat. At constant temperature and pressure, evaluate ΔG = ΔH − TΔS with consistent units to determine thermodynamic favorability.

</LessonSection>
