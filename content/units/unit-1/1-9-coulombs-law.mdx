# Charge and distance control electrostatic interactions

Coulomb's law describes the electrostatic force between two charged particles. It gives a quantitative version of the idea that charge magnitude and distance determine the strength of an interaction. In AP Chemistry, this model helps explain ion formation, atomic structure, and periodic trends.

Unlike force, potential energy varies inversely with distance rather than distance squared. The sign matters: opposite charges have negative potential energy under the usual zero-at-infinite-separation convention, while like charges have positive potential energy.

The force describes how strongly particles push or pull at a given separation. Potential energy describes the energy associated with their arrangement. Do not apply the inverse-square distance dependence of force to potential energy.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="3. Explain atomic and periodic behavior" description="Translate the model into a chemical argument.">

- A proton attracts an electron because their charges have opposite signs.
- Increasing nuclear charge strengthens attraction to electrons if distance and shielding are otherwise comparable.
- Greater electron-nucleus distance weakens the attraction.
- Electron-electron repulsion can oppose nucleus-electron attraction and contributes to shielding.
- In an ionic lattice, ions with greater charge magnitudes and shorter separations generally interact more strongly.

These ideas support periodic trends, but an atom contains many interacting particles. Use Coulomb's law as a qualitative model rather than pretending that one electron experiences only one isolated charge.

</LessonSection>
