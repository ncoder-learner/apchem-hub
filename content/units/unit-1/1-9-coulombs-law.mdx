Coulomb's law turns the idea of electrostatic attraction into a quantitative model. It explains why charge magnitude and distance matter when comparing electrons and nuclei, ions in a lattice, and the attraction between charged particles.

The first decision is crucial: **is the question asking about force or potential energy?** They depend on distance differently.

<InteractiveLab kind="coulomb" title="Change charge and distance to test Coulomb's inverse-square relationship" />

<LessonSection title="1. Coulomb's law: force between two charges" description="Identify charge, distance, and whether the interaction attracts or repels" defaultOpen>

<ChemVisual kind="coulomb" />

For two point charges, the magnitude of the electrostatic force is:

**F = k × |q₁q₂| / r²**

- F = force magnitude
- k = Coulomb's constant
- q₁ and q₂ = the two charges
- r = distance between the charge centers

The force is **attractive** when the charges have opposite signs and **repulsive** when the charges have the same sign. The equation above gives the magnitude; the signs determine the direction.

Two relationships follow:
- Larger charge magnitudes produce a stronger force when distance is unchanged.
- Greater separation weakens the force according to the inverse square of distance.

The square matters. Doubling the distance makes the force one-fourth as large, not one-half. Tripling distance makes the force one-ninth as large.

<QuickCheck question="If the distance between two charges triples while both charges stay the same, what happens to the force magnitude?" options={["It triples","It becomes one-third as large","It becomes one-ninth as large","It becomes nine times as large"]} correctIndex={2} explanation="Force is inversely proportional to r². Tripling r divides the force by 3² = 9." />

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="2. Compare forces using ratios" description="You usually do not need Coulomb's constant">

When comparing two situations, use the proportionality F ∝ |q₁q₂|/r². This lets you calculate a ratio without knowing the numerical value of k.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example 1: double one charge">
Two charges are separated by the same distance. The magnitude of one charge doubles while the other charge stays the same.

The product |q₁q₂| doubles, and r² is unchanged. Therefore, the force magnitude **doubles**.
</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample title="Worked example 2: double the distance and triple a charge">
In a second situation, one charge is tripled and the separation is doubled; the other charge stays constant.

- Charge product changes by a factor of 3.
- Distance squared changes by a factor of 2² = 4.
- New force / original force = 3/4.

The new force magnitude is **three-fourths of the original force**. Increasing a charge strengthens the force, but increasing the distance weakens it; both effects must be included.
</WorkedExample>

<QuickCheck question="One charge doubles and the separation also doubles, while the other charge stays constant. How does the force magnitude change?" options={["It doubles","It is halved","It is unchanged","It becomes four times as large"]} correctIndex={1} explanation="The charge product doubles, but r² increases by a factor of 4. The net factor is 2/4 = 1/2." />

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="3. Force is not potential energy" description="Potential energy varies as one over distance, not one over distance squared">

Electrostatic potential energy for two point charges is:

**E = k × q₁q₂ / r**

Unlike force, potential energy is inversely proportional to distance, not distance squared. The sign matters under the usual convention that potential energy is zero at infinite separation:
- Opposite charges have negative electrostatic potential energy because the interaction is attractive.
- Like charges have positive electrostatic potential energy because the interaction is repulsive.

The force tells you how strongly the particles push or pull at a given separation. Potential energy describes the energy associated with their arrangement.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example 3: compare force and potential energy">
Two opposite charges are moved from a separation r to 2r.

The force magnitude changes by a factor of 1/2² = 1/4. The potential energy changes in magnitude by a factor of 1/2. Since the charges are opposite, the potential energy remains negative, but it becomes less negative as the charges move farther apart.

This is why force and potential energy cannot be compared using the same distance factor.
</WorkedExample>

<QuickCheck question="If two point charges are moved from distance r to distance 3r, how does the magnitude of their electrostatic potential energy change?" options={["It becomes one-third as large","It becomes one-ninth as large","It triples","It becomes nine times as large"]} correctIndex={0} explanation="Potential energy is proportional to 1/r, so tripling the separation reduces its magnitude to one-third." />

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="4. Apply Coulomb's law to atoms and ions" description="Connect the model to periodic behavior">

Coulomb's law helps explain why nuclear charge, distance, and shielding affect electron behavior.

- A proton attracts an electron because their charges have opposite signs.
- Greater nuclear charge strengthens attraction to an electron if distance and shielding are otherwise comparable.
- Greater electron–nucleus distance weakens attraction.
- Inner electrons repel outer electrons and shield them from some of the nucleus's attraction.
- Ions with larger charge magnitudes and smaller separations generally experience stronger electrostatic interactions.

For an atom, this is a simplified model because many electrons and the nucleus interact simultaneously. Use it to explain trends, not as a claim that each electron feels only one isolated charge.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example 4: compare two isoelectronic ions">
Consider O²⁻ and Mg²⁺. Both have 10 electrons, but Mg²⁺ has 12 protons while O²⁻ has 8. Because the electron populations are the same, the larger nuclear charge in Mg²⁺ pulls the electrons closer. Mg²⁺ is smaller, and its remaining electrons are held more tightly.

This argument works especially well for isoelectronic species because the number of electrons is held constant while nuclear charge changes.
</WorkedExample>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="5. Common reasoning errors" description="Check the variable and the relationship">

<Mistake title="Using the inverse-square relationship for potential energy">
Force follows 1/r²; electrostatic potential energy follows 1/r. If distance triples, force magnitude becomes one-ninth, while potential-energy magnitude becomes one-third.
</Mistake>

<Mistake title="Ignoring charge signs">
The magnitude formula uses absolute values, but whether the force is attractive or repulsive depends on the signs. Opposite charges attract; like charges repel.
</Mistake>

<Mistake title="Assuming charge is the only factor in atomic attraction">
Greater nuclear charge can strengthen attraction, but distance and shielding also matter. When comparing atoms in different shells, do not explain the trend using proton count alone.
</Mistake>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="6. AP-style practice" description="Compare electrostatic forces and explain the result">

Two pairs of point charges are compared.

- Pair A: charges +1 and −1, separated by distance r.
- Pair B: charges +2 and −1, separated by distance 2r.

**(a)** Compare the force magnitudes in A and B.

**(b)** State whether the interaction in each pair is attractive or repulsive.

**(c)** Explain why the charge change and distance change have competing effects.

<LessonSection title="Reveal the reasoning" description="Use proportionality and charge signs">

**(a)** For A, force magnitude is proportional to 1/r². For B, the charge product doubles and the distance squared increases by a factor of 4. Thus B's force magnitude is 2/4 = **one-half** the force magnitude of A.

**(b)** Both pairs have opposite charges, so both interactions are attractive.

**(c)** Doubling one charge strengthens the force by a factor of 2, while doubling distance weakens it by a factor of 4 because distance is squared. The combined factor is 1/2.
</LessonSection>

</LessonSection>
