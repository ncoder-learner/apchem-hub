# Count particles by weighing them

Chemists rarely count atoms one at a time. A sample contains an enormous number of particles, so chemistry uses the **mole** as a counting unit. One mole contains exactly 6.02214076 × 10²³ specified entities. This number is the Avogadro constant. The entities might be atoms, molecules, ions, or formula units; always name what you are counting.

<LessonSection title="1. What a mole actually counts" description="Separate particle count from sample mass." defaultOpen>

A dozen means 12 objects, regardless of whether the objects are grapes or pencils. A mole works the same way, except it represents 6.022 × 10²³ entities. One mole of carbon atoms and one mole of water molecules contain the same number of entities, but they do not have the same mass.

- **Atoms:** 1 mol of Ne contains Nₐ neon atoms.
- **Molecules:** 1 mol of H₂O contains Nₐ water molecules, or 2Nₐ H atoms and Nₐ O atoms.
- **Formula units:** 1 mol of NaCl represents Nₐ NaCl formula units in the ionic solid. It does not mean the solid contains separate NaCl molecules.

<ChemVisual kind="mole-count" />

The key idea: **moles connect the microscopic world to measurable amounts**. The mole is not a mass unit; molar mass provides the conversion between moles and grams.

<LessonSection title="2. Molar mass: grams per mole" description="Read the periodic table and account for every atom.">

**Molar mass** is the mass of one mole of a substance, usually in g/mol. For an element, use its average atomic mass from the periodic table. For a compound, add the atomic masses of every atom shown in its chemical formula, including subscripts and parentheses.

**Worked example: molar mass of Ca(NO₃)₂**

- Ca: 1 × 40.08 = 40.08 g/mol
- N: 2 × 14.01 = 28.02 g/mol
- O: 6 × 16.00 = 96.00 g/mol
- Total: 164.10 g/mol

The subscript 2 outside the parentheses multiplies both N and O. A common error is to count only three oxygen atoms rather than six.

<WorkedExample title="Find the amount of substance in 24.0 g of Mg">
Molar mass of Mg = 24.305 g/mol.

n = m / M = 24.0 g / (24.305 g/mol) = 0.988 mol

The grams cancel, leaving moles. Keep extra digits during calculations and round the final answer to the precision justified by the given data.
</WorkedExample>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="3. Convert between grams, moles, and particles" description="Choose the conversion factor that cancels the starting unit.">

Use these relationships:

| Starting quantity | Conversion | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Grams | divide by molar mass | Moles |
| Moles | multiply by molar mass | Grams |
| Moles | multiply by Nₐ | Particles |
| Particles | divide by Nₐ | Moles |

A reliable setup is dimensional analysis: write the starting quantity, multiply by a fraction equal to 1, and arrange units so unwanted units cancel.

<WorkedExample title="How many CO₂ molecules are in 11.0 g?">
Molar mass CO₂ = 44.01 g/mol.

11.0 g CO_2×(1 mol)/(44.01 g)×frac6.022×10^23 molecules1 mol =1.50×10^23 molecules

The answer is a particle count, so the final unit is molecules, not moles.
</WorkedExample>

<LessonSection title="4. AP exam traps and self-check" description="Use particle identity and unit cancellation to catch mistakes.">

- A mole of molecules is not the same as a mole of atoms if each molecule contains multiple atoms.
- The periodic-table atomic mass is a weighted average for the naturally occurring isotopes, not the mass number of one specific atom.
- Molar mass has units of g/mol; sample mass has units of g.
- Ionic compounds are counted in formula units; covalent compounds are often counted in molecules.
- Significant figures affect the reported result, not the exact definition of the mole.

Before moving on, ask: What entity is being counted? What is the starting unit? Which conversion factor cancels it?
</LessonSection>
