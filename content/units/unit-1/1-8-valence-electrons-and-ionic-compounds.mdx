Valence electrons are the outer electrons most directly involved in bonding and ion formation. The periodic table repeats patterns because elements in the same main-group column have similar valence-electron configurations. Those patterns help predict common ion charges—but a correct formula still has to obey charge balance.

<LessonSection title="1. Count valence electrons" description="Use the main-group columns as a starting pattern" defaultOpen>

<ChemVisual kind="valence-electrons" />

For main-group elements, the group gives a useful first estimate of valence-electron count:

| Main-group column | Typical valence electrons | Common ion pattern |
|---|---:|---|
| Group 1 | 1 | +1 |
| Group 2 | 2 | +2 |
| Group 13 | 3 | +3 |
| Group 15 | 5 | −3 in simple ionic models |
| Group 16 | 6 | −2 |
| Group 17 | 7 | −1 |
| Group 18 | 8, except He has 2 | Usually no common ion |

Metals on the left often lose valence electrons and form cations. Nonmetals on the right often gain electrons and form anions. These patterns are useful, not universal: transition metals can form ions with different charges, and many compounds involve covalent bonding rather than complete electron transfer.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="2. Why ions form and what an ionic solid is" description="Electron transfer is not the whole story">

A simple ionic model describes electron transfer from one atom to another. For example, sodium can lose one valence electron to form Na⁺, while chlorine can gain one electron to form Cl⁻. The oppositely charged ions attract electrostatically.

But do not imagine a crystal as a set of isolated NaCl molecules. Solid NaCl is an **extended ionic lattice**: each ion interacts with multiple neighboring ions. The formula NaCl expresses the simplest whole-number ratio of Na⁺ to Cl⁻ in the lattice.

<ChemVisual kind="ionic-lattice" />

The electron transfer explains how the ions acquire charges. Electrostatic attraction between the ions explains why they form a stable ionic structure. Those are related but distinct parts of the model.

One Ca²⁺ balances the charge of two Cl⁻ ions, giving CaCl₂." />

<LessonSection title="3. Write a neutral ionic formula" description="Balance total charge, then reduce to the simplest ratio">

An ionic compound must have a total charge of zero. Determine the common charge on each ion, then find the smallest whole-number ratio that makes the positive and negative charges cancel.

A reliable procedure:
1. Write the cation and anion with their charges.
2. Find the smallest number of each ion that gives equal total positive and negative charge.
3. Use those numbers as subscripts.
4. Reduce to the simplest ratio if possible.
5. Check that the total charge adds to zero.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example 1: write aluminum oxide">
Aluminum commonly forms Al³⁺; oxygen forms O²⁻.

- Two Al³⁺ ions contribute total charge +6.
- Three O²⁻ ions contribute total charge −6.
- The smallest neutral ratio is 2 Al ions to 3 oxide ions.

The formula is **Al₂O₃**.

Do not simply copy the charge numbers into the formula in reverse order without checking. Charge balance is the reason the ratio is 2:3.
</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample title="Worked example 2: write magnesium nitride">
Magnesium forms Mg²⁺, and nitrogen forms N³⁻.

- Three Mg²⁺ ions contribute +6.
- Two N³⁻ ions contribute −6.

The neutral formula is **Mg₃N₂**. The total charge is 3(+2) + 2(−3) = 0.
</WorkedExample>

<QuickCheck question="What is the correct formula for a compound made from Al³⁺ and S²⁻ ions?" options={["AlS","Al₂S₃","Al₃S₂","Al₂S"]} correctIndex={1} explanation="Two Al³⁺ ions give +6 and three S²⁻ ions give −6. The smallest neutral formula is Al₂S₃." />

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="4. Formulas with polyatomic ions" description="Keep the group intact when it acts as one ion">

A polyatomic ion is a group of covalently bonded atoms with an overall charge. When writing an ionic formula, treat the polyatomic ion as one charged unit, while keeping its internal formula intact.

If more than one copy of a polyatomic ion is required, use parentheses around the ion before adding the subscript.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example 3: write calcium nitrate">
Calcium forms Ca²⁺ and nitrate is NO₃⁻.

One Ca²⁺ requires two nitrate ions to balance the +2 charge. The formula is **Ca(NO₃)₂**. The parentheses and subscript 2 indicate two complete nitrate ions: two N atoms and six O atoms in the formula unit.
</WorkedExample>

<QuickCheck question="What is the correct formula for aluminum sulfate if the ions are Al³⁺ and SO₄²⁻?" options={["AlSO₄","Al₂(SO₄)₃","Al₃(SO₄)₂","Al(SO₄)₃"]} correctIndex={1} explanation="Two Al³⁺ ions give +6; three sulfate ions, each 2−, give −6. The formula is Al₂(SO₄)₃." />

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="5. Use Lewis dots to connect valence electrons to ion formation" description="Show the outer electrons without drawing every inner electron">

A Lewis dot symbol represents an element's valence electrons as dots around its chemical symbol. For main-group elements, the number of dots corresponds to the number of valence electrons. It is a useful model for showing why an atom may lose or gain electrons to form a common ion.

For example, sodium has one valence electron and commonly forms Na⁺ by losing it. Chlorine has seven valence electrons and commonly forms Cl⁻ by gaining one. After transfer, both ions have electron configurations resembling noble gases, but the reason an ionic solid is stable also depends on the electrostatic interactions in the lattice.

The octet rule is a useful pattern for many main-group ions, not a law that every atom must obey in every compound. Some compounds are covalent, some atoms form unusual ions, and transition metals have more complex electron structures.

<Mistake title="Assuming all atoms gain or lose electrons until they have eight">
The octet rule helps predict common main-group ion charges, but it is a model with limits. Do not force every element into an octet or assume every compound forms by complete electron transfer.
</Mistake>

<Mistake title="Writing a non-neutral ionic formula">
The subscripts must make the total charge zero. After writing a formula, multiply each ion's charge by its subscript and add the charges. If the sum is not zero, the formula is wrong.
</Mistake>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="6. AP-style practice" description="Predict ions and construct a formula">

An unknown main-group metal M is in Group 2. A nonmetal X is in Group 17.

**(a)** Predict the common ion charge for M and X.

**(b)** Write the simplest ionic formula for the compound formed from these ions.

**(c)** Explain why the formula uses that ratio rather than one M ion for every two X ions.

<LessonSection title="Reveal the reasoning" description="Use valence patterns and charge balance">

**(a)** Group 2 metal M commonly forms M²⁺ by losing two valence electrons. Group 17 nonmetal X commonly forms X⁻ by gaining one electron.

**(b)** The formula is **MX₂**.

**(c)** One M²⁺ has charge +2, and each X⁻ has charge −1. Two X⁻ ions are required to balance one M²⁺, giving total charge +2 + 2(−1) = 0.
</LessonSection>

</LessonSection>
