# The periodic table encodes atomic structure

Periodic trends are not facts to memorize in isolation. They emerge from three structural ideas: nuclear charge, shielding by other electrons, and the average distance of the valence electrons from the nucleus.

<LessonSection title="1. Effective nuclear charge and shielding" description="Explain why valence electrons feel different attractions." defaultOpen>

The nucleus attracts electrons because it is positively charged. Inner electrons repel outer electrons and partially shield them from the full nuclear charge. **Effective nuclear charge** is the net positive attraction experienced by an electron after shielding is considered.

Across a period, proton count increases while added electrons enter the same principal energy level. Shielding does not increase enough to cancel the additional nuclear charge, so effective nuclear charge generally rises. Down a group, valence electrons occupy higher principal energy levels, are farther from the nucleus, and are more shielded by inner shells.

<ChemVisual kind="periodic-trends" />

This gives a causal chain: stronger effective nuclear attraction tends to pull electrons closer; greater distance and shielding tend to weaken the attraction experienced by valence electrons.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="2. Atomic and ionic radius" description="Compare species with careful attention to charge and electron count.">

**Atomic radius** generally decreases from left to right across a period and increases down a group. Across a period, rising effective nuclear charge pulls the electron cloud closer. Down a group, additional occupied shells increase size.

For ions:
- A cation is usually smaller than its neutral atom because it loses electrons, often losing an entire outer shell, and the remaining electrons experience greater effective attraction.
- An anion is usually larger than its neutral atom because adding electrons increases electron-electron repulsion and spreads the electron cloud.

For an isoelectronic series (same number of electrons), the species with more protons is generally smaller because the same electron population is pulled by a stronger nuclear charge. For example, O²⁻, F⁻, Ne, Na⁺, and Mg²⁺ all have 10 electrons; Mg²⁺ is smallest and O²⁻ largest.

<LessonSection title="3. First ionization energy" description="Connect the trend to the energy needed to remove an electron.">

The **first ionization energy** is the energy required to remove one electron from each atom in one mole of gaseous atoms:

X(g)→ X^+(g)+e^-

It generally increases across a period and decreases down a group. Across a period, valence electrons feel stronger effective nuclear attraction and are harder to remove. Down a group, the outer electron is farther away and more shielded, so it is generally easier to remove.

There are important subshell and pairing exceptions. For example, the first ionization energy of oxygen is slightly lower than nitrogen's because one oxygen 2p orbital contains a pair of electrons, and electron-electron repulsion makes removing one of that pair somewhat easier. Aluminum's first ionization energy is lower than magnesium's because the electron removed from Al is in a higher-energy 3p subshell rather than 3s.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="4. Electronegativity and electron affinity" description="Keep the definitions distinct.">

**Electronegativity** describes an atom's tendency to attract shared electrons in a chemical bond. It generally increases across a period and decreases down a group, with fluorine among the highest.

**Electron affinity** refers to the energy change associated with adding an electron to a gaseous atom. Sign conventions vary across references, so pay attention to whether the question describes energy released or the numerical electron-affinity value. Do not use electron affinity and electronegativity as synonyms.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="5. Explain a trend, not just state it" description="Use the three-part reasoning pattern.">

A strong AP response names the trend, identifies the structural cause, and links that cause to the observed property.

Example: “Atomic radius decreases from Na to Cl because nuclear charge increases across the period while added electrons enter the same principal energy level. The increased effective nuclear charge pulls the electron cloud closer to the nucleus.”

Avoid explanations such as “it decreases because it is farther right.” Position is a description, not the mechanism.
</LessonSection>
