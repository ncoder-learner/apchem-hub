Periodic trends are not facts to memorize in isolation. They emerge from three structural ideas: **nuclear charge**, **shielding by other electrons**, and **distance from the nucleus**. Once you understand how these factors affect an electron, you can explain why atomic size and the energy needed to remove an electron change across the periodic table.

<LessonSection title="1. Build the model: nuclear charge, shielding, and distance" description="Why electrons experience different attractions" defaultOpen>

<ChemVisual kind="periodic-trends" />

The nucleus is positively charged because it contains protons, so it attracts electrons. Inner electrons repel outer electrons and partially shield them from the nucleus. **Effective nuclear charge** is the net positive attraction experienced by an electron after shielding is considered.

Across a period:
- The number of protons increases.
- Added electrons enter the same general principal energy level.
- Shielding does not increase enough to cancel the extra nuclear charge.
- Effective nuclear charge on the valence electrons generally increases.

Down a group:
- Each step adds a new occupied principal energy level.
- Valence electrons are farther from the nucleus.
- More inner electrons shield the valence electrons.
- The nucleus generally attracts the outer electrons less strongly.

This gives a causal model: stronger effective nuclear attraction tends to pull electrons closer; greater distance and shielding tend to weaken the attraction experienced by valence electrons.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="2. Atomic radius: how large is the atom?" description="Compare across a period and down a group">

Atomic radius generally **decreases across a period** and **increases down a group**.

Across a period, effective nuclear charge increases, so the electron cloud is pulled closer to the nucleus. Down a group, the valence electrons occupy a higher principal energy level and are farther from the nucleus, so the atom becomes larger despite the increase in proton count.

Atomic radius is not a hard boundary around an atom. Electron clouds do not end at a sharp edge, so radius is defined operationally from measured distances between atoms. For trend questions, the important point is the relative size.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example 1: compare Na and Cl">
Sodium and chlorine are in the same period. Chlorine has more protons, while the valence electrons are added to the same general principal energy level. The greater effective nuclear charge pulls chlorine's electron cloud closer to the nucleus. Therefore, **Cl has a smaller atomic radius than Na**.
</WorkedExample>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="3. Ionic radius: charge changes the electron cloud" description="Compare cations, anions, and isoelectronic species">

A **cation** is usually smaller than its neutral atom. Losing electrons reduces electron-electron repulsion and may remove the entire outermost shell. The remaining electrons experience a stronger effective attraction per electron.

An **anion** is usually larger than its neutral atom. Adding electrons increases electron-electron repulsion within the valence shell, spreading the electron cloud out.

For an **isoelectronic series**, all species have the same number of electrons. The species with more protons is generally smaller because a larger nuclear charge pulls the same electron population more strongly.

For example, O²⁻, F⁻, Ne, Na⁺, and Mg²⁺ each have 10 electrons. Mg²⁺ has 12 protons and is smallest; O²⁻ has only 8 protons and is largest.

<QuickCheck question="Which species is smallest in the isoelectronic series O²⁻, F⁻, Ne, Na⁺, Mg²⁺?" options={["O²⁻","F⁻","Ne","Mg²⁺"]} correctIndex={3} explanation="All five have 10 electrons. Mg²⁺ has the greatest nuclear charge, so it attracts the same electron population most strongly and has the smallest radius." />

<WorkedExample title="Worked example 2: explain why Na⁺ is smaller than Na">
Neutral Na has 11 protons and 11 electrons, with one electron in the third principal shell. Na⁺ has lost that outer 3s electron, leaving 10 electrons. Its occupied outer shell is now n = 2, and the remaining electrons are held more tightly by the nucleus. Therefore, Na⁺ is much smaller than neutral Na.
</WorkedExample>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="4. First ionization energy: how hard is it to remove an electron?" description="Connect the energy trend to electron attraction">

The **first ionization energy** is the energy required to remove one electron from each atom in one mole of gaseous atoms:

**X(g) → X⁺(g) + e⁻**

First ionization energy generally **increases across a period** because effective nuclear attraction increases and the valence electron is harder to remove. It generally **decreases down a group** because the outer electron is farther from the nucleus and more shielded, making it easier to remove.

There are important small exceptions because subshell energies and electron-electron repulsion matter too:
- **Be has a slightly higher first ionization energy than B.** The electron removed from B is in a higher-energy 2p subshell, which is easier to remove than a 2s electron.
- **N has a slightly higher first ionization energy than O.** Nitrogen's 2p subshell has three unpaired electrons. Oxygen has one paired 2p orbital, and repulsion between the paired electrons makes removing one slightly easier.

These exceptions do not invalidate the main trend. They show why the full electron configuration is needed when comparing nearby elements.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example 3: compare Mg and K">
Magnesium and potassium are in different periods. Potassium's valence electron is in the 4s subshell, farther from the nucleus and shielded by more inner electrons than magnesium's 3s valence electrons. It is generally easier to remove potassium's outer electron, so potassium has a lower first ionization energy than magnesium.
</WorkedExample>

<LessonSection title="5. Electronegativity and electron affinity are different" description="Do not treat all periodic properties as synonyms">

**Electronegativity** describes how strongly an atom attracts shared electrons in a chemical bond. It generally increases across a period and decreases down a group. Fluorine is among the most electronegative elements.

**Electron affinity** describes the energy change when a gaseous atom gains an electron. It is related to the tendency to accept an electron, but its numerical sign convention differs between references and the trend has more exceptions. If a question asks about electron affinity, use the definition and sign convention supplied rather than assuming it is identical to electronegativity.

Ionization energy, electronegativity, and electron affinity all involve electrons, but they measure different processes:
- Ionization energy: remove an electron from a gaseous atom.
- Electron affinity: add an electron to a gaseous atom.
- Electronegativity: attraction for shared electrons in a bond.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="6. Write a complete trend explanation" description="Observation → structural cause → property">

A strong AP explanation names the trend, identifies the structural cause, and links that cause to the observed property.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example 4: explain a radius trend">
**Prompt:** Explain why atomic radius decreases from Na to Cl.

**Complete reasoning:** From Na to Cl, the number of protons increases while added electrons enter the same general principal energy level. Shielding does not fully offset the added nuclear charge, so effective nuclear charge increases and pulls the electron cloud closer to the nucleus. Therefore, atomic radius decreases.

**Why “because chlorine is farther right” is weak:** it describes where chlorine is on the periodic table but does not explain the particle-level cause.
</WorkedExample>

<Mistake title="Using one trend without checking what is being compared">
Atomic radius, ionic radius, ionization energy, and electronegativity are different properties. A question about an isoelectronic series is not the same as a question about neutral atoms down a group. Identify the species and property before applying a trend.
</Mistake>

</LessonSection>
