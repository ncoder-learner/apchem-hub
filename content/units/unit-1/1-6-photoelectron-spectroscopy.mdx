# PES turns electron binding into evidence

Photoelectron spectroscopy (PES) measures the energy needed to remove electrons from a sample. Photons eject electrons, and the instrument measures their kinetic energies. From the photon energy and measured kinetic energy, the binding energy can be determined.

<LessonSection title="1. Understand the axes and peaks" description="Peak position tells binding energy; peak area tells electron count." defaultOpen>

<ChemVisual kind="pes" />

In a typical AP Chemistry PES plot, binding energy is on the horizontal axis and is often shown increasing to the left. Always check the axis labels and direction before interpreting the graph.

- **Binding energy:** the energy needed to remove an electron from a particular state.
- **Peak position:** identifies the relative binding energy of electrons in a subshell.
- **Peak area or integrated intensity:** relates to the number of electrons represented by that peak.
- **More tightly held electrons:** have higher binding energy.

Core electrons are generally more tightly bound than valence electrons because they are closer to the nucleus and experience less shielding from inner electrons. Electrons in different subshells therefore appear at different binding energies.

<LessonSection title="2. Assign peaks to subshells" description="Use relative binding energy and electron capacity.">

For a main-group atom, the subshells with the highest binding energies are usually the inner/core subshells. The outermost occupied subshell typically has the lowest binding energy. Peak sizes can be compared with subshell capacities: an s subshell holds up to 2 electrons, p holds 6, and d holds 10.

A peak area ratio of about 2:6 may be consistent with a filled s subshell and a filled p subshell, if the graph and element support that assignment. Do not assign a peak based on position alone; use the number of peaks, relative areas, known filling order, and total electron count together.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="3. Compare elements using PES" description="Use effective nuclear charge and electron-shell structure.">

For electrons in comparable subshells, an increase in nuclear charge generally increases binding energy, provided the shielding and shell structure are comparable. Across a period, valence electrons often become more tightly bound as effective nuclear charge increases.

Down a group, valence electrons occupy a higher principal energy level and are farther from the nucleus, so their binding energy is generally lower. The precise comparison depends on which subshells are being compared.

**Exam reasoning pattern:** identify the peak, compare binding energy, connect it to nuclear attraction and shielding, then state the conclusion. Avoid claiming that every peak in one element must lie to the left or right of every peak in another without matching comparable subshells.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="4. A reliable PES workflow" description="Count first, assign second, explain last.">

1. Read which direction binding energy increases.
2. Estimate the number of occupied subshell peaks.
3. Compare peak areas to infer electrons per subshell.
4. Assign subshells using relative binding energies and the electron configuration.
5. Check that all peak areas account for the atom's total electron count.
6. For comparisons, explain how nuclear charge, distance, and shielding affect binding energy.

PES data support a model of quantized electron energies. They do not show literal electron trajectories or exact orbital shapes.
</LessonSection>
