Photoelectron spectroscopy (PES) turns electron binding into measurable evidence. A sample is struck with high-energy photons that eject electrons. The instrument measures the kinetic energies of the ejected electrons, allowing the binding energies of the original electrons to be determined.

A PES spectrum helps answer two questions: **How tightly was each group of electrons held, and how many electrons were in that group?**

<LessonSection title="1. Understand the experiment and the axes" description="Peak position and peak size tell you different things" defaultOpen>

<ChemVisual kind="pes" />

![Photoelectron spectroscopy infographic showing subshell peaks, binding-energy positions, and electron counts. Source: LivePhysics, Chemistry: Photoelectron Spectroscopy.](https://cdn.livephysics.com/infographics/chemistry-photoelectron-spectroscopy.png)

The incident photon supplies energy to remove an electron. The energy is used to overcome the electron's binding energy, and any excess becomes the ejected electron's kinetic energy.

**Photon energy = binding energy + kinetic energy**

Therefore:

**Binding energy = photon energy − kinetic energy**

For one experiment, the incident photon energy is known. The instrument measures the photoelectron kinetic energy, so the binding energy can be calculated.

Read a PES graph carefully:
- **Horizontal position:** binding energy. Higher binding energy means electrons are held more tightly. AP-style plots often show binding energy increasing to the left, but always check the axis.
- **Peak size:** represents the relative number of electrons in the subshell. Many simplified AP graphs use peak height; physically, integrated peak area is the more direct population measure.
- **Each peak:** represents a group of electrons with a particular binding energy, usually a subshell in an idealized atomic spectrum.

<LessonSection title="2. Why different subshells have different binding energies" description="Distance from the nucleus and shielding matter">

Electrons are attracted to the positively charged nucleus. Electrons closer to the nucleus generally experience a stronger attraction and are harder to remove. Inner-shell electrons are also shielded less by other electrons than outer-shell electrons are.

That produces a typical pattern:
- **Core electrons** have high binding energies because they are close to the nucleus and experience relatively strong attraction.
- **Valence electrons** have lower binding energies because they are farther from the nucleus and more shielded by inner electrons.

The energy order is not identical to the order of peak size. A 1s peak is usually at very high binding energy but represents only two electrons. A 2p peak can be much taller because it represents up to six electrons even though its electrons are less tightly bound than 1s electrons.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example 1: identify the valence peak">
A PES graph has three peaks. One is at very high binding energy, one at intermediate binding energy, and one at the lowest binding energy.

The lowest-binding-energy peak represents the electrons that are easiest to remove. For a ground-state atom, these are typically the valence electrons in the outermost occupied subshell. The highest-binding-energy peak typically represents the 1s core electrons.
</WorkedExample>

<LessonSection title="3. Use peak sizes to reconstruct electron configurations" description="Count electrons in each occupied subshell">

An electron configuration predicts how many occupied subshells appear in an idealized PES spectrum and how many electrons each peak represents.

- s subshell: maximum 2 electrons
- p subshell: maximum 6 electrons
- d subshell: maximum 10 electrons

A filled 2p subshell contains three times as many electrons as a filled 2s subshell (6 versus 2). If the peaks are drawn with comparable widths, the 2p peak should be about three times as high as the 2s peak. Do not use peak height to infer binding energy; use the horizontal position.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example 2: identify an unknown with three peaks">
An unknown main-group element has a PES spectrum with three peaks from highest to lowest binding energy. Their relative electron populations are 2, 2, and 6.

1. The highest-binding-energy peak is consistent with 1s².
2. The next peak contains 2 electrons, consistent with 2s².
3. The lowest-binding-energy peak contains 6 electrons, consistent with 2p⁶.
4. The configuration is 1s² 2s² 2p⁶, which contains 10 electrons.

The neutral atom is neon (Ne). This conclusion uses both peak positions and peak populations; it is not based on the tallest peak alone.
</WorkedExample>

<LessonSection title="4. Compare two elements" description="Use comparable subshells, not just left-versus-right">

For electrons in comparable subshells, increasing nuclear charge generally increases binding energy if shielding and shell structure are similar. Across a period, protons are added while electrons enter the same principal shell, so the effective nuclear charge experienced by comparable valence electrons often increases.

Down a group, valence electrons occupy a higher principal energy level and are farther from the nucleus, so they are generally less tightly bound and have lower binding energies. A comparison must match the same type of electron or subshell; different shells and subshells can have very different binding energies.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example 3: compare the 2p electrons of nitrogen and oxygen">
Nitrogen and oxygen have electrons in the 2p subshell. Oxygen has one more proton than nitrogen, while the added electron enters the same general shell. The greater nuclear charge increases the attraction experienced by comparable 2p electrons, so oxygen's 2p electrons generally have a higher binding energy than nitrogen's 2p electrons.

The reasoning compares the same subshell across neighboring elements. It does not claim that every oxygen peak must be more tightly bound than every nitrogen peak.
</WorkedExample>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="5. A reliable PES workflow" description="Count first, assign second, explain last">

When given an unfamiliar spectrum:

1. Read the axis labels and identify the direction in which binding energy increases.
2. Count the distinct occupied-subshell peaks.
3. Use peak sizes to estimate how many electrons occupy each subshell.
4. Assign subshells from highest to lowest binding energy, using electron configuration and shell structure.
5. Add the electron counts and compare with the expected atomic number.
6. For an element comparison, explain the shift using nuclear charge, distance, and shielding.

PES is evidence for quantized electron energies. It does not show literal electron trajectories or a photograph of orbitals.

<Mistake title="Assuming the tallest peak has the highest binding energy">
Peak size reflects electron population. Peak position reflects binding energy. A six-electron p subshell can make a taller peak than a two-electron s subshell even though its electrons are less tightly bound.
</Mistake>

<Mistake title="Ignoring the axis direction">
Some spectra place high binding energy on the left. Always inspect the numerical scale; do not assume that moving right always means greater energy.
</Mistake>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="6. AP-style practice" description="Identify an element and explain a peak shift">

A neutral atom has an idealized PES spectrum with five peaks. From highest to lowest binding energy, the peak populations are 2, 2, 6, 2, and 1 electrons.

**(a)** Determine the electron configuration and identify the element.

**(b)** Explain why the first peak (highest binding energy) represents core electrons.

**(c)** The 3p peak of a neighboring element to the right in the same period occurs at a higher binding energy. Explain the trend using atomic structure.

<LessonSection title="Reveal the reasoning" description="Use both population and binding energy">

**(a)** The configuration is 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p¹. The electron count is 13, so the element is aluminum (Al).

**(b)** The first peak represents 1s² electrons. They are closest to the nucleus and shielded least by other electrons, so more energy is required to remove them.

**(c)** Moving to the right across a period increases nuclear charge while the valence electrons remain in the same general principal shell. The effective attraction for comparable 3p electrons generally increases, so their binding energy increases.
</LessonSection>

</LessonSection>
