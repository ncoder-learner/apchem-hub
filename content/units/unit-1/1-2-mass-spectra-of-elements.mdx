# An element can have multiple masses

An element is defined by its proton count, not by one exact mass. Naturally occurring samples can contain different isotopes of the same element. A mass spectrometer separates ions by their mass-to-charge ratio, producing peaks that provide evidence about isotopic masses and relative abundances.

<LessonSection title="1. Read the spectrum" description="Understand the axes and what a peak represents." defaultOpen>

A simplified mass spectrum plots relative abundance against mass-to-charge ratio, often written (m/z). The instrument ionizes particles and separates them according to how they respond to electric or magnetic fields. In common introductory examples, ions have a +1 charge, so (m/z) is numerically close to the isotope's mass.

<ChemVisual kind="mass-spectrum" />

- **Horizontal position:** the mass-to-charge ratio of the detected ion.
- **Peak height or area:** relative abundance, depending on how the spectrum is represented.
- **Base peak:** the most abundant detected ion, normalized to 100% in a relative-intensity spectrum.

A peak at (m/z=24) does not mean every atom of the element has mass 24. It indicates a detected isotope or ion with that mass-to-charge ratio. Real spectra may also contain molecular fragments or multiply charged ions, so interpret the context given in the question.

<LessonSection title="2. Calculate average atomic mass" description="Use a weighted average, not a simple mean.">

The atomic mass shown on a periodic table is generally a **weighted average** of the naturally occurring isotopes. More abundant isotopes contribute more to the average.

average atomic mass=sum(isotope mass)(fractional abundance)

Convert percent abundance to a decimal fraction before multiplying. The fractions should sum to 1.00, or the percentages should sum to 100%.

<WorkedExample title="An element has two isotopes">
Isotope A has mass 10.0 amu and abundance 20.0%; isotope B has mass 11.0 amu and abundance 80.0%.

(10.0)(0.200)+(11.0)(0.800)=10.8 amu

The average is closer to 11.0 amu because isotope B is much more abundant. A simple average of 10.0 and 11.0 would incorrectly assume equal abundance.
</WorkedExample>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="3. Work backward from average mass" description="Set the isotope fractions before solving.">

For a two-isotope element, let (x) be the fractional abundance of isotope A. Then isotope B has fraction (1-x).

average mass=m_Ax+m_B(1-x)

Expand, solve for (x), and convert the fraction to a percentage. Check that the result lies between 0 and 1. If the average mass is closer to isotope A's mass, isotope A must be the more abundant isotope.

**Common error:** treating percent abundance as a whole number. If abundance is 35%, use 0.35 in the weighted-average equation, not 35.
</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="4. Exam reasoning" description="Explain what the data support, without overclaiming.">

- Isotopes have the same number of protons and different numbers of neutrons.
- A larger peak generally indicates greater relative abundance, not a greater charge or stronger bond.
- Average atomic mass may be non-integer because it averages isotope masses and abundances.
- If isotope abundances change between samples, the measured average can change even though the element identity does not.

When explaining a spectrum, refer to peak positions for isotope masses and relative peak sizes for abundance.
</LessonSection>
