An element is defined by its number of protons, not by one exact mass. Many elements occur naturally as mixtures of isotopes: atoms with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons. A mass spectrum gives experimental evidence about the masses of those isotopes and how abundant each one is.

<LessonSection title="1. Read a mass spectrum" description="Position gives mass-to-charge ratio; peak size gives abundance" defaultOpen>

A simplified mass spectrum plots **mass-to-charge ratio (m/z)** on the horizontal axis and **relative abundance** on the vertical axis. In the usual introductory AP Chemistry isotope questions, the detected ions have a +1 charge, so m/z is approximately the isotope's mass.

See [Chemistry Student's mass-spectrometry explanation](https://www.chemistrystudent.com/ap-chemistry/1-atomic-structure/mass-spectrometry.html) for a sourced isotope-spectrum example.

Read the spectrum in two separate ways:

- **Peak position:** tells you the mass-to-charge ratio of the detected ion. For a +1 isotope ion, this identifies the isotope's approximate mass.
- **Peak height or area:** indicates relative abundance, depending on how the instrument's spectrum is represented. A taller isotope peak means that isotope is more abundant in the sample.

The number of distinct isotope peaks shows how many isotopes are detected in the simplified spectrum. The peak at m/z 37 does not mean chlorine has 37 protons; it indicates an isotope with mass number 37 and 17 protons.

<LessonSection title="2. Why isotopes have different masses" description="Same protons, different neutrons">

Isotopes of the same element have:
- the same number of protons, so they are the same element;
- different numbers of neutrons, so they have different mass numbers;
- nearly the same chemical behavior in ordinary reactions, because their electron structures are the same.

For example, chlorine-35 and chlorine-37 each have 17 protons. Chlorine-35 has 18 neutrons; chlorine-37 has 20 neutrons. Both are chlorine, but their masses differ.

The mass spectrum is experimental evidence for this isotope mixture. It does not imply that one isotope is a different element.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="3. Calculate average atomic mass" description="Use a weighted average, not a simple mean">

The atomic mass on the periodic table is generally a weighted average of the masses of naturally occurring isotopes. A more abundant isotope contributes more to the average.

**Average atomic mass = Σ(isotope mass × fractional abundance)**

Convert each percentage to a decimal before multiplying. For example, 75% becomes 0.75. All fractional abundances should add to 1.00, or all percentage abundances should add to 100%.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example 1: calculate chlorine's average atomic mass">
Suppose chlorine has two isotopes:
- Cl-35: mass 34.97 u, abundance 75.8%
- Cl-37: mass 36.97 u, abundance 24.2%

Convert percentages to decimals and calculate:

Average mass = (34.97)(0.758) + (36.97)(0.242)

= 26.51 + 8.95 = **35.46 u** (rounded).

The answer is closer to 34.97 u because chlorine-35 is more abundant. A simple average of the two isotope masses would incorrectly assume they occur in equal amounts.
</WorkedExample>

<LessonSection title="4. Work backward from the average" description="Solve for an unknown isotope abundance">

If an element has two isotopes and the abundance of one is x, the other isotope's abundance is 1 − x. Use the weighted-average equation:

Average mass = (mass A)(x) + (mass B)(1 − x)

Then solve for x. If you use percentages instead, make sure they add to 100%.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example 2: find an unknown abundance">
An element has isotopes with masses 10.0 u and 11.0 u. Its average atomic mass is 10.8 u. Find the abundance of the 10.0 u isotope.

Let x be the fraction of the 10.0 u isotope. The other fraction is 1 − x.

10.8 = (10.0)x + (11.0)(1 − x)

10.8 = 10.0x + 11.0 − 11.0x

−0.2 = −1.0x, so x = 0.20.

The 10.0 u isotope is **20% abundant**, and the 11.0 u isotope is 80% abundant. The average is closer to 11.0 u, so the heavier isotope must be more abundant.
</WorkedExample>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="5. Common traps" description="Separate mass, abundance, and element identity">

<Mistake title="Using a simple average">
If two isotopes are present in unequal amounts, averaging their masses equally is wrong. Multiply each isotope's mass by its fractional abundance first, then add the contributions.
</Mistake>

<Mistake title="Reading peak height as mass">
The horizontal position gives m/z; peak height or area gives relative abundance. A taller peak means more of that isotope was detected, not that it has a greater mass.
</Mistake>

<Mistake title="Confusing mass number and atomic number">
Mass number = protons + neutrons. Atomic number = protons only. Isotopes have the same atomic number but different mass numbers.
</Mistake>

<Mistake title="Forgetting that fractions must add to one">
If one isotope has abundance x, the other has abundance 1 − x. If you use percentages, the total must be 100%. A calculation that produces a negative abundance or a value above 100% signals an error.
</Mistake>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="6. AP-style practice" description="Read a spectrum and calculate the weighted average">

A simplified mass spectrum of element Q has two isotope peaks:

| Isotope | Approximate mass | Relative abundance |
|---|---:|---:|
| Q-10 | 10.0 u | 30.0% |
| Q-11 | 11.0 u | 70.0% |

**(a)** Calculate the average atomic mass of Q.

**(b)** Explain why the average atomic mass is not 10.5 u.

**(c)** A second sample has the same two isotopes, but the Q-10 peak is taller relative to Q-11. Predict how the sample's average atomic mass compares with the first sample.

<LessonSection title="Reveal the reasoning" description="Check the calculation and the evidence">

**(a)** Average mass = (10.0)(0.300) + (11.0)(0.700) = 3.00 + 7.70 = **10.70 u**.

**(b)** 10.5 u is the simple average that would apply only if the isotopes were equally abundant. Q-11 makes up 70% of the sample, so it contributes more to the weighted average.

**(c)** The second sample's average atomic mass is lower than the first sample's because the lighter isotope Q-10 is more abundant. The isotope identities do not change; only their relative amounts change.
</LessonSection>

</LessonSection>
