# A formula is a ratio of atoms

A compound's formula describes the relative numbers of atoms in its chemical composition. Experimental data often begin with masses, while a formula requires a ratio of particles. The conversion path is usually **mass → moles → simplest whole-number ratio**.

<LessonSection title="1. Percent composition by mass" description="Find what fraction of a compound’s mass comes from each element." defaultOpen>

Mass percent is the mass contributed by one element divided by the total compound mass, multiplied by 100%.

% element=(mass of element in 1 mol compound)/(molar mass of compound)×100%

For water, H₂O has a molar mass of approximately 18.02 g/mol. Hydrogen contributes about 2.016 g/mol, so hydrogen's mass percent is (2.016/18.02×100%=11.2%. Oxygen accounts for the remaining 88.8%.

Check that the percentages of all elements add to approximately 100%; small differences can result from rounding.

<LessonSection title="2. Determine an empirical formula" description="Convert each element’s mass to moles, then divide by the smallest.">

The **empirical formula** is the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms. If you are given percentages, assume a 100 g sample: each percentage becomes the same numerical mass in grams. If actual masses are given, use those values directly.

Procedure:

1. Convert each element's mass to moles using its molar mass.
2. Divide every mole amount by the smallest mole amount.
3. If ratios are close to whole numbers, round appropriately.
4. If ratios are near familiar fractions, multiply all ratios by a common integer to obtain whole numbers.

<WorkedExample title="Find the empirical formula from 40.0% C, 6.7% H, and 53.3% O">
Assume 100.0 g: 40.0 g C, 6.7 g H, 53.3 g O.

- C: (40.0/12.01=3.33) mol
- H: (6.7/1.008=6.65) mol
- O: (53.3/16.00=3.33) mol

Divide by 3.33: C : H : O ≈ 1 : 2 : 1. The empirical formula is CH₂O.
</WorkedExample>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="3. Empirical formula vs. molecular formula" description="The molecular formula is a whole-number multiple of the empirical formula.">

The empirical formula gives the simplest ratio; the molecular formula gives the actual atom count in a molecule. Determine the empirical-formula mass, then compare it with the compound's measured molar mass.

n=(molecular molar mass)/(empirical-formula mass)

The ratio (n) should be a positive whole number within experimental uncertainty. Multiply every empirical-formula subscript by (n).

For example, empirical formula CH₂O has mass about 30.03 g/mol. If the measured molar mass is about 180.2 g/mol, (napprox6), so the molecular formula is C₆H₁₂O₆.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="4. Check the chemistry and the math" description="Avoid rounding too early.">

- Keep several digits during mole calculations; round the final ratio only when the data support it.
- A ratio of 1.50 is not 1.5 atoms. Multiply all ratios by 2 to obtain a 3:2 ratio.
- An empirical formula does not always identify one unique molecule.
- Mass percentages should total about 100%.
- The molecular formula must be an integer multiple of the empirical formula.

If a ratio is far from a plausible whole-number or simple fractional ratio, recheck arithmetic, molar masses, units, and the experimental data before forcing it to an integer.
</LessonSection>
