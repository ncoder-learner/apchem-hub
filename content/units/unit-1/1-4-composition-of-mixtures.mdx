# Mixtures contain more than one substance

A pure substance has a fixed chemical identity and composition. A mixture contains two or more substances physically combined; its overall composition can vary. Mixtures may be homogeneous, with uniform composition at the sampled scale, or heterogeneous, with distinguishable regions or phases.

<LessonSection title="1. Classify the particles" description="Focus on the species present, not how many colors are drawn." defaultOpen>

<ChemVisual kind="mixture" />

- **Pure element:** one type of atom, such as a sample containing only Ne atoms.
- **Pure compound:** one chemical species with a fixed elemental ratio, such as pure H₂O.
- **Homogeneous mixture:** multiple species distributed uniformly, such as salt dissolved in water.
- **Heterogeneous mixture:** composition differs from place to place, such as sand mixed with iron filings.

A sample can look uniform and still be a mixture. A solution is homogeneous at the molecular level, but it contains solvent particles and dissolved solute species. Do not confuse a uniform appearance with a pure substance.

<LessonSection title="2. Mass fraction and percent composition" description="Compare the mass of one component with the total mixture mass.">

For a mixture with components A and B,

mass percent A=(m_A)/(m_mixture)×100%

For a mixture containing only A and B, (m_mixture=m_A+m_B). Mass percent is not the same as particle percent or volume percent; the question's stated basis matters.

<WorkedExample title="Find the mass percent of salt">
A 25.0 g sample of salt water contains 3.5 g of dissolved NaCl.

% NaCl=(3.5 g)/(25.0 g)×100%=14%

The denominator is the total mass of the solution, not just the water. The remaining 86% by mass is water, assuming only these two components are present.
</WorkedExample>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="3. Use particle models and conservation" description="Physical mixing does not automatically create a new compound.">

In a physical mixture, the component substances retain their chemical identities unless a chemical reaction occurs. Separation can often use differences in physical properties: filtration for an insoluble solid in a liquid, distillation for components with different volatilities, or chromatography for different attractions to stationary and mobile phases.

When interpreting particle diagrams, count each particle type and distinguish individual atoms from bonded groups. If a diagram shows intact water molecules alongside dissolved ions, it represents a solution, not a new compound whose formula combines all the symbols in the picture.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="4. AP-style checks" description="Name the denominator and the composition basis.">

- Mass percent uses mass divided by total mass.
- Particle percent uses number of particles divided by total particle count.
- A pure compound has a fixed composition; a mixture does not have to.
- Homogeneous and pure are not synonyms.
- If a component is removed physically, the mixture's composition changes even though the identity of the remaining components may not.

Always state whether your calculation refers to the total mixture, one phase, or one chemical species.
</LessonSection>
