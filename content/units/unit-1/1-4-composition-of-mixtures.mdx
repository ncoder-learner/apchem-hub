A sample can look uniform and still contain more than one substance. To determine whether a sample is pure or a mixture, identify the chemical species present—not just the number of visible colors or phases. To calculate composition, identify what fraction of the total sample is made up by each component.

<LessonSection title="1. Pure substances versus mixtures" description="Classify the species present" defaultOpen>

<ChemVisual kind="mixture" />

A **pure substance** contains one chemical species with a fixed identity and composition. It may be an element, such as Ne, or a compound, such as H₂O.

A **mixture** contains two or more substances physically combined. The proportions can vary from sample to sample. The components retain their chemical identities unless a chemical reaction occurs.

- **Pure element:** one type of atom, such as a sample containing only Ne atoms.
- **Pure compound:** one chemical species with a fixed elemental ratio, such as pure H₂O.
- **Homogeneous mixture:** multiple species distributed uniformly at the sampled scale, such as salt water.
- **Heterogeneous mixture:** composition differs from place to place or distinct phases are present, such as sand mixed with iron filings.

A solution is homogeneous but not pure: it contains solvent particles and dissolved solute species. Uniform appearance is not proof of purity.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="2. Read particle diagrams as evidence" description="Count distinct species, not just circles">

When interpreting a particle diagram, begin with the key. A circle may represent an atom, an ion, or an entire molecule depending on the diagram. Do not infer chemical bonds from colors or proximity alone; use the key and the drawing's conventions.

A particle diagram of pure O₂ contains only O₂ molecules. A diagram of a mixture of O₂ and N₂ contains two molecular species. A diagram of salt water may show water molecules and separate Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions. It is a mixture because multiple chemical species are present, even if they are evenly distributed.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example 1: classify a particle diagram">
A diagram contains identical triatomic molecules throughout the box, with no other species shown.

If each symbol represents one molecule of the same compound, the sample is a pure compound. The fact that the molecule contains more than one element does not make the sample a mixture; every particle is the same chemical species.

Now suppose half the box contains those molecules and half contains different diatomic molecules. If the two species are physically mixed and not chemically bonded into one new compound, the sample is a mixture.
</WorkedExample>

<LessonSection title="3. Mass fraction and mass percent" description="Choose the total mixture mass as the denominator">

Mass percent describes how much of a mixture's total mass comes from one component.

**Mass percent of A = (mass of A ÷ total mass of mixture) × 100%**

If the mixture contains only A and B, total mass = mass A + mass B. Mass percent is not the same as particle percent or volume percent; use the composition basis specified in the question.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example 2: mass percent of salt in salt water">
A 25.0 g sample of salt water contains 3.50 g NaCl.

Mass percent NaCl = (3.50 g / 25.0 g) × 100% = **14.0%**.

The denominator is the total mass of the solution, not the mass of the water alone. If only salt and water are present, the water mass is 25.0 − 3.50 = 21.5 g, so the water mass percent is 86.0%.
</WorkedExample>

<QuickCheck question="A 40.0 g mixture contains 10.0 g of component A. What is the mass percent of A?" options={["10.0%","25.0%","30.0%","40.0%"]} correctIndex={1} explanation="Mass percent = (10.0 g / 40.0 g) × 100% = 25.0%." />

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="4. Different composition measures answer different questions" description="Mass fraction is not particle fraction">

**Mass percent** compares mass. **Particle fraction** compares the number of specified particles. **Volume percent** compares volumes and is used only when the problem defines that basis. These quantities can differ greatly because different substances have different molar masses and densities.

For example, a mixture can contain equal numbers of molecules of A and B but have a mass that is mostly contributed by B if B's molecules are much heavier. Equal particle counts do not imply equal masses.

When a problem gives mass data and asks for mass percent, do not convert to moles unless needed for another part. When it gives a particle diagram and asks for relative particle count, count the particles represented by the key.

<Mistake title="Using the wrong denominator">
Mass percent uses the total mixture mass. If a solution contains 3.5 g salt and 21.5 g water, the total is 25.0 g. Dividing the salt mass by the water mass gives a salt-to-water ratio, not the salt mass percent of the solution.
</Mistake>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="5. Physical mixtures and separation" description="Components can retain their identities while being separated">

Mixing is a physical process unless a chemical reaction occurs. The component species can often be separated using differences in physical properties:
- filtration can separate suspended insoluble particles from a fluid;
- distillation can separate components with different volatilities;
- chromatography can separate dissolved components that interact differently with mobile and stationary phases;
- evaporation or crystallization can recover a dissolved solid under suitable conditions.

Choosing a method requires knowing the kind of mixture and the physical property that differs. A dissolved ion is not a suspended chunk, so ordinary filtration does not remove dissolved salt from water.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="6. AP-style practice" description="Classify and calculate from a short data set">

A sample contains 12.0 g of sodium chloride and 88.0 g of water.

**(a)** Is the sample a pure substance or a mixture? If it is a mixture, classify it as homogeneous or heterogeneous if the salt is fully dissolved.

**(b)** Calculate the mass percent of NaCl.

**(c)** A student says the sample is pure because it looks clear. Explain the error.

<LessonSection title="Reveal the reasoning" description="Use species identity and the total mass">

**(a)** It is a homogeneous mixture because water and dissolved Na⁺/Cl⁻ species are present and uniformly distributed.

**(b)** Total mass = 12.0 + 88.0 = 100.0 g. Mass percent NaCl = (12.0/100.0) × 100% = **12.0%**.

**(c)** A clear appearance does not prove purity. Salt water contains more than one chemical species even though the solution is uniform and transparent.
</LessonSection>
