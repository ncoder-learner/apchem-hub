# Electrons occupy quantized states

The modern atomic model does not describe electrons as tiny planets following exact circular paths. It describes electrons using quantum states and orbitals: regions of space associated with high probability of finding an electron. Electron configurations summarize how electrons occupy these states.

<LessonSection title="1. Protons, neutrons, and electrons" description="Use nuclear charge and mass number to identify an atom or ion." defaultOpen>

The **atomic number** (Z) equals the number of protons and identifies the element. The **mass number** (A) equals protons plus neutrons for one isotope.

neutrons=A-Z

A neutral atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons. An ion has gained or lost electrons: a cation is positive because it has fewer electrons than protons, and an anion is negative because it has more electrons than protons.

<LessonSection title="2. Orbitals and quantum numbers" description="Know what s, p, d, and f describe.">

An orbital is a quantum state that can hold up to two electrons with opposite spins. Subshells contain orbitals:

| Subshell | Number of orbitals | Maximum electrons |
|---|---:|---:|
| s | 1 | 2 |
| p | 3 | 6 |
| d | 5 | 10 |
| f | 7 | 14 |

The principal quantum number (n) describes the main energy level and is related to orbital size. The angular-momentum quantum number identifies the subshell type. Magnetic quantum numbers distinguish orbitals within a subshell, and spin quantum number distinguishes the two allowed spin states.

These are not decorative labels: the allowed quantum states constrain electron occupancy and help explain periodic structure.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="3. Fill electron configurations" description="Apply three rules consistently.">

**Aufbau principle:** fill lower-energy orbitals before higher-energy orbitals in the usual ground-state ordering. **Pauli exclusion principle:** an orbital holds at most two electrons, and they must have opposite spins. **Hund's rule:** orbitals of equal energy fill singly with parallel spins before pairing.

<ChemVisual kind="electron-configuration" />

A common filling sequence begins (1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p). The energy ordering has exceptions for some atoms, especially transition metals, so use the configuration convention taught for the problem and know the common exceptions if required.

**Example: sulfur, Z = 16**

1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 3s^2 3p^4

The superscripts add to 16 electrons. A noble-gas shorthand is ([Ne]3s^2 3p^4). For transition-metal cations, electrons are generally removed from the highest principal energy level first; for example, 4s electrons are removed before 3d electrons when forming common first-row transition-metal cations.
