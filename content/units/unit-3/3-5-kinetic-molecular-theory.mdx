Kinetic molecular theory (KMT) connects what we can measure about a gas—pressure, temperature, and volume—to the motion of particles we cannot see. It is a model: deliberately simple assumptions that explain many gas behaviors and also help us predict when real gases will differ.

<LessonSection title="1. Build the ideal-gas particle model" description="What the model assumes" defaultOpen>

<Unit3Visual kind="kinetic" />

For an ideal gas, the kinetic molecular theory model assumes:

1. Gas particles are in constant, random motion.
2. The particles' individual volumes are negligible compared with the volume of the container.
3. Particles exert no significant attractive or repulsive forces on one another except during collisions.
4. Collisions between particles and with the container walls are elastic: total kinetic energy is conserved in a collision.
5. The average translational kinetic energy of the particles is proportional to the absolute temperature in kelvin.

These assumptions are not perfectly true for real gases. They are useful when particles are far apart and moving with enough kinetic energy that attractions matter relatively little.

## Why a gas has pressure

A gas particle travels through the container and collides with a wall. During a collision, its momentum changes, so it exerts a force on the wall. A huge number of collisions produce the pressure we measure. Pressure is force per unit area.

This particle picture predicts:
- More particles in the same volume and at the same temperature → more wall collisions per second → higher pressure.
- Higher temperature at the same volume and amount → higher average kinetic energy and typically more forceful wall collisions → higher pressure.
- Larger volume at the same amount and temperature → fewer collisions with each unit area of wall per unit time → lower pressure.

<LessonSection title="2. Temperature and average kinetic energy" description="The meaning of temperature at the particle level">

For an ideal gas, the average translational kinetic energy depends only on kelvin temperature. A higher temperature means greater average kinetic energy. At the same temperature, different ideal gases have the same average translational kinetic energy per particle.

That last statement surprises students. A helium atom and a xenon atom at the same temperature do **not** have the same average speed. They have the same average translational kinetic energy, but xenon is much more massive. Since kinetic energy depends on both mass and speed, a lighter particle must move faster on average to have the same kinetic energy.

Conceptually:

**Average kinetic energy ∝ temperature in kelvin**

For an ideal gas, the average translational kinetic energy per particle is (3/2)kT, where k is Boltzmann's constant. You usually do not need this expression to answer a conceptual AP question, but it explains why kelvin temperature—not Celsius—is the relevant scale.

<LessonSection title="3. Particle speeds form a distribution" description="Why one average speed does not describe every particle">

Gas particles do not all move at one exact speed. At a given temperature, some particles move more slowly than average and others move more quickly. A speed-distribution graph represents how many particles have different speeds.

When temperature increases:
- The distribution spreads toward higher speeds.
- The most probable speed increases.
- The average kinetic energy increases.

The curve does not mean that every particle's speed rises by exactly the same amount. Particles continue to collide and exchange energy, so their individual speeds constantly change.

At the same temperature, a lighter gas has a speed distribution shifted toward higher speeds than a heavier gas. The average kinetic energies remain equal, but the masses differ.

<LessonSection title="4. Connect KMT to the gas laws" description="Derive the trends from particle collisions">

KMT gives a particle-level explanation for the ideal gas law relationships.

- **Increase temperature at fixed volume and amount:** particles move faster on average and collide with the walls more forcefully, so pressure increases.
- **Decrease volume at fixed temperature and amount:** particles travel a shorter distance between wall collisions and strike the walls more frequently, so pressure increases.
- **Add gas particles at fixed temperature and volume:** there are more particles available to collide with the walls, so pressure increases.
- **Increase volume at fixed temperature and pressure:** the gas can occupy more space, allowing the same amount of gas to maintain the same collision rate per unit wall area.

These are different ways of describing the same relationship. The equation PV = nRT summarizes the macroscopic result; KMT explains why the relationship makes sense.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example 1: two containers">
Two rigid containers have the same volume and temperature. Container A holds twice as many moles of ideal gas as container B.

At the same temperature, the average kinetic energy per particle is the same in both containers. But container A has twice as many particles in the same volume, so there are more wall collisions per unit time. Its pressure is twice as high under the ideal-gas model.
</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample title="Worked example 2: same temperature, different gases">
A sealed container contains equal numbers of helium and xenon atoms at the same temperature.

The atoms have the same average translational kinetic energy because the temperature is the same. Helium atoms have much less mass, so their typical speeds are greater. Their speeds differ even though their average kinetic energies do not.
</WorkedExample>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="5. What the model does not say" description="Avoid common reasoning traps">

<Mistake title="“At the same temperature, every gas has the same speed”">
Temperature determines average translational kinetic energy, not average speed by itself. Particle mass also matters. Lighter particles move faster on average at the same temperature.
</Mistake>

<Mistake title="“Gas particles have no forces under all real conditions”">
The no-significant-attractions assumption belongs to the ideal-gas model. Real particles attract one another. Those attractions become more important at lower temperatures and higher pressures, which is why real gases deviate from ideal behavior.
</Mistake>

<Mistake title="“The particles themselves expand when heated”">
The particles do not become larger in the KMT model. Heating increases their average kinetic energy. If pressure is held constant, the gas expands because the container volume increases, not because each particle grows.
</Mistake>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="6. AP-style practice" description="Use the model to justify a prediction">

A rigid container holds 0.20 mol of an ideal gas at 250 K. A second identical container holds 0.40 mol of the same ideal gas at 250 K.

**(a)** Compare the average translational kinetic energy per particle in the two containers.

**(b)** Compare their pressures and explain your prediction using particle collisions.

**(c)** The gas in the second container is heated to 500 K without changing its volume or amount. Predict the new pressure relative to the first container's original pressure.

<LessonSection title="Reveal the reasoning" description="Connect temperature, number of particles, and wall collisions">

**(a)** The average translational kinetic energy per particle is the same because both gases are at 250 K.

**(b)** The second container has twice as many moles at the same temperature and volume, so it has twice the pressure. There are twice as many particles producing wall collisions in the same space.

**(c)** The second container originally has twice the first container's pressure. Doubling its kelvin temperature at fixed amount and volume doubles its own pressure again. Its final pressure is four times the first container's original pressure.
</LessonSection>
