When a substance dissolves, its particles do not simply vanish. Solute particles separate from one another and become surrounded by solvent particles. Whether that process is favorable depends on the interactions that must be disrupted and the new interactions that form.

The central question is: **Are the new solute–solvent interactions favorable enough to make mixing occur?**

<LessonSection title="1. What a solution is at the particle level" description="Solute, solvent, and uniform distribution" defaultOpen>

<Unit3Visual kind="solutions" />

A **solution** is a homogeneous mixture: its composition is uniform at the scale being considered. The **solute** is the substance being dissolved; the **solvent** is the medium doing the dissolving and is often the component present in greater amount.

In salt water, NaCl is the solute and water is the solvent. In sugar water, sugar molecules are the solute and water molecules are the solvent. The resulting solution may look clear, but the dissolved particles are still present. Salt water conducts electricity because mobile ions are present; sugar water usually does not conduct well because dissolved sugar remains as neutral molecules.

<LessonSection title="2. What has to happen for a solution to form?" description="Break some interactions and form others">

Think of solution formation as three particle-level changes:

1. Solute particles must separate from other solute particles. This requires disrupting solute–solute attractions.
2. Solvent particles must make room for the solute. This requires disrupting some solvent–solvent attractions.
3. New solute–solvent interactions form as the solute disperses through the solvent.

The energy changes and entropy change associated with these steps together determine whether mixing is thermodynamically favorable. For AP Chemistry, do not decide solubility from a single force label alone. Compare the kinds of interactions present before and after mixing, and recognize that mixing also changes the number of possible particle arrangements.

<Lightbulb title="Why “like dissolves like” is only a starting point">
Polar solvents tend to dissolve polar or ionic solutes more effectively because they can form favorable electrostatic interactions. Nonpolar solvents tend to dissolve nonpolar solutes because dispersion interactions are compatible. But “like dissolves like” is a shortcut, not a complete mechanism: the actual outcome depends on all the interactions being disrupted and formed, plus entropy and conditions.
</Lightbulb>

## Molecular solutes in molecular solvents

Polar molecules can interact with polar solvents through dipole–dipole attractions and sometimes hydrogen bonding. Nonpolar molecules interact mainly through dispersion forces. A polar solute is often more soluble in a polar solvent than in a nonpolar one because the solvent can form more favorable interactions with it.

## Ionic solutes in water

Water is polar. Its oxygen end is partially negative and is attracted to cations; its hydrogen ends are partially positive and are attracted to anions. Water molecules surround the ions in a process called **hydration**. Ion–dipole attractions help stabilize separated ions in the solution.

Dissolving an ionic solid does not necessarily mean every ionic compound is highly soluble. The attractions between ions in the solid lattice can be strong, and solubility depends on the balance of interactions and the relevant thermodynamics.

<LessonSection title="3. Predict whether two liquids mix" description="Use molecular structure and the interactions that can form">

Two liquids that mix uniformly are **miscible**; liquids that separate into layers are **immiscible** or only partly miscible under those conditions.

Water and ethanol mix because ethanol has a polar O–H group that can form hydrogen-bonding interactions with water. Hexane is nonpolar and cannot form hydrogen bonds with water; mixing disrupts water–water hydrogen bonding without replacing it with comparably favorable interactions, so water and hexane form separate layers.

Molecular structure matters. A large molecule may contain both polar and nonpolar regions. The presence of one polar bond does not automatically make the whole molecule highly polar or water-soluble. Consider the whole molecular shape and the relative sizes of polar and nonpolar regions.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example 1: water and ethanol">
**Prediction:** Water and ethanol are miscible.

**Reasoning:** Both are polar, and ethanol has an O–H group that can participate in hydrogen bonding with water. Mixing disrupts some water–water and ethanol–ethanol interactions, but it also forms favorable water–ethanol interactions. This supports uniform mixing.
</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample title="Worked example 2: water and hexane">
**Prediction:** Water and hexane are largely immiscible.

**Reasoning:** Water molecules form a network of polar interactions, including hydrogen bonding. Hexane is nonpolar and offers mainly dispersion interactions. Mixing would disrupt water–water interactions without forming equally favorable interactions with hexane, so the mixture separates into layers.
</WorkedExample>

<LessonSection title="4. What dissolving does—and does not—change" description="Distinguish dispersion from chemical reaction">

Dissolving is usually a physical process: particles become dispersed, but their chemical identities remain the same. When NaCl dissolves, the ionic lattice separates into hydrated Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions. When sucrose dissolves, sucrose molecules become surrounded by water molecules while remaining sucrose molecules.

The solution's properties can differ from those of the pure substances because the particles are now mixed and can move or interact differently. But a change in appearance alone does not prove a chemical reaction occurred.

<LessonSection title="5. AP-style practice" description="Use the particle model to explain a solubility observation">

A student adds equal small amounts of sodium chloride and sucrose to separate beakers of water. Both solids dissolve. The sodium chloride solution conducts electricity, but the sucrose solution does not conduct electricity well.

**(a)** Describe the particles present in each solution.

**(b)** Explain why the sodium chloride solution conducts electricity.

**(c)** Explain why both substances can dissolve even though their particles are different.

<LessonSection title="Reveal the reasoning" description="Check the particle identities and interaction logic">

**(a)** The sodium chloride solution contains dispersed Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions surrounded by water molecules. The sucrose solution contains dispersed neutral sucrose molecules among water molecules.

**(b)** The dissolved ions in the NaCl solution are mobile and carry electric charge. Sucrose molecules are neutral, so their movement does not provide the same mobile charge carriers.

**(c)** Water is polar and can form ion–dipole attractions with Na⁺ and Cl⁻. It can also form favorable hydrogen-bonding interactions with the O–H groups on sucrose. The mechanisms differ, but both solutes can form favorable interactions with water.
</LessonSection>
