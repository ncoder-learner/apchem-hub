# State depends on motion and particle interactions

<Unit3Visual kind="phases" />

Solids retain shape and volume; liquids retain volume but take the shape of their container; gases expand to fill the available volume and are highly compressible. These macroscopic differences follow from particle spacing, motion, and the strength of attractions relative to particle kinetic energy.

<LessonSection title="1. Particle-level models" description="Translate a diagram into a physical claim" defaultOpen>

- **Solid:** particles occupy relatively fixed positions and vibrate. In a crystalline solid, the arrangement has long-range order; amorphous solids lack that regular long-range pattern.
- **Liquid:** particles remain close together but can rearrange and flow. Attractive interactions remain important even though particles are not locked into fixed lattice positions.
- **Gas:** particles are far apart relative to their size and move randomly. The sample's volume is largely empty space, which explains why gases compress readily.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="2. Phase changes do not usually break molecules" description="Separate particle identity from arrangement">

Melting, freezing, vaporization, condensation, sublimation, and deposition change the arrangement and energy of particles. For a molecular substance, these changes usually overcome or form intermolecular attractions while leaving the covalent bonds within each molecule intact. Chemical decomposition is a different process.

During a phase change at constant pressure for a pure substance, added energy can increase potential energy by separating particles rather than increasing temperature. Temperature remains approximately constant while the phase transition proceeds.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="3. Use a particle diagram as evidence" description="Look for spacing, order, and movement">

On an exam, identify whether the particles are ordered, close together but disordered, or widely separated. Then explain the observed property. Do not infer that solid particles are motionless or that gas particles have no attractions; both are incorrect oversimplifications.
