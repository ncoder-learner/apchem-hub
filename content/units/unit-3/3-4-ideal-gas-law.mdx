The ideal gas law is more than a formula to plug numbers into. It summarizes how gas pressure, volume, temperature, and amount depend on one another. If you understand what each variable means and which variables are held constant, you can predict how a gas responds before doing any arithmetic.

<LessonSection title="1. What the variables mean" description="Read the model before choosing numbers" defaultOpen>

<Unit3Visual kind="gas-law" />

The ideal gas law is:

**PV = nRT**

- **P** = pressure of the gas
- **V** = volume occupied by the gas
- **n** = amount of gas in moles
- **R** = ideal gas constant
- **T** = absolute temperature in kelvin

The equation links the macroscopic quantities you can measure to the amount and thermal motion of gas particles. Pressure comes from particles colliding with the container walls. Temperature is related to the particles' average translational kinetic energy. Volume describes the space available to the gas, and n describes how many particles are present.

## Units must match the value of R

For pressure in atmospheres and volume in liters, use:

**R = 0.08206 L·atm·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹**

Then use P in atm, V in L, n in mol, and T in K. If pressure is given in kPa or volume in mL, convert it or use a matching gas constant. Do not mix units accidentally.

**Always convert Celsius to kelvin:** T(K) = temperature in °C + 273.15. The equation requires absolute temperature. A temperature of 0°C is not zero thermal energy, so it cannot be entered as T = 0.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="2. Derive the trends instead of memorizing them" description="Hold the other variables constant">

Start with PV = nRT. The key move in any prediction is to identify which variables are fixed.

## Pressure and volume

If n and T remain constant, nRT is constant, so P × V must stay constant. If volume decreases, pressure increases. The same number of particles has less room, so wall collisions occur more frequently per unit area. This is an inverse relationship: doubling V halves P, under the ideal-gas assumptions.

## Pressure and temperature

If n and V remain constant, P/T is constant. Increasing the kelvin temperature increases average particle kinetic energy. Particles hit the walls more forcefully, so pressure increases. P is directly proportional to **kelvin** temperature when n and V are fixed.

## Volume and temperature

If n and P remain constant, V/T is constant. Heating increases particle kinetic energy; the gas expands until the wall-collision pressure matches the external pressure again. V is directly proportional to kelvin temperature when n and P are fixed.

## Amount and volume or pressure

At fixed T and P, volume is directly proportional to the amount of gas: doubling n doubles V. At fixed T and V, pressure is directly proportional to n: adding twice as many gas particles doubles P under the ideal-gas model.

| Conditions held constant | Relationship | If one variable doubles... |
|---|---|---|
| n, T | P and V are inversely related | V doubles → P halves |
| n, V | P is directly proportional to T(K) | T doubles → P doubles |
| n, P | V is directly proportional to T(K) | T doubles → V doubles |
| T, P | V is directly proportional to n | n doubles → V doubles |
| T, V | P is directly proportional to n | n doubles → P doubles |

<Mistake title="Using Celsius for gas-law ratios">
A change from 20°C to 40°C is not a doubling of absolute temperature. In kelvin, the temperatures are about 293 K and 313 K. Proportional gas-law reasoning uses kelvin, not Celsius.
</Mistake>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="3. Solve a single-state problem step by step" description="Choose the equation form from the unknown">

A good calculation follows the same order every time:

1. List the known variables with units.
2. Convert temperature to kelvin and make units consistent with R.
3. Rearrange PV = nRT to isolate the unknown.
4. Substitute values with units.
5. Calculate and check whether the magnitude makes physical sense.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example 1: calculate pressure">
A sample contains 0.500 mol of ideal gas in a 10.0 L container at 300 K. Find the pressure in atm.

**Known:** n = 0.500 mol, V = 10.0 L, T = 300 K, R = 0.08206 L·atm·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹.

Rearrange: P = nRT / V.

Substitute: P = (0.500 mol)(0.08206 L·atm·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹)(300 K) / 10.0 L.

The units cancel to atm, and P ≈ **1.23 atm**.

Check: half a mole at room temperature in 10 L should produce a pressure on the order of 1 atm, so the result is reasonable.
</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample title="Worked example 2: solve for the amount of gas">
A gas occupies 4.00 L at 1.20 atm and 298 K. How many moles are present?

Rearrange PV = nRT to n = PV / RT.

Substitute: n = (1.20 atm)(4.00 L) / [(0.08206 L·atm·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹)(298 K)].

The result is approximately **0.196 mol**. The pressure-volume units cancel with the units in R, leaving moles.
</WorkedExample>

<LessonSection title="4. Compare two states without finding every variable" description="Use the combined gas law when the amount stays constant">

If the amount of gas does not change, PV/T is constant. For two states:

**P₁V₁/T₁ = P₂V₂/T₂**

This form is useful when a gas changes pressure, volume, and temperature but no gas enters or leaves. Temperature must be in kelvin.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example 3: a gas is compressed">
A fixed amount of gas initially occupies 3.00 L at 1.00 atm and 300 K. It is compressed to 1.50 L while the temperature remains 300 K. Find the final pressure.

Since T and n are constant, P₁V₁ = P₂V₂.

P₂ = P₁V₁/V₂ = (1.00 atm)(3.00 L)/(1.50 L) = **2.00 atm**.

The volume is halved, so pressure doubles. This agrees with the particle model: the same particles collide with the container walls more frequently per unit area in the smaller volume.
</WorkedExample>

<LessonSection title="5. What the ideal-gas model assumes" description="Know what the equation represents and when it can fail">

An ideal gas is a simplified model with two central assumptions:

- Gas particles have negligible volume compared with the container volume.
- There are no significant intermolecular attractions or repulsions between particles except during collisions.

Real gases behave most ideally at **high temperature and low pressure**. At high temperature, particle kinetic energy makes attractions less important relative to motion. At low pressure, particles are farther apart, so their own volume and intermolecular attractions matter less.

Real gases deviate more at low temperature, when attractions matter more, and at high pressure, when particles are crowded and their finite volume matters. This is the bridge to Topic 3.6.

<QuickCheck question="Under which conditions is a real gas generally closest to ideal behavior?" options={["Low temperature and high pressure","High temperature and low pressure","Low temperature and low volume only","Near condensation"]} correctIndex={1} explanation="High temperature makes attractions less important relative to kinetic energy, and low pressure keeps particles far apart." />

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="6. AP-style practice: calculate and explain" description="Do the reasoning before revealing the worked response">

A rigid 2.00 L container holds 0.100 mol of ideal gas at 300 K.

**(a)** Calculate the pressure in atm.

**(b)** The temperature is increased to 450 K while the container remains rigid and sealed. Predict the new pressure and explain the relationship.

**(c)** Explain why the pressure increase is not caused by the gas particles becoming larger.

<LessonSection title="Reveal a complete solution" description="Check arithmetic, units, and the particle explanation">

**(a)** P = nRT/V = (0.100)(0.08206)(300)/(2.00) ≈ **1.23 atm**.

**(b)** At fixed n and V, pressure is proportional to kelvin temperature. P₂ = P₁(T₂/T₁) = (1.23 atm)(450/300) ≈ **1.85 atm**.

**(c)** The gas particles do not become larger. Their average kinetic energy increases with temperature, so they collide with the walls more forcefully. Since the container volume and number of particles are fixed, the pressure rises.
</LessonSection>
