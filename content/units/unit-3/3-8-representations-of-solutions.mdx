A particle diagram is a model of what is present in a solution—not a picture of what the solution looks like to the naked eye. AP Chemistry often asks you to translate between a formula, a particle diagram, and an observable property. To do that correctly, first decide what particles the solute becomes when it dissolves.

<LessonSection title="1. Read the particle model before counting" description="Identify the solvent, solute, and chemical species" defaultOpen>

<Unit3Visual kind="solutions" />

A solution contains solute particles distributed among solvent particles. Diagrams often omit the solvent molecules to make the solute easier to count, but that does not mean the solvent is absent.

Use this sequence:

1. **Identify the key.** Determine what each circle, color, or symbol represents. Do not assume a circle represents an atom unless the key says so.
2. **Identify the dissolved species.** Does the solute remain as neutral molecules, or does it separate into ions?
3. **Check the ratio.** For an ionic compound, the number ratio of cations to anions must match the formula and charge balance.
4. **Count particles only after identifying the species.** One dissolved formula unit of NaCl corresponds to one Na⁺ ion and one Cl⁻ ion, not one intact NaCl molecule.
5. **Compare equal volumes if asked about concentration.** More solute particles in the same volume means a higher particle concentration, assuming the particles represent the same species.

<LessonSection title="2. Molecular solutes versus ionic solutes" description="Do not draw the wrong chemical species">

## Molecular solute: particles remain molecules

When sucrose dissolves in water, sucrose molecules disperse among water molecules. They do not normally split into carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen atoms, and they do not dissociate into ions simply because they dissolve.

A particle diagram for sucrose solution should therefore show intact sucrose particles dispersed throughout the solvent, using whatever symbol the key assigns to a sucrose molecule.

## Ionic solute: particles separate into ions

When NaCl dissolves in water, the ionic lattice separates into Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions. Water molecules orient around the ions: the oxygen end is attracted to Na⁺, while the hydrogen ends are attracted to Cl⁻. A particle diagram may omit water to reduce clutter, but it should still show separate ions in the correct ratio.

For calcium chloride, CaCl₂, the formula unit yields one Ca²⁺ ion and two Cl⁻ ions. The correct particle-number ratio is 1:2. Charge balance is a powerful check: +2 from one calcium ion balances −2 from two chloride ions.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example 1: represent dissolved CaCl₂">
Suppose a diagram represents 4 formula units of CaCl₂ fully dissolved in water.

1. Each formula unit produces 1 Ca²⁺ and 2 Cl⁻.
2. Four formula units therefore produce 4 Ca²⁺ ions and 8 Cl⁻ ions.
3. The diagram should show separate ions dispersed through the solution, with an ion count ratio of 4:8, or 1:2.
4. Check charge: 4(+2) + 8(−1) = 0 overall.

A drawing with equal numbers of Ca²⁺ and Cl⁻ would violate the compound's formula and charge balance.
</WorkedExample>

<LessonSection title="3. Compare concentration from particle diagrams" description="Count particles per volume, not total particles alone">

Concentration describes how much solute is present relative to the volume of solution. If two diagrams show the **same volume** and the same kind of solute particle, the diagram with more solute particles represents the more concentrated solution.

But total particle count alone can mislead if the volumes differ. A larger container can contain more particles and still have a lower concentration if its volume increases by a larger factor. Always check the volume scale or the prompt's statement about equal volumes.

For ionic compounds, be clear about what you are counting. One dissolved formula unit of CaCl₂ produces three ions. If the question asks for the number of solute particles after dissociation, count the ions—not the original formula units.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example 2: same volume, different particle count">
Beaker A and Beaker B each contain 100 mL of solution. A contains 10 dissolved neutral molecules; B contains 20 of the same molecules.

B has twice as many solute particles in the same volume, so B has twice the particle concentration of A. This conclusion assumes the diagram uses the same scale and that both beakers contain the same kind of solute.
</WorkedExample>

<LessonSection title="4. What particle diagrams can and cannot tell you" description="Read the evidence without inventing details">

Particle diagrams are simplified models. They can show:
- which species are present;
- whether particles are dispersed or arranged in a separate phase;
- relative numbers and ratios;
- relative concentration when volumes and scale are known;
- whether an ionic compound is represented as dissociated ions.

They do not automatically show:
- exact particle sizes or distances;
- the actual speed of each particle;
- the strength of every intermolecular interaction;
- whether a sample is concentrated unless particle counts can be compared to volume;
- a chemical reaction unless the before-and-after species support that conclusion.

The diagram key and the question's conditions control the interpretation. Do not assume a drawing is to scale unless the prompt says so.

<Mistake title="Counting a dissolved ionic formula unit as one particle">
A solid formula such as CaCl₂ describes the ratio of ions in the compound; it does not mean intact CaCl₂ molecules float through aqueous solution. Once fully dissolved, represent separate Ca²⁺ and Cl⁻ ions in a 1:2 ratio.
</Mistake>

<Mistake title="Comparing total counts when the volumes differ">
If one diagram represents 100 mL and another represents 500 mL, the container with more particles is not necessarily more concentrated. Compare particles per volume, not the total number by itself.
</Mistake>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="5. AP-style practice" description="Translate from a formula to a model and justify your choice">

A student draws two particle diagrams for the same volume of solution.

- **Diagram A:** 6 Na⁺ ions and 6 Cl⁻ ions dispersed throughout the water.
- **Diagram B:** 6 NaCl pairs, each drawn as a joined blue-green pair, dispersed throughout the water.

**(a)** Which diagram better represents fully dissolved NaCl? Explain.

**(b)** A second solution contains fully dissolved MgCl₂. If its diagram shows 4 Mg²⁺ ions, how many Cl⁻ ions should it contain?

**(c)** A student compares equal-volume diagrams of the same molecular solute. One has 18 solute molecules; the other has 9. State the concentration relationship and the assumption needed.

<LessonSection title="Reveal the reasoning" description="Check species, ratios, and conditions">

**(a)** Diagram A is better. Dissolved NaCl is represented by separate hydrated Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions, not intact joined NaCl pairs. The 1:1 ratio is correct.

**(b)** Eight Cl⁻ ions are required. Each Mg²⁺ balances two Cl⁻ ions, so 4 Mg²⁺ requires 8 Cl⁻.

**(c)** The solution with 18 molecules has twice the concentration of the solution with 9 molecules, provided the volumes are equal and both diagrams use the same particle scale and solute identity.
</LessonSection>
