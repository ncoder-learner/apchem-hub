Why is water a liquid at room temperature while methane is a gas? Why can two molecules with similar molar masses have very different boiling points? The useful answer is not simply “one has stronger forces.” You need to identify the particles, determine how their charge distributions interact, and connect those interactions to the energy needed to separate particles.

This is one of the most important reasoning patterns in Unit 3: **particle structure → intermolecular interactions → observable property**.

<LessonSection title="1. First separate two ideas that sound alike" description="What holds a molecule together versus what holds particles near each other" defaultOpen>

An **intramolecular bond** acts within a particle. In a water molecule, each O–H covalent bond holds oxygen and hydrogen atoms together.

An **intermolecular force (IMF)** is an attraction between separate particles. In liquid water, one water molecule attracts neighboring water molecules. These are different interactions at different scales.

<AsyncImage query="Royal Society of Chemistry intermolecular forces water molecules hydrogen bonds diagram covalent bonds between molecules" aspectRatio="16:9" width="100%" />

When molecular water boils, water molecules separate from one another and enter the gas phase. The O–H covalent bonds inside each H₂O molecule remain intact. Boiling water does not normally turn H₂O into hydrogen gas and oxygen gas.

This distinction explains the energy involved:
- Separating molecules against their intermolecular attractions requires energy.
- Breaking a covalent bond requires a chemical change and generally much more energy than an ordinary phase change.
- A temperature change or phase change does not automatically mean that intramolecular bonds broke.

<LessonSection title="2. Why attractions form between particles" description="Use charge distributions to build the model">

Electrons are constantly moving, and their distribution is not always perfectly even. Since opposite charges attract and like charges repel, uneven charge distributions can create attractions between nearby particles. There are several important ways this happens.

## London dispersion forces: temporary dipoles

Even a nonpolar atom or molecule can attract another. At a particular instant, its electron cloud may be unevenly distributed, creating a temporary dipole: one region becomes slightly negative and another slightly positive. That temporary dipole can distort the electron cloud of a nearby particle, inducing a dipole there. The resulting attractions are called **London dispersion forces (LDF)**.

Dispersion forces occur between **all atoms and molecules**. A polar molecule has dispersion forces too; it does not choose between dispersion and dipole–dipole forces.

What makes dispersion stronger? An electron cloud that is easier to distort is more **polarizable**. Larger particles with more electrons are often more polarizable, producing stronger dispersion forces. Shape matters as well: molecules that can make more close contact often experience stronger net dispersion attractions than compact molecules of similar composition.

<Lightbulb title="A useful mental picture">
Think of the electron cloud as flexible rather than rigid. A larger, more easily distorted cloud can create stronger temporary dipoles. The attraction is real even though the dipoles do not remain fixed in one direction.
</Lightbulb>

## Dipole–dipole attractions: permanent dipoles

A polar molecule has an uneven charge distribution that gives it a permanent molecular dipole. The partially positive end of one polar molecule can attract the partially negative end of another. These are **dipole–dipole attractions**.

To decide whether a molecule is polar, do not look only for polar bonds. Determine the three-dimensional shape and whether the bond dipoles cancel. CO₂ has polar C=O bonds, but its linear, symmetric shape makes the two bond dipoles cancel. H₂O is bent, so its O–H bond dipoles do not cancel; water is polar.

## Hydrogen bonding: a particularly strong, specific interaction

Hydrogen bonding is a particularly strong dipole–dipole interaction in the AP Chemistry model. For the usual cases, hydrogen must be covalently bonded to **N, O, or F** in one molecule, and it is attracted to a lone pair on N, O, or F in a neighboring molecule or particle.

There are two roles:
- **Donor:** the molecule has an N–H, O–H, or F–H bond that supplies the hydrogen involved.
- **Acceptor:** a nearby N, O, or F atom has an available lone pair that can interact with that hydrogen.

A molecule with O–H can donate a hydrogen bond, but whether it forms hydrogen bonds with another substance depends on the other particle's structure. For example, ethanol molecules can hydrogen-bond with each other and with water. Hexane lacks the N–H, O–H, or F–H bond and cannot donate a conventional hydrogen bond.

## Ion–dipole attractions: ions and polar molecules

An ion–dipole attraction occurs between a charged ion and a polar molecule. When NaCl dissolves in water, the oxygen end of water's dipole is attracted to Na⁺, while the hydrogen ends are oriented toward Cl⁻. These ion–dipole attractions help stabilize the separated ions in solution.

<LessonSection title="3. Identify the interactions in a substance" description="Use a decision process instead of memorizing one label">

Use this sequence whenever you are given a formula or a particle diagram.

1. **Are ions interacting with polar molecules?** If yes, ion–dipole attractions may be important, as in an aqueous ionic solution.
2. **Are molecular particles present?** If so, dispersion forces are always present between them.
3. **Are the molecules polar?** Use molecular shape and bond dipoles. If they are polar, dipole–dipole attractions also occur.
4. **Can the particles form hydrogen bonds?** Look for an N–H, O–H, or F–H donor and a suitable N, O, or F lone-pair acceptor on a neighboring particle.

This is not a list where you select only one force. A sample of liquid CH₃OH has dispersion, dipole–dipole, and hydrogen-bonding attractions between molecules. A sample of liquid CH₄ has dispersion forces, even though CH₄ is nonpolar.

| Substance | Are its molecules polar? | Important attractions between its molecules |
|---|---|---|
| CH₄ | No | London dispersion |
| CO₂ | No overall; bond dipoles cancel | London dispersion |
| HCl | Yes | London dispersion and dipole–dipole |
| CH₃OH | Yes | London dispersion, dipole–dipole, and hydrogen bonding |
| H₂O | Yes | London dispersion, dipole–dipole, and hydrogen bonding |

<WorkedExample title="Example 1: compare CH₄ and HCl">
CH₄ is nonpolar, so its molecules have dispersion forces but no permanent dipole–dipole attraction. HCl is polar, so HCl molecules experience both dispersion and dipole–dipole attractions. This identifies the kinds of interactions present; predicting the exact boiling-point order still requires comparing the overall strength of the attractions and the molecules' size.
</WorkedExample>

<Mistake title="Treating the forces as mutually exclusive">
A common wrong answer is “water has hydrogen bonding, so it does not have dispersion forces.” Hydrogen bonding is an additional type of attraction, not a replacement for dispersion. When a question asks which forces are present, list all applicable interactions; when it asks which interaction best explains an unusual trend, identify the most relevant difference.
</Mistake>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="4. From intermolecular forces to boiling point and vapor pressure" description="Explain observable trends from the particle model">

To boil a liquid, molecules must escape the liquid into the gas phase. Attractions between molecules oppose that separation. If comparable substances have stronger intermolecular attractions, more energy is generally needed to separate their molecules.

This leads to useful trends:

- Stronger intermolecular attractions generally correspond to a **higher boiling point**.
- At the same temperature, stronger attractions generally correspond to a **lower vapor pressure**, because fewer molecules escape into the vapor.
- Stronger attractions often correspond to a larger **enthalpy of vaporization**, because more energy is needed to separate a given amount of liquid into gas.
- Viscosity can increase with stronger attractions, but molecular shape and size also affect how easily a liquid flows.

These are trends, not universal one-line laws. When comparing different molecules, account for both the type of interaction and factors such as polarizability, size, and shape. A large nonpolar molecule can have stronger dispersion attractions than a small polar molecule.

<WorkedExample title="Example 2: similar molar masses, different boiling points">
Propane (C₃H₈) and ethanal (CH₃CHO) have similar molar masses, but propane is nonpolar while ethanal is polar. Both have dispersion forces; ethanal also has dipole–dipole attractions. Those additional permanent-dipole attractions help explain why ethanal has a higher boiling point than propane. A strong explanation names the interactions and connects them to the extra energy needed to separate molecules.
</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample title="Example 3: why larger nonpolar molecules can boil at higher temperatures">
Compare CH₄ and CCl₄. Both are nonpolar overall, so both have dispersion forces. CCl₄ has a much larger, more polarizable electron cloud than CH₄, so its dispersion attractions are much stronger overall. More energy is needed to separate CCl₄ molecules, giving CCl₄ a much higher boiling point. Polarity alone cannot explain this comparison.
</WorkedExample>

<LessonSection title="5. How AP questions test this topic" description="Build explanations from a comparison, not a slogan">

A common AP task gives two substances and asks you to compare boiling points, vapor pressures, or another physical property. A strong explanation has three parts:

1. **Identify the structural difference.** For example, one molecule is polar while the other is nonpolar, or one has a larger electron cloud.
2. **Name the relevant interaction.** For example, dipole–dipole attractions are present in the polar substance, or greater polarizability strengthens dispersion.
3. **Connect the interaction to the property.** Stronger attractions require more energy for molecules to escape the liquid, so boiling point tends to increase and vapor pressure at a fixed temperature tends to decrease.

Avoid writing only “A has stronger IMFs.” That is the conclusion, not the explanation. Also avoid saying “the covalent bonds in A are stronger” when the question asks about boiling: ordinary boiling separates molecules rather than breaking their internal covalent bonds.

<WorkedExample title="Example 4: a complete comparison">
Substance A is polar and substance B is nonpolar. Their sizes are similar. At the same temperature, A has a lower vapor pressure than B. Explain why this is reasonable.

**Model reasoning:** Because A is polar, its molecules experience dipole–dipole attractions in addition to London dispersion forces. B is nonpolar and has dispersion forces. Given their similar sizes, the additional dipole–dipole attractions in A can make its intermolecular attractions stronger overall. More energy is needed for A molecules to escape the liquid, so fewer enter the vapor at the same temperature and A has the lower vapor pressure.

**What makes this answer complete:** it identifies the molecular difference, names the interactions, and explains how they affect escape into the vapor. It also respects the prompt's condition that the molecules are similar in size.
</WorkedExample>

## Original AP-style practice

A student measures the boiling points of three molecular substances and records the following data.

| Substance | Structure clue | Boiling point |
|---|---|---:|
| P | Nonpolar; relatively small electron cloud | −42 °C |
| Q | Polar; similar molar mass to P | 20 °C |
| R | Contains an O–H bond and can hydrogen-bond with itself | 78 °C |

**(a)** Identify the strongest relevant type of intermolecular attraction for each substance, using the structure clues.

**(b)** Explain why Q has a higher boiling point than P.

**(c)** Explain why R has a higher boiling point than Q, assuming the stated structures are representative of the dominant difference.

**(d)** A student claims that R's covalent bonds must be the strongest because it has the highest boiling point. Explain the error.

<LessonSection title="Reveal the reasoning" description="Check whether each response explains the mechanism">

**(a)** P is nonpolar, so dispersion is its only intermolecular attraction type. Q is polar, so it has dispersion and dipole–dipole attractions. R can form hydrogen bonds with other R molecules, and it also has dispersion and dipole–dipole attractions.

**(b)** Q is polar and has dipole–dipole attractions in addition to dispersion. Given the similar molar masses, these additional attractions help explain its higher boiling point.

**(c)** R can form hydrogen bonds between molecules. These relatively strong, directional attractions increase the energy needed to separate molecules into the gas phase, helping explain the higher boiling point.

**(d)** Boiling separates molecules from one another; it does not ordinarily break the covalent bonds within each molecule. R's higher boiling point reflects its intermolecular attractions, not proof that its intramolecular covalent bonds are stronger.
</LessonSection>
