# Intermolecular forces explain how particles interact

An **intramolecular bond** holds atoms together inside a molecule or network. An **intermolecular force (IMF)** is an attraction between separate particles. This distinction is essential: boiling molecular water separates H₂O molecules; it does not normally break the O–H covalent bonds.

<Unit3Visual kind="forces" />

<LessonSection title="1. The four interactions to recognize" description="Identify the particles and the source of attraction" defaultOpen>

- **London dispersion forces (LDF):** temporary fluctuations in electron distribution create instantaneous dipoles that induce dipoles in nearby particles. They occur between all atoms and molecules. Larger, more polarizable electron clouds usually produce stronger dispersion forces.
- **Dipole–dipole attractions:** permanent partial positive and partial negative ends of polar molecules attract when their orientations allow it.
- **Hydrogen bonding:** a particularly strong dipole–dipole interaction when H is covalently bonded to N, O, or F and is attracted to a lone pair on N, O, or F in a nearby particle. A molecule containing O–H does not automatically hydrogen-bond to every substance; the interacting partners matter.
- **Ion–dipole attractions:** an ion attracts the partial charge at the end of a polar molecule, such as Na⁺ interacting with the oxygen end of water.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="2. Predict relative IMF strength carefully" description="Use structure, not a memorized universal ranking">

For comparable substances, hydrogen bonding often produces stronger attractions than ordinary dipole–dipole interactions, and dispersion can dominate for large, highly polarizable particles. But there is no single ranking that works for every pair of substances. Molecular size, shape, polarizability, and the number and accessibility of interaction sites all matter.

A fair comparison process:
1. Determine whether each particle is ionic or molecular.
2. Identify molecular polarity from bond polarity and shape.
3. Check for hydrogen-bond donors (N–H, O–H, or F–H) and acceptor lone pairs.
4. Compare size and polarizability for dispersion.
5. Link the strongest relevant attractions to the property asked about.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="3. IMF strength and macroscopic properties" description="Boiling point, volatility, and viscosity">

Stronger attractions generally mean more energy is needed to separate particles. For similarly sized molecular substances, stronger IMFs tend to correspond to higher boiling points, higher enthalpies of vaporization, and lower vapor pressures at the same temperature. Viscosity often increases with stronger attractions, though shape and molecular size also affect flow.

<LessonSection title="4. AP-style reasoning" description="Explain the cause, not just the trend">

A strong written response says which particles are present, identifies the relevant attraction, and explains how that attraction changes the energy required for separation. Avoid “the molecules are heavier, so the boiling point is higher” unless you connect mass to the actual mechanism: a larger electron cloud is more polarizable and can have stronger dispersion forces.

</LessonSection>
