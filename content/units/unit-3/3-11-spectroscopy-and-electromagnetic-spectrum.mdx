Spectroscopy is a way to learn about matter by studying how it interacts with light. Atoms and molecules do not absorb or emit every possible energy equally. Their spectra contain patterns that reveal allowed energy changes and can help identify a substance.

<LessonSection title="1. Read the electromagnetic spectrum" description="Wavelength, frequency, and energy are linked" defaultOpen>

<Unit3Visual kind="spectroscopy" />

![NASA diagram of the electromagnetic spectrum with wavelength, frequency, and photon-energy scales. Image credit: NASA / Imagine the Universe.](https://imagine.gsfc.nasa.gov/Images/science/EM_spectrum_compare_level2.jpg)

Electromagnetic radiation travels as a wave and can also be described as photons. Three ideas matter here:

- **Wavelength (λ):** the distance between corresponding points on adjacent waves, such as crest to crest.
- **Frequency (ν):** the number of wave cycles passing a point each second, measured in hertz (Hz).
- **Speed of light (c):** in a vacuum, electromagnetic radiation travels at approximately 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s.

Wavelength and frequency are inversely related:

**c = λν**

Since the speed of light is constant in a vacuum, a shorter wavelength means a higher frequency, and a longer wavelength means a lower frequency.

The electromagnetic spectrum, from lowest frequency / longest wavelength to highest frequency / shortest wavelength, is:

**radio → microwave → infrared → visible → ultraviolet → X-ray → gamma ray**

Visible light is only a small part of the full spectrum. Violet light has a shorter wavelength and higher frequency than red light.

<LessonSection title="2. What a spectrum actually shows" description="Continuous spectra versus line spectra">

A **continuous spectrum** contains a continuous range of wavelengths across a region. A hot, dense object can produce a continuous spectrum. An **emission line spectrum** contains bright lines at specific wavelengths emitted by excited atoms or molecules. An **absorption spectrum** shows dark lines or dips where specific wavelengths have been absorbed from a continuous source.

Why are atomic spectra not usually continuous? Electrons in atoms can occupy only particular allowed energy levels. An electron can move between levels by absorbing or emitting a photon whose energy matches the energy difference between those levels. Because only certain energy differences are allowed, only certain photon energies—and therefore certain wavelengths—are absorbed or emitted.

## Emission

An atom absorbs energy and an electron moves to a higher allowed energy level. When the electron later moves to a lower level, the atom emits a photon. The photon energy equals the difference between the two energy levels.

## Absorption

An electron in a lower energy level absorbs a photon with the correct energy and moves to a higher allowed level. If the photon energy does not match an allowed transition, the atom will not absorb it through that transition.

A line spectrum acts like a pattern of allowed energy differences. Different elements have different energy-level structures, so they produce different sets of spectral lines. Scientists can compare a measured spectrum with reference spectra to identify elements.

<LessonSection title="3. Connect a photon to an energy-level diagram" description="Read the direction of the transition">

Imagine two allowed energy levels, with level 2 above level 1.

- **Absorption:** the electron moves from level 1 to level 2. The atom gains energy by absorbing a photon.
- **Emission:** the electron moves from level 2 to level 1. The atom loses energy by emitting a photon.

The energy difference is the same magnitude for the forward and reverse transitions. If a particular transition requires a photon of energy 3.0 × 10⁻¹⁹ J to excite the electron, the reverse transition emits a photon with that same energy.

A bigger energy gap corresponds to a higher-energy photon, which has a higher frequency and shorter wavelength. A smaller gap corresponds to a lower-energy photon, with lower frequency and longer wavelength.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example 1: compare two emitted photons">
An atom can emit either a photon from a large energy-level drop or a photon from a smaller energy-level drop.

The larger drop releases more energy, so its photon has higher energy and frequency. Because photon energy and frequency are directly related while wavelength is inversely related to frequency, the larger-drop photon has a shorter wavelength.
</WorkedExample>

<LessonSection title="4. Calculate wavelength or frequency" description="Use the wave relationship with consistent units">

The relationship c = λν can be rearranged:

- Frequency: **ν = c / λ**
- Wavelength: **λ = c / ν**

Use wavelength in meters when using c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s. A nanometer is 1 × 10⁻⁹ m, so convert nanometers to meters before calculating frequency.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example 2: calculate the frequency of visible light">
Light has a wavelength of 500 nm.

1. Convert: 500 nm = 500 × 10⁻⁹ m = 5.00 × 10⁻⁷ m.
2. Rearrange: ν = c/λ.
3. Substitute: ν = (3.00 × 10⁸ m/s)/(5.00 × 10⁻⁷ m).
4. Calculate: **ν = 6.00 × 10¹⁴ Hz**.

The meters cancel, leaving s⁻¹, which is equivalent to Hz.
</WorkedExample>

<LessonSection title="5. What spectroscopy can tell you—and what it cannot" description="Use spectral evidence carefully">

A spectrum can reveal which wavelengths are absorbed or emitted. A line pattern can help identify an element because each element has a characteristic set of allowed transitions. The intensity of a spectral line may also depend on how many particles are in the relevant state and on experimental conditions.

Do not confuse a line's position with its intensity:
- **Wavelength / frequency / photon energy** tell you about the energy difference involved in a transition.
- **Intensity** tells you about how strong the measured signal is, and can depend on the number of emitters or absorbers and on the experiment.

A single spectral line is usually not enough to identify an unknown sample uniquely. Matching a pattern of lines against reference data is more informative.

<Mistake title="Assuming electrons absorb any amount of energy">
Electrons in atoms have quantized energy levels. An electron absorbs a photon for a transition only when the photon energy matches an allowed energy difference. Do not say that any photon simply raises an electron to any energy.
</Mistake>

<Mistake title="Confusing wavelength with energy">
Wavelength and photon energy move in opposite directions. Short wavelength means high frequency and high photon energy; long wavelength means low frequency and low photon energy.
</Mistake>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="6. AP-style practice" description="Interpret spectra and energy transitions">

A sample of a gas is excited and produces bright emission lines at several wavelengths. A reference spectrum for element X has lines at the same set of wavelengths, while the reference spectrum for element Y has a different pattern.

**(a)** Explain why element X is the better match.

**(b)** One observed line corresponds to a larger electron energy drop than another line. Compare the two emitted photons' energies and wavelengths.

**(c)** A student claims that an electron emits a photon when it moves from a lower energy level to a higher one. Correct the claim.

<LessonSection title="Reveal the reasoning" description="Use energy levels to support each conclusion">

**(a)** Element X is the better match because its reference spectrum has the same pattern of allowed emission wavelengths as the sample. Different elements have different energy-level structures and characteristic line patterns.

**(b)** The larger energy drop emits the higher-energy photon. It has a higher frequency and a shorter wavelength than the photon from the smaller drop.

**(c)** Moving from a lower to a higher energy level requires the atom to absorb energy, typically by absorbing a photon of the appropriate energy. Emission occurs when an electron moves to a lower energy level and the atom releases the energy difference as a photon.
</LessonSection>

</LessonSection>
