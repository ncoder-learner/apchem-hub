# Light interacts with matter in quantized ways

Light can be described by wavelength (λ), frequency (ν), and photon energy (E). These quantities are related:

\[
c=\lambda\nu \qquad E=h\nu=\frac{hc}{\lambda}
\]

<Unit3Visual kind="spectroscopy" />

<LessonSection title="1. Compare wavelength, frequency, and energy" description="Know which direction each variable changes" defaultOpen>

The speed of light in vacuum is constant. Therefore, longer wavelength means lower frequency, and lower frequency means lower photon energy. Shorter wavelength means higher frequency and higher photon energy. Use SI units when calculating: wavelength in meters, frequency in s⁻¹, and energy in joules per photon.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="2. Absorption and emission spectra" description="Energy gaps determine photon energies">

An atom or molecule absorbs a photon when its energy matches an allowed energy difference. When an excited species emits a photon as it moves to a lower energy state, the photon energy corresponds to that gap. Because allowed energy states are quantized, spectra contain characteristic wavelengths rather than a completely continuous set of lines for isolated atoms.

A larger energy gap corresponds to a higher-energy photon, higher frequency, and shorter wavelength. Read axes carefully: a spectrum plotted against wavelength looks different from one plotted against frequency or energy.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="3. Connect the model to evidence" description="Use peaks and lines to support a claim">

When comparing spectra, identify the peak or line and its axis value before explaining what it means. Do not claim that brighter light necessarily has higher-energy photons: intensity relates to the number of photons delivered per unit time, while frequency determines energy per photon.

</LessonSection>
