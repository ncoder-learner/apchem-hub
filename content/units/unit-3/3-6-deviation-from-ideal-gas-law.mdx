# Ideal gases are a model, not a perfect description

The ideal-gas model assumes gas particles have negligible volume and no intermolecular attractions. Real particles have finite size and attract one another, so measured pressure or volume can differ from ideal predictions.

<LessonSection title="1. Two assumptions and their limits" description="Name the failed assumption" defaultOpen>

- **Particle volume:** at high pressure, particles occupy a meaningful fraction of the container volume. The free space available for motion is less than the container's full volume.
- **Intermolecular attractions:** at lower temperatures, attractions can reduce the force and frequency of wall impacts compared with the ideal model, lowering measured pressure under some conditions.

Deviations become important when particles are crowded (high pressure) or when thermal motion is not large compared with attractions (low temperature). Real gases approach ideal behavior at low pressure and high temperature.

<LessonSection title="2. Explain a model's limitation" description="Connect assumptions to evidence">

When asked to evaluate a model, state the assumption, explain how the real system violates it, and predict the direction of the effect only when the conditions justify that prediction. Avoid claiming that every real gas always has a lower pressure than an ideal gas; finite particle volume and attractions can affect behavior in competing ways.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="3. AP-style response" description="Use conditional reasoning">

A complete response might say: “At high pressure, the finite volume of gas particles is no longer negligible relative to the container volume, so the ideal model's point-particle assumption becomes less accurate.” That is stronger than simply saying “real gases are different,” because it identifies the assumption and the physical cause.

</LessonSection>
