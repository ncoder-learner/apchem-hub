The ideal gas law works well because it simplifies the behavior of gas particles. But real particles have size, and they attract one another. When those facts begin to matter, a real gas no longer behaves exactly as the ideal model predicts.

The useful question is **which ideal-gas assumption is failing, and under what conditions?**

<LessonSection title="1. Two assumptions that real gases violate" description="Finite particle volume and intermolecular attractions" defaultOpen>

<Unit3Visual kind="kinetic" />

The ideal-gas model makes two simplifications:

1. **Particle volume is negligible.** Real atoms and molecules occupy space. At low pressure, particles are far apart and their own volume is small compared with the container. At high pressure, particles are crowded and their finite volume becomes important.
2. **Intermolecular attractions are negligible.** Real particles attract one another. At high temperature, their kinetic energy makes attractions less influential relative to their motion. At low temperature, particles move more slowly and attractions have more time to affect their behavior.

The model is most reliable at **high temperature and low pressure**. It becomes less reliable at low temperature and high pressure, especially near condensation.

At high temperature, intermolecular attractions matter less relative to particle kinetic energy." />

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="2. How attractions affect measured pressure" description="Why particles do not hit the walls exactly as the ideal model predicts">

Imagine a gas particle moving toward a container wall. In the ideal model, other particles do not attract it, so it travels as though no intermolecular forces are acting. In a real gas, neighboring particles can attract the particle toward the interior of the gas. This can reduce the momentum it transfers to the wall compared with the ideal-gas prediction.

At conditions where attractions are important, the measured pressure can therefore be lower than the ideal model predicts for the same amount, volume, and temperature.

This is especially important at lower temperatures, because particles move more slowly and attractions have a greater relative effect. It also becomes important when particles are close enough to interact frequently.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="3. How finite particle volume matters" description="Why crowding matters at high pressure">

Real gas particles are not point-like. They occupy finite space, so the volume available for their motion is smaller than the full container volume. At low pressure, the particles are far apart and this correction is usually small. At high pressure, the particles are crowded, and their own volume becomes a meaningful fraction of the container.

When a gas is compressed, the ideal model treats particles as if their individual volume were negligible no matter how close they are. A real gas cannot be compressed indefinitely in that way. The excluded volume becomes increasingly important, and the ideal prediction becomes inaccurate.

<QuickCheck question="Why does finite particle volume become more important at high pressure?" options={["Particles become chemically larger when pressure rises","Particles are closer together, so the space they themselves occupy is no longer negligible","High pressure removes all intermolecular attractions","The number of moles automatically decreases"]} correctIndex={1} explanation="Compression brings particles closer together, making their finite size a more significant fraction of the container volume." />

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="4. Predict when and why deviation increases" description="Use temperature and pressure together">

| Condition | What becomes more important? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Low pressure | Neither correction is usually large | Particles are far apart |
| High temperature | Attractions matter less relative to motion | Particles have greater average kinetic energy |
| Low temperature | Intermolecular attractions | Particles move more slowly and can be influenced more by attractions |
| High pressure | Finite particle volume; also frequent interactions | Particles are crowded |

Do not memorize “high pressure means deviation” without explaining why. High pressure matters because it crowds the particles and makes their finite volume important. Low temperature matters because it makes intermolecular attractions more consequential.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example 1: choose the more ideal condition">
A sample of gas is compared at 1 atm and 400 K versus 20 atm and 250 K.

The 1 atm, 400 K condition is expected to be more ideal. Its lower pressure means the particles are farther apart, so their finite volume and attractions matter less. Its higher temperature means particle kinetic energy is larger relative to intermolecular attractions. Both conditions favor ideal behavior.
</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample title="Worked example 2: explain a lower-than-predicted pressure">
A real gas is held at fixed amount, volume, and temperature. Its measured pressure is lower than the ideal-gas calculation.

One reasonable explanation is that intermolecular attractions are significant. Attractions between particles can pull them away from the container walls or reduce the momentum they transfer to the walls. Fewer or less forceful wall collisions lower the measured pressure relative to the ideal prediction.
</WorkedExample>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="5. Link the model to condensation" description="Why low temperature and attractions are connected">

A gas condenses when particles come close enough and lose enough kinetic energy for attractions to keep them together as a liquid. Near condensation, the assumptions of negligible attractions and negligible particle volume become poor approximations. That is why gases near their condensation conditions show substantial deviations from ideal behavior.

This also links back to intermolecular forces: gases whose particles attract one another strongly can often be condensed more readily than comparable gases with weaker attractions. Do not infer an exact condensation temperature from the IMF label alone; molecular structure and the conditions matter.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="6. AP-style practice" description="Explain which ideal-gas assumption breaks down">

**Question 1.** A student measures a gas at low temperature and finds that its pressure is lower than the ideal-gas law predicts at the same n, V, and T.

**(a)** Identify the ideal-gas assumption most likely responsible for this result.

**(b)** Explain how the particles' behavior changes the pressure.

**Question 2.** Two samples of the same real gas are measured: sample A at 2 atm and 500 K, sample B at 25 atm and 250 K. Which sample is expected to deviate more from ideal behavior? Explain both relevant conditions.

<LessonSection title="Reveal the reasoning" description="Name the assumption and connect it to the evidence">

**Question 1:** The assumption of negligible intermolecular attractions is failing. At low temperature, particles move more slowly and attractions can influence their motion. Those attractions can reduce momentum transfer to the container walls, lowering pressure compared with the ideal prediction.

**Question 2:** Sample B is expected to deviate more. Its higher pressure crowds particles, making their finite volume and interactions more significant. Its lower temperature makes attractions more influential relative to particle kinetic energy. Both conditions move the gas away from ideal behavior.
</LessonSection>
