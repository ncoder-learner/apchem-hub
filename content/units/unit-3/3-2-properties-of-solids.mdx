A solid's properties are not random facts to memorize. They follow from two questions: **What particles make up the solid, and what holds those particles together?** Once you know the structure, you can explain why a material melts, breaks, bends, or conducts electricity.

<LessonSection title="1. Start with the particles" description="Four common solid structures" defaultOpen>

The phrase “solid” describes a state of matter, not one kind of bonding. A solid can be made of ions, discrete molecules, atoms connected in a continuous covalent network, or metal-ion cores with mobile electrons.

<ChemVisual kind="ionic-lattice" />

- **Ionic solids** contain positive and negative ions arranged in an extended lattice. Do not picture a crystal as separate NaCl molecules; each ion interacts with many neighboring ions.
- **Molecular solids** contain separate molecules. The molecules are held together by intermolecular attractions, although the covalent bonds inside each molecule remain intact.
- **Covalent-network solids** contain atoms connected throughout a continuous network of covalent bonds. Diamond and silicon dioxide are common examples of network structures.
- **Metallic solids** can be modeled as positive metal-ion cores surrounded by delocalized valence electrons.

These structures are different explanations for how particles are held in place. The labels are useful only when you connect them to a property.

<LessonSection title="2. Ionic solids: strong attractions, brittleness, and conductivity" description="Explain each property using the lattice">

In an ionic crystal, oppositely charged ions attract through electrostatic forces. Each ion interacts with several neighbors, so melting the crystal requires enough energy to disrupt the ordered lattice. Many ionic solids therefore have relatively high melting points, although the exact value depends on ion charges, sizes, and lattice structure.

## Why ionic solids are brittle

Imagine two adjacent rows of alternating positive and negative ions. When a force shifts one layer, ions that were previously next to opposite charges can become aligned with like charges. Like charges repel strongly. That repulsion can split the crystal along a plane, so the solid fractures instead of bending easily.

## Why solid salt does not conduct, but molten salt can

In a solid ionic lattice, ions carry charge but are fixed in position. They cannot travel through the solid to carry current. When the ionic solid melts or dissolves, the ions become mobile, so they can move in response to an electric field and conduct electricity.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example 1: explain why an ionic crystal fractures">
**Observation:** A crystal shatters when struck instead of flattening.

**Structure:** The crystal contains alternating positive and negative ions in a repeating lattice.

**Mechanism:** When layers shift, like-charged ions can become adjacent. Their electrostatic repulsion destabilizes the arrangement, causing the crystal to fracture.

A complete answer connects the shift in the lattice to the repulsion. “Ionic solids are brittle because ionic bonds are strong” is incomplete: it does not explain why a strong lattice fractures rather than bends.
</WorkedExample>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="3. Molecular solids: the molecules stay intact" description="Separate intermolecular attractions from covalent bonds">

In a molecular solid, each molecule has its own internal covalent bonds, while intermolecular forces attract neighboring molecules. When the solid melts, molecules gain enough mobility to move past one another; the covalent bonds inside the molecules usually remain intact.

Many molecular solids have lower melting points than ionic or covalent-network solids because melting mainly disrupts intermolecular attractions rather than a continuous network of strong covalent bonds. Do not treat this as an absolute rule: molecular size, shape, packing, and intermolecular forces all affect melting behavior.

Examples:
- Solid CO₂ (dry ice) consists of CO₂ molecules; the molecules do not become carbon and oxygen atoms when it sublimes.
- Ice consists of H₂O molecules connected by intermolecular hydrogen-bonding interactions. The O–H covalent bonds remain intact when ice melts.
- Iodine solid consists of I₂ molecules; stronger dispersion forces in its relatively large electron clouds help hold the molecular solid together.

<QuickCheck question="When ice melts into liquid water, what primarily changes?" options={["The O–H covalent bonds are broken and new hydrogen atoms form","Water molecules gain mobility as the intermolecular arrangement changes","Each H₂O molecule becomes separate H and O atoms","The protons leave the oxygen nuclei"]} correctIndex={1} explanation="Melting changes the arrangement and mobility of intact water molecules. It does not normally break their internal O–H covalent bonds." />

<WorkedExample title="Worked example 2: explain a molecular solid's relatively low melting point">
A molecular solid melts at a much lower temperature than a covalent-network solid.

The molecular solid consists of discrete molecules held together by intermolecular attractions. Melting allows those molecules to move relative to one another by disrupting those attractions. A covalent-network solid has covalent bonds extending throughout the structure, so substantially more energy is often needed to disrupt its network. This structural difference explains the melting-point trend.
</WorkedExample>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="4. Covalent-network solids: bonds extend through the structure" description="Why diamond and quartz behave differently from molecular solids">

In a covalent-network solid, atoms are covalently bonded to neighboring atoms throughout an extended structure. There is no collection of separate molecules that can slide past one another after only weak intermolecular attractions are disrupted.

Because melting or deforming many network solids requires disrupting a large number of covalent bonds, they often have high melting points and are hard. Diamond is a three-dimensional carbon network; silicon dioxide has an extended network of Si–O bonds. Their hardness and high melting points follow from the connected structure.

Not every substance with covalent bonds is a network solid. Methane contains covalent bonds inside each CH₄ molecule but is a molecular substance. Classify the structure of the whole solid, not just the bond type inside one particle.

Deforming or melting it requires disrupting this extended network." />

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="5. Metallic solids: why metals conduct and bend" description="Connect delocalized electrons to properties">

<ChemVisual kind="metallic-bonding" />

A useful model for a metal is a lattice of positive metal-ion cores surrounded by **delocalized valence electrons**. The electrons are not assigned to one pair of atoms; they can move through the solid.

This model explains two important properties:

- **Electrical conductivity:** mobile electrons respond to an electric field and carry charge through the metal.
- **Malleability and ductility:** metal layers can shift while the delocalized electrons continue to attract the positive ion cores. The structure can change shape without forcing a row of like-charged ions into the same brittle arrangement typical of an ionic crystal.

Metals vary widely in melting point and hardness, so do not infer an exact value from the label “metallic.” The model explains characteristic behavior, not every numerical property.

<QuickCheck question="Why can many metals be hammered into sheets without shattering like an ionic crystal?" options={["Metal atoms have no attractions between them","Delocalized electrons continue to attract the positive metal-ion cores as layers shift","All metal atoms are neutralized into gas particles","Metallic solids contain alternating cations and anions"]} correctIndex={1} explanation="The mobile electron sea can continue holding the positive cores together as layers shift, allowing deformation." />

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="6. Crystalline versus amorphous solids" description="Order is another structural feature">

A **crystalline solid** has long-range repeating order. A crystal lattice can be described using a repeating unit cell. An **amorphous solid**, such as ordinary glass, lacks the same long-range periodic arrangement even though its particles still interact and remain close together.

Crystalline order can affect how a material fractures, how it melts, and whether its properties depend on direction. Amorphous materials do not have the same repeating crystal pattern. Do not confuse “amorphous” with “no structure”: the particles are still arranged locally and held together.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="7. Compare structures with a property table" description="Use this as a reasoning map, not a list to memorize">

| Solid type | Particles / structure | Typical property pattern | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ionic | Cations and anions in a lattice | Often high melting point; brittle; conducts when molten or dissolved, not as a solid | Electrostatic attractions; ions are fixed in the solid but mobile in melt/solution |
| Molecular | Discrete molecules | Often lower melting point; usually poor electrical conductor | Melting mainly disrupts intermolecular attractions; particles are not mobile charge carriers |
| Covalent network | Atoms joined through extended covalent bonds | Often hard, high melting point; generally poor conductor, with important exceptions such as graphite | Covalent bonds extend throughout the structure |
| Metallic | Positive metal-ion cores and delocalized electrons | Conductive, malleable, ductile; melting points vary | Mobile electrons carry charge; electron sea maintains attraction as layers shift |

**Important exception:** graphite is a covalent-network solid but can conduct electricity because some electrons are delocalized within its layered structure. This is why “covalent solids never conduct” is too broad. The structure and availability of mobile charge carriers matter.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example 3: identify an unknown solid from observations">
A solid has a high melting point, fractures when struck, does not conduct electricity as a solid, and conducts when molten.

1. High melting point suggests strong attractions in the solid structure.
2. Brittleness is consistent with a lattice that fractures when layers shift.
3. No solid-state conductivity means the charge carriers are not mobile in the solid.
4. Conductivity when molten indicates mobile charged particles become available.

Together, these observations support an **ionic solid** model. Each observation contributes evidence; no single property alone is always enough to identify a material.
</WorkedExample>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="8. AP-style practice" description="Explain the property from the model">

A student tests three solids.

| Observation | Solid A | Solid B | Solid C |
|---|---|---|---|
| Melting point | High | Low | High |
| Conducts as a solid | Yes | No | No |
| Conducts when molten | Yes | No | Yes |
| Response to hammering | Flattens | Crumbles easily | Fractures |

**(a)** Which solid is most consistent with metallic bonding? Explain using one observation.

**(b)** Which solid is most consistent with an ionic solid? Explain why the conductivity changes when it melts.

**(c)** Solid B is made of discrete molecules. Explain why its melting point can be lower than that of a covalent-network solid.

<LessonSection title="Reveal a model response" description="Check whether you linked structure and properties">

**(a)** Solid A is consistent with a metal. Its electrical conductivity as a solid supports the presence of mobile delocalized electrons, and its ability to flatten is consistent with metallic layers shifting while attraction is maintained.

**(b)** Solid C is consistent with an ionic solid. In the solid, ions are fixed in the lattice and cannot carry current through the material. When molten, the ions can move and carry charge.

**(c)** Solid B's discrete molecules are held together by intermolecular attractions. Melting mainly disrupts these attractions so the molecules can move relative to one another. A covalent-network solid has covalent bonds extending through the structure, so disrupting it generally requires more energy.
</LessonSection>
