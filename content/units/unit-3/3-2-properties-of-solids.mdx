# Properties of solids follow from their structure

Solids differ in the identities of their particles and in how those particles are arranged and held together. The structure determines how easily particles move, how much energy is needed to disrupt the solid, and whether charge can move through it.

<Unit3Visual kind="phases" />

<LessonSection title="1. Types of solids" description="Classify by particles and attractions" defaultOpen>

- **Ionic solids** contain cations and anions in an extended lattice. Strong electrostatic attractions often produce high melting points. They are brittle because shifting layers can align like charges, causing repulsion.
- **Molecular solids** contain discrete molecules held together by intermolecular forces. Melting usually separates molecules rather than breaking every covalent bond inside each molecule.
- **Covalent-network solids** contain atoms joined through an extended network of covalent bonds. Breaking this network requires substantial energy, so many are hard and have high melting points.
- **Metallic solids** contain positive metal-ion cores and delocalized electrons. Mobile electrons explain electrical conductivity; layers can shift while the electron sea continues to hold the solid together.

<LessonSection title="2. Crystalline structure" description="Order matters">

In a crystalline solid, particles occupy a repeating, ordered arrangement. A unit cell is a repeating structural unit used to describe a crystal lattice. Amorphous solids, such as many glasses, lack long-range crystalline order. Different arrangements and particle identities can produce different properties even when samples look similar.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="3. Explain the property from evidence" description="Move from model to observation">

A complete explanation links a structural feature to a property: “The solid conducts electricity because delocalized electrons can move through the lattice.” Or: “The ionic crystal is brittle because shifting layers aligns like charges, causing strong repulsion.” Avoid simply naming the solid type without explaining the mechanism.

</LessonSection>
