# Solubility is a balance of interactions

Solubility is the amount of solute that can dissolve in a given amount of solvent under specified conditions. “Like dissolves like” is a useful first clue, not a universal law. Dissolution depends on disrupting existing interactions and forming new ones, alongside entropy changes.

<Unit3Visual kind="solubility" />

<LessonSection title="1. Solute–solvent interactions" description="Compare what is broken and what is formed" defaultOpen>

A polar solvent such as water often dissolves ionic and polar substances well because it can form favorable ion–dipole or dipole interactions. Nonpolar solvents often dissolve nonpolar solutes through dispersion interactions. A strong solute–solvent attraction can favor dissolution, but it must be considered alongside the energy required to separate solute and solvent particles.

<LessonSection title="2. Temperature and pressure" description="Solids and gases behave differently">

For many solid solutes, solubility increases as temperature rises, but this is not universal; the enthalpy of dissolution matters. Gas solubility in a liquid usually decreases as temperature increases because dissolved gas escapes more readily. At fixed temperature, increasing the partial pressure of a gas above a liquid generally increases the gas's solubility (Henry's law behavior).

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="3. Unsaturated, saturated, supersaturated" description="Equilibrium language">

An unsaturated solution can dissolve more solute under the current conditions. A saturated solution is at its equilibrium solubility limit. A supersaturated solution contains more dissolved solute than the stable equilibrium amount and can crystallize when disturbed.

</LessonSection>
