# Translate between scales

AP Chemistry frequently asks students to connect a visible observation, a particle model, and a symbolic representation. A correct solution diagram must preserve the identity, relative number, charge, and distribution of particles.

<Unit3Visual kind="solutions" />

<LessonSection title="1. What a particle diagram must show" description="Count species and respect charge" defaultOpen>

A diagram of a soluble ionic compound should show cations and anions separated and dispersed, with the correct ratio dictated by the formula. A diagram of a molecular solute may show intact molecules dispersed among solvent molecules. Do not draw ions for a molecular solute unless the chemistry indicates ionization.

For an electrolyte, mobile ions allow a solution to conduct electricity. A nonelectrolyte can dissolve as neutral molecules but generally does not produce many mobile ions.

<LessonSection title="2. Link concentration to particle count" description="Same volume, different number of solute particles">

For solutions in the same volume, greater molarity means more moles of solute and therefore more solute entities (or formula units, depending on the solute) per volume. When comparing ionic solutions, distinguish the formula-unit concentration from the concentrations of the individual ions.

**Example:** 0.20 M CaCl₂ corresponds ideally to 0.20 M Ca²⁺ and 0.40 M Cl⁻ after complete dissociation. This follows from the stoichiometric ratio, not from a change in the total amount of salt.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="3. Evidence and explanation" description="Avoid common representation errors">

When comparing two diagrams, state what is held constant (volume, solvent, or solute identity), count particles, and then make a claim about concentration or conductivity. Do not infer that a solution is more concentrated merely because its dots are drawn larger; size may be a graphic convention rather than a quantity.

</LessonSection>
