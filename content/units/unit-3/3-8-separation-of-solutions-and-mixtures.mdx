# Separation methods exploit physical differences

Mixtures can often be separated without changing the chemical identity of their components. Choose the method by identifying the physical property that differs.

<LessonSection title="1. Match method to property" description="What physical difference makes separation possible?" defaultOpen>

- **Filtration:** separates insoluble solid particles from a fluid based on particle size and the filter's pores.
- **Distillation:** separates components by differences in volatility or boiling point. The more volatile component tends to enter the vapor more readily; the vapor is then condensed.
- **Chromatography:** separates substances because they partition differently between a mobile phase and a stationary phase. Stronger attraction to the stationary phase usually means slower movement under the same conditions.
- **Crystallization / evaporation:** can recover a dissolved solid as solvent is removed or conditions change, depending on solubility.

<LessonSection title="2. Interpret a chromatogram" description="Use relative travel, not color alone">

In paper chromatography, the retention factor is commonly defined as:

\[
R_f=\frac{\text{distance traveled by solute}}{\text{distance traveled by solvent front}}
\]

Measure both distances from the origin line. Under the same conditions, a larger Rf indicates that the substance traveled farther relative to the solvent front. Rf values are not universal identifiers; solvent, stationary phase, and experimental conditions matter.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="3. Explain the mechanism" description="Connect the observation to intermolecular interactions">

A complete answer does not merely name the technique. It explains why the components behave differently: one dye spends more time interacting with the stationary phase, another is carried more effectively by the mobile phase, or one liquid vaporizes more readily than another.

</LessonSection>
