A photon is a packet of electromagnetic energy. When an atom or molecule absorbs a photon, its energy increases by exactly the photon's energy. When it emits a photon, its energy decreases by that amount. The photon energy must match the energy change involved in the transition.



<InteractiveLab kind="photon" title="Test the inverse relationship between wavelength and photon energy" />

<LessonSection title="1. The two relationships you need" description="Connect wavelength, frequency, and photon energy" defaultOpen>

<Unit3Visual kind="spectroscopy" />

Two equations connect the properties of a photon:

**c = λν**

- c = speed of light in a vacuum, approximately 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s
- λ = wavelength in meters
- ν = frequency in hertz (Hz)

**E = hν**

- E = energy of one photon in joules (J)
- h = Planck's constant, approximately 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s
- ν = frequency in Hz

Combine the equations by replacing ν with c/λ:

**E = hc / λ**

This combined form makes the inverse relationship obvious: for light traveling at the same speed, a shorter wavelength means a higher frequency and a higher-energy photon. A longer wavelength means a lower frequency and a lower-energy photon.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="2. Absorption and emission are opposite energy changes" description="Track the atom or molecule, not just the light">

An atom or molecule has allowed energy states. If it absorbs a photon, the energy of the species increases by the photon's energy. If it emits a photon, the energy of the species decreases by the photon's energy.

- **Absorption:** photon enters; the atom or molecule gains energy and can move to a higher allowed energy state.
- **Emission:** photon leaves; the atom or molecule loses energy and can move to a lower allowed energy state.

The magnitude of the energy change equals the photon energy. If a transition requires an energy increase of 4.0 × 10⁻¹⁹ J, the absorbed photon must have that energy. The reverse transition emits a photon of the same energy.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example 1: connect a transition to a photon">
An atom absorbs a photon with energy 3.2 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.

The atom's energy increases by 3.2 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. If the atom later returns through the exact reverse transition, it emits a photon with energy 3.2 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. The emitted and absorbed photons have the same energy magnitude because the energy-level difference is the same.
</WorkedExample>

<LessonSection title="3. Calculate photon energy from wavelength" description="Convert first, substitute second">

For wavelength-based problems, use **E = hc/λ**. The wavelength must be in meters, because c is in m/s and h is in J·s.

Useful conversion: **1 nm = 1 × 10⁻⁹ m**.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example 2: energy of a 400 nm photon">
Calculate the energy of one photon with wavelength 400 nm.

**Step 1: Convert wavelength.** 400 nm = 400 × 10⁻⁹ m = 4.00 × 10⁻⁷ m.

**Step 2: Choose the equation.** E = hc/λ.

**Step 3: Substitute.** E = [(6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s)(3.00 × 10⁸ m/s)] / (4.00 × 10⁻⁷ m).

**Step 4: Calculate.** E ≈ **4.97 × 10⁻¹⁹ J per photon**.

The units reduce to joules: (J·s)(m/s)/m = J.
</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample title="Worked example 3: compare two wavelengths without calculating both energies">
Photon A has a wavelength of 500 nm; photon B has a wavelength of 250 nm.

B has half the wavelength of A. Since E = hc/λ, halving wavelength doubles photon energy. Photon B therefore has twice the energy of photon A. This ratio reasoning is faster than calculating both energies separately.
</WorkedExample>

<LessonSection title="4. Use frequency when it is given" description="Keep the units consistent">

If frequency is given, use **E = hν** directly. Since 1 Hz = 1 s⁻¹, multiplying h (J·s) by frequency (s⁻¹) leaves joules.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example 4: calculate energy from frequency">
A photon has frequency 8.0 × 10¹⁴ Hz.

E = hν = (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s)(8.0 × 10¹⁴ s⁻¹).

E ≈ **5.3 × 10⁻¹⁹ J per photon** to two significant figures.

This photon has higher energy than one with a lower frequency. The speed of light does not change from one vacuum wavelength to another; wavelength changes because frequency changes.
</WorkedExample>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="5. Connect photon energy to wavelength and transitions" description="Make predictions without a calculator">

Use these relationships together:

| If... | Then... | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Wavelength decreases | Frequency increases | c = λν and c is constant |
| Frequency increases | Photon energy increases | E = hν |
| Wavelength decreases | Photon energy increases | E = hc/λ |
| A photon is absorbed | Species energy increases by E | Energy enters the atom or molecule |
| A photon is emitted | Species energy decreases by E | Energy leaves the atom or molecule |

The energy gap between allowed states determines which photon can be absorbed or emitted. A larger gap corresponds to a higher-energy photon, which has a higher frequency and shorter wavelength. A smaller gap corresponds to a lower-energy photon, with lower frequency and longer wavelength.

<Mistake title="Mixing up wavelength and energy">
The relationship is inverse: shorter wavelength means higher photon energy. If a student says “red light has more energy because its wavelength is longer,” the relationship has been reversed. Red light has a longer wavelength than violet light, so an individual violet photon has more energy than an individual red photon.
</Mistake>

<Mistake title="Using wavelength as frequency">
E = hν uses frequency, not wavelength. If wavelength is supplied, either calculate ν = c/λ first and then use E = hν, or use the combined equation E = hc/λ. Do not substitute a number in nanometers directly into the frequency variable.
</Mistake>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="6. AP-style practice" description="Calculate and interpret the energy change">

An atom absorbs a photon with a wavelength of 300 nm.

**(a)** Calculate the frequency of the photon.

**(b)** Calculate the energy of one photon.

**(c)** State how the atom's energy changes during absorption.

**(d)** A second photon has a wavelength of 600 nm. Compare its energy with the first photon without repeating the full calculation.

<LessonSection title="Reveal the reasoning" description="Check units, powers of ten, and energy direction">

**(a)** Convert 300 nm to 3.00 × 10⁻⁷ m. Then ν = c/λ = (3.00 × 10⁸)/(3.00 × 10⁻⁷) = **1.00 × 10¹⁵ Hz**.

**(b)** E = hν = (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴)(1.00 × 10¹⁵) = **6.63 × 10⁻¹⁹ J per photon**.

**(c)** The atom's energy increases by 6.63 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.

**(d)** 600 nm is twice the wavelength of 300 nm. Since E = hc/λ, the 600 nm photon has half the energy of the 300 nm photon.
</LessonSection>

</LessonSection>
