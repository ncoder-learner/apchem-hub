Solubility asks how much of a solute can dissolve in a particular solvent under specified conditions. It is not the same as how quickly a substance dissolves. A substance may dissolve slowly but still be soluble; stirring can speed up the process without necessarily changing the equilibrium amount that can dissolve.

<LessonSection title="1. What solubility means" description="Separate solubility from dissolving rate" defaultOpen>

<Unit3Visual kind="solubility" />

**Solubility** is the amount of solute that can dissolve in a given amount of solvent under specified conditions, often at a stated temperature and pressure. A **saturated solution** contains as much dissolved solute as is stable under those conditions; adding more solute does not increase the dissolved amount unless the conditions change. An **unsaturated solution** can dissolve additional solute.

Three ideas are different:
- **Solubility:** the maximum equilibrium amount that dissolves under stated conditions.
- **Dissolving rate:** how quickly the solute dissolves.
- **Concentration:** how much solute is currently present relative to the amount or volume of solution.

Stirring and crushing a solid into smaller pieces often increase the rate because more solute surface contacts the solvent. They do not automatically increase the equilibrium solubility at the same temperature.

<LessonSection title="2. Why one solute dissolves better than another" description="Compare the interactions before and after mixing">

Solubility depends partly on whether favorable interactions form between solute and solvent particles. To dissolve, particles must separate from one another and the solvent must make room for them. New solute–solvent interactions form in the mixture.

- Ionic solutes can dissolve in polar water when ion–dipole interactions help stabilize separated ions, but the attraction holding the ionic solid together also matters.
- Polar molecular solutes may dissolve well in polar solvents when dipole–dipole attractions or hydrogen bonds can form.
- Nonpolar molecular solutes often dissolve better in nonpolar solvents because their interactions are largely dispersion-based.

“Like dissolves like” is a useful first guess, not a guarantee. The actual solubility depends on the full balance of interactions and the change in particle arrangements. Molecular size and structure matter too: a molecule with both polar and nonpolar regions may have limited solubility depending on which region dominates its interactions with the solvent.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example 1: compare two solvents">
A polar dye dissolves much better in water than in hexane.

Water is polar and can form favorable electrostatic interactions with the polar regions of the dye. Hexane is nonpolar and offers mainly dispersion interactions. Water therefore stabilizes the dissolved dye more effectively under the stated conditions. The explanation connects the dye's structure to the types of solute–solvent interactions available.
</WorkedExample>

<QuickCheck question="Why might an ionic compound dissolve in water but not in a nonpolar solvent?" options={["Water can form ion–dipole attractions with the ions, while the nonpolar solvent cannot provide comparable stabilization","Ionic compounds contain no charges","Nonpolar solvents always have higher temperatures","Water converts ions into neutral nuclei"]} correctIndex={0} explanation="Water's permanent dipole can stabilize cations and anions through ion–dipole interactions. A nonpolar solvent generally cannot stabilize separated ions as effectively." />

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="3. Temperature and solid solutes" description="Read the trend from data or a solubility curve">

For many solid solutes, solubility increases as temperature rises, but this is not a universal rule. The direction and size of the change depend on the substance and the energy changes involved in dissolving it. On an AP question, use the provided data or solubility curve rather than assuming every solid becomes more soluble when heated.

A solubility curve usually plots the maximum amount of solute that dissolves in a specified amount of solvent against temperature. Read the axes and units carefully. A point below the curve represents an unsaturated solution; a point on the curve represents saturation. A point above the curve indicates more solute than is normally stable at equilibrium, so some solute may crystallize out.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example 2: use a solubility curve">
A hypothetical salt has a solubility of 20 g per 100 g water at 20°C and 35 g per 100 g water at 60°C.

At 20°C, adding 30 g of salt to 100 g water exceeds the stated solubility by 10 g, so at equilibrium about 20 g can remain dissolved and the excess can remain solid. At 60°C, the stated solubility is 35 g per 100 g water, so 30 g can dissolve under those conditions. The conclusion follows from the data, not from a universal claim about all salts.
</WorkedExample>

<LessonSection title="4. Temperature and pressure affect gases differently" description="Explain why warm drinks lose dissolved gas">

For many gases dissolved in liquids, increasing pressure of the gas above the liquid increases the equilibrium concentration of dissolved gas. More gas particles are available at the liquid surface to enter the solution, and the higher gas pressure favors a greater dissolved amount.

For many gases, increasing temperature decreases solubility in a liquid. As temperature rises, dissolved gas particles have greater kinetic energy and more readily escape into the gas phase. This is why a warm carbonated drink loses dissolved carbon dioxide more readily than a cold one.

Do not apply the usual solid-solute temperature trend to gases. Many solids become more soluble at higher temperature, while gases commonly become less soluble. Use the substance and conditions specified in the question.

<QuickCheck question="A sealed soda is warmed while the CO₂ pressure above the liquid is held comparable. What usually happens to the solubility of CO₂ in the liquid as temperature increases?" options={["It increases for all gases","It generally decreases, so dissolved CO₂ escapes more readily","It becomes independent of temperature","CO₂ becomes an ionic solid"]} correctIndex={1} explanation="Gas solubility in liquids commonly decreases as temperature rises because dissolved gas particles can escape more readily." />

<WorkedExample title="Worked example 3: compare gas solubility">
A scuba diver ascends and the surrounding pressure decreases. Dissolved nitrogen in the diver's blood becomes less soluble at the lower pressure and can come out of solution as bubbles if ascent is too rapid.

The chemistry principle is that lower gas pressure above a liquid generally corresponds to a lower equilibrium concentration of dissolved gas. The biological details are more complex, but the solubility trend follows the pressure relationship.
</WorkedExample>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="5. Read solubility evidence carefully" description="Identify what changed before explaining the result">

For any solubility question, check:

1. What is the solute and what is the solvent?
2. Is the solute solid, liquid, or gas?
3. Which conditions changed: temperature, pressure, or solvent identity?
4. Does the prompt provide a curve, table, or measured concentration?
5. Is it asking about equilibrium solubility or how quickly dissolution occurs?

The same observation can have different explanations depending on what changed. A solid dissolving faster after stirring is a rate effect. More solid dissolving after heating may be a solubility effect. More gas dissolving under increased gas pressure is a pressure effect.

<Mistake title="Confusing stirring with solubility">
Stirring increases mixing and can speed up dissolution. At the same temperature and pressure, it does not generally change the equilibrium amount that can dissolve.
</Mistake>

<Mistake title="Assuming every solute becomes more soluble when heated">
Many solid solutes become more soluble as temperature rises, but not all. Gases commonly become less soluble in warmer liquids. Read the data supplied for the specific substance.
</Mistake>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="6. AP-style practice" description="Use the conditions to justify a solubility prediction">

A student compares two systems.

- **System A:** A solid salt has a solubility of 18 g per 100 g water at 20°C and 31 g per 100 g water at 60°C.
- **System B:** Carbon dioxide is dissolved in water in a sealed container. The temperature increases while the container is adjusted so that the CO₂ pressure above the liquid remains constant.

**(a)** How much of the salt can remain dissolved in 100 g water at 20°C if 25 g is added and equilibrium is reached?

**(b)** Predict whether more or less salt can remain dissolved at 60°C, based on the data.

**(c)** Predict the general effect of warming on CO₂ solubility in System B and explain the particle-level reasoning.

<LessonSection title="Reveal the reasoning" description="Separate data-based conclusions from general trends">

**(a)** About 18 g can remain dissolved at 20°C. The remaining 7 g exceeds the stated solubility and can remain undissolved.

**(b)** More can dissolve at 60°C: the data give 31 g per 100 g water, compared with 18 g at 20°C.

**(c)** CO₂ solubility generally decreases as temperature increases. Dissolved gas particles have greater kinetic energy and escape into the gas phase more readily. The exact amount depends on the conditions and the gas-liquid system.
</LessonSection>
