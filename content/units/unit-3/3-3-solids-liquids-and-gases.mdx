A state of matter is not just a label attached to a substance. It describes how its particles are arranged, how freely they move, and how their motion compares with the attractions between them. A particle model lets you explain why a solid keeps its shape, a liquid flows, and a gas expands to fill a container.

<LessonSection title="1. Read the particle model" description="Spacing, arrangement, and motion" defaultOpen>

<Unit3Visual kind="phases" />

**Solid:** particles are close together in a relatively fixed arrangement. They vibrate around positions but do not freely move past one another. This helps a solid maintain its shape and volume.

**Liquid:** particles remain close together, but their arrangement is not fixed. They can move past neighboring particles, allowing the liquid to flow and take the shape of its container. Liquids usually keep nearly the same volume.

**Gas:** particles are far apart relative to their sizes and move freely in random directions. A gas expands to fill the available container volume and is readily compressed because there is substantial empty space between particles.

The particles themselves do not become larger when a substance melts or boils. The major change is their arrangement, spacing, motion, and the balance between particle kinetic energy and attractions.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="2. What temperature actually tells you" description="Connect temperature to average kinetic energy">

Temperature is related to the average kinetic energy of the particles' random motion. For the same substance, increasing temperature generally means particles have greater average kinetic energy and move more rapidly.

Be careful with the phrase “particles move faster.” It is a useful description for increasing temperature, but it does not mean every particle has exactly the same speed. Particles have a distribution of speeds, and temperature describes an average.

Temperature is not a direct measurement of how strong the intermolecular attractions are. Two substances can be at the same temperature and have very different attractions. Temperature tells you about particle motion; intermolecular forces describe how particles interact.

It does not imply that different substances have the same intermolecular forces or that every particle has the same speed." />

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="3. Phase changes: change the arrangement, not the identity" description="Melting, freezing, vaporization, condensation, and sublimation">

A **phase change** is a physical change in which a substance changes state. In an ordinary phase change, the particles remain the same chemical species. When ice melts, H₂O molecules remain H₂O molecules; when liquid water boils, they enter the vapor phase without normally breaking their O–H covalent bonds.

| Change | Direction | What changes in the particle model |
|---|---|---|
| Melting | Solid → liquid | Particles gain enough freedom to move past neighbors |
| Freezing | Liquid → solid | Particles settle into a more ordered solid arrangement |
| Vaporization | Liquid → gas | Particles separate into the gas phase |
| Condensation | Gas → liquid | Particles become close together in the liquid |
| Sublimation | Solid → gas | Particles enter the gas phase without passing through a liquid |
| Deposition | Gas → solid | Particles form a solid directly from the gas |

## What heating does during a phase change

Before a phase change begins, adding energy often increases average kinetic energy and therefore temperature. During a phase change of a pure substance at constant pressure, added energy is used primarily to rearrange particles against their attractions rather than to increase their average kinetic energy. As a result, the temperature can remain nearly constant while the phase changes.

This distinction is important for interpreting heating curves. A sloped segment indicates a temperature change within one phase; a flat segment indicates that energy is being transferred while a phase change occurs. The energy is not “doing nothing”—it changes the particle arrangement and separation.

<LessonSection title="4. Why a substance has a particular state" description="Balance particle motion against attractions">

Whether a substance is solid, liquid, or gas at a given temperature depends on the balance between particle motion and the attractions among particles, along with pressure.

- If attractions hold particles in a relatively fixed arrangement under the conditions, the substance is solid.
- If particles remain close but can move past each other, the substance is liquid.
- If particles have enough freedom to remain widely separated and fill the container, the substance is gas.

For molecular substances, stronger intermolecular attractions generally make it harder for particles to separate into a gas. That is why, at comparable conditions, stronger attractions tend to correspond to higher boiling points. But state is not determined by attraction strength alone: temperature and pressure matter too.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example 1: explain why water vapor condenses">
Water vapor is cooled and becomes liquid water.

As the temperature decreases, the average kinetic energy of the water molecules decreases. The molecules are less able to remain widely separated, so intermolecular attractions can hold them closer together in the liquid phase. The chemical identity remains H₂O throughout the phase change.

A complete explanation names both the change in particle motion and the role of intermolecular attractions.
</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample title="Worked example 2: compare liquid and gas in a sealed container">
A sealed container holds a liquid and its vapor at the same temperature.

The molecules in both phases are H₂O, but their average separation differs. In the liquid, molecules remain close together and interact frequently. In the vapor, molecules are much farther apart and occupy the available gas volume. The vapor is therefore much more compressible than the liquid.
</WorkedExample>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="5. Common models that mislead students" description="Correct the idea, not just the vocabulary">

<Mistake title="“Particles expand when heated”">
For most AP particle diagrams, draw particles as the same size before and after heating. Heating increases average kinetic energy; it usually increases the spacing or changes the distribution of particles, not the size of the molecules themselves.
</Mistake>

<Mistake title="“Intermolecular forces are broken into atoms during boiling”">
Boiling a molecular substance normally separates whole molecules. If the substance is water, the vapor contains H₂O molecules, not separate hydrogen and oxygen atoms. Breaking O–H covalent bonds would be a chemical change.
</Mistake>

<Mistake title="“Gas particles stop moving when the gas condenses”">
Particles continue moving in the liquid. They have less average kinetic energy after cooling, and their motion is constrained by closer neighbors and intermolecular attractions, but they do not become motionless.
</Mistake>

</LessonSection>

**Question 2.** A heating curve for a pure substance has a flat segment while the sample is changing from liquid to gas.

**(a)** Explain why the temperature remains nearly constant during the flat segment even though energy is added.

**(b)** State what happens to the average separation of particles as the sample vaporizes.

<LessonSection title="Reveal a strong response" description="Look for particle-level cause and effect">

**(a)** The added energy is used primarily to separate molecules against their intermolecular attractions during vaporization, rather than increasing their average kinetic energy. Because temperature tracks average kinetic energy, the temperature remains nearly constant during the phase change.

**(b)** The average separation increases greatly as particles enter the gas phase and become widely separated compared with particles in the liquid.
