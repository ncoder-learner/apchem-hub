# States of matter are particle models

<Unit3Visual kind="phases" />

Solids retain shape and volume, liquids retain volume but take the shape of their container, and gases expand to fill their container. These differences follow from particle arrangement, motion, spacing, and the importance of intermolecular attractions.

<LessonSection title="1. Compare the three states" description="Describe particles precisely" defaultOpen>

- **Solid:** particles are close together and vibrate about relatively fixed positions. Crystalline solids have long-range order; amorphous solids do not.
- **Liquid:** particles remain close together but rearrange, allowing the liquid to flow while retaining a nearly fixed volume.
- **Gas:** particles are far apart relative to their size and move randomly. The large empty spaces between particles explain high compressibility.

<QuickCheck question="Which observation is best explained by the large empty spaces between gas particles?" options={["A gas is readily compressed","A solid has a fixed shape","A liquid has a nearly fixed volume","A crystal has a repeating pattern"]} correctIndex={0} explanation="Compression reduces the space between gas particles; liquids and solids already have particles close together."/>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="2. Phase changes and energy" description="Separate temperature from potential energy">

During melting, vaporization, or sublimation, energy is used to overcome attractive interactions and separate particles. During freezing, condensation, or deposition, energy is released as particles form more favorable arrangements. For a pure substance changing phase at constant pressure, temperature remains approximately constant while the transition proceeds.

For molecular substances, phase changes usually alter intermolecular separation rather than break covalent bonds inside each molecule.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="3. Read particle diagrams" description="Use spacing and arrangement as evidence">

Compare spacing, order, and particle identity, then connect the representation to the observation. A larger drawn particle does not necessarily mean more substance unless the diagram says it is to scale.
