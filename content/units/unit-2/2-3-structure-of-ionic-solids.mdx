# Think in lattices, not molecules

A crystal of sodium chloride is not a pile of independent NaCl molecules. It is a repeating three-dimensional arrangement of cations and anions. Each ion interacts electrostatically with many nearby ions, and the entire lattice is held together by the net attraction among opposite charges.

<LessonSection title="1. Why the lattice forms" description="Use charge and distance to reason about attraction." defaultOpen>

Opposite charges attract and like charges repel. A stable ionic crystal forms when the arrangement of ions lowers the system's overall potential energy. Each cation is surrounded by anions and each anion by cations, in a repeating structure.

Coulombic attraction becomes stronger when:
- the magnitude of the charges increases;
- the distance between the interacting charges decreases.

A useful proportional relationship is:

\[
|F| \propto \frac{|q_1q_2|}{r^2}
\]

This is a trend model for the force between two point charges. A real lattice contains many interacting ions, so use the relationship to compare similar cases rather than treating one ion pair as the entire crystal.

<LessonSection title="2. Lattice energy trends" description="Compare charge first, then ion size." >

**Lattice energy** describes the energy change associated with forming an ionic solid from separated gaseous ions (or the equal-magnitude energy required for the reverse process, depending on the sign convention). Stronger electrostatic attractions generally mean a larger magnitude of lattice energy.

Use this order when comparing ionic compounds:

1. **Compare ion charges.** Higher charge magnitudes usually have the larger effect on attraction.
2. **Compare ion sizes.** Smaller ions can approach more closely, increasing attraction.
3. **State the trend and reason.** Do not just say "it is stronger"; identify charge or separation.

<WorkedExample title="Compare NaCl and MgO">
MgO contains Mg²⁺ and O²⁻, while NaCl contains Na⁺ and Cl⁻. The charge product has a greater magnitude in MgO, so the electrostatic attractions are generally much stronger. The ions' sizes also matter, but the charge difference already predicts that MgO has a much larger lattice-energy magnitude and a much higher melting point than NaCl.
</WorkedExample>

<LessonSection title="3. Explain the properties of ionic solids" description="Connect microscopic structure to bulk observations.">

**High melting points.** A substantial amount of energy is needed to disrupt the network of electrostatic attractions holding the lattice together. Stronger lattice attractions generally correlate with higher melting points, though actual melting behavior depends on the full structure.

**Electrical conductivity.** In a solid ionic crystal, ions occupy fixed positions and cannot travel through the material, so the solid does not conduct well. When molten or dissolved in water, ions can move and carry charge; these forms can conduct electricity.

**Brittleness.** If a force shifts the crystal layers, ions of the same charge can be brought next to each other. Their repulsion destabilizes the lattice and the crystal fractures rather than deforming smoothly.

<LessonSection title="4. Explain, don't just label" description="Build AP-style cause-and-effect explanations.">

A strong explanation links three levels:

1. **Particle model:** identify the ions, charges, and repeating lattice.
2. **Interaction:** explain electrostatic attraction, repulsion, or particle mobility.
3. **Observation:** connect the interaction to melting point, brittleness, or conductivity.

For example: "Solid potassium bromide does not conduct electricity because K⁺ and Br⁻ ions are held in fixed lattice positions. Since the ions cannot move through the solid, they cannot transport charge. When molten, the ions become mobile and can conduct."

<Lightbulb title="Be specific about the charge carriers">
In metals, mobile electrons carry charge. In molten ionic compounds and ionic solutions, mobile ions carry charge. The distinction is frequently more important than simply saying a substance "conducts."

</Lightbulb>

</LessonSection>

## Exam-ready summary

- Ionic solids are extended lattices, not collections of separate molecules.
- Attraction grows with larger charge magnitudes and shorter interionic distances.
- Stronger lattice attractions generally produce higher melting points.
- Solid ionic compounds are poor conductors because their ions are fixed; molten or dissolved ions can move.
- Brittleness follows from like charges being forced next to one another when layers shift.
