A crystal of sodium chloride is not a pile of independent NaCl molecules. It is a repeating three-dimensional arrangement of cations and anions. Each ion interacts electrostatically with multiple neighbors, and the full lattice is stable because the sum of these interactions lowers the system's potential energy.

<LessonSection title="1. Read the lattice model" description="A formula unit describes a ratio, not a separate molecule" defaultOpen>

<ChemVisual kind="ionic-lattice" />

For a textbook lattice illustration with a discussion of the three-dimensional arrangement, see [OpenStax Chemistry 2e: Lattice Structures in Crystalline Solids](https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/10-6-lattice-structures-in-crystalline-solids). Use it to compare how the repeating unit extends through the crystal; the schematic above is not drawn to scale.

In an ionic solid:
- **Cations** are positively charged ions.
- **Anions** are negatively charged ions.
- Opposite charges attract; like charges repel.
- The ions occupy a repeating arrangement called a **crystal lattice**.

The formula NaCl means the simplest whole-number ratio is one Na⁺ for every Cl⁻. It does not mean a crystal contains only one sodium ion and one chloride ion, or that the solid consists of separate NaCl molecules.

In the NaCl structure, each ion has six nearest neighbors of the opposite charge. The lattice extends in three dimensions; a flat diagram is only a slice or projection of that structure.

<LessonSection title="2. Why the lattice is energetically stable" description="The total arrangement matters, not just one ion pair">

Each ion attracts multiple oppositely charged neighbors and repels like-charged neighbors. A stable crystal is the arrangement in which the total electrostatic interactions produce a low potential energy. The attractive contribution is not simply the force between one cation and one anion; the whole lattice matters.

A useful qualitative model is Coulombic attraction:
- Larger charge magnitudes increase attraction.
- Smaller interionic distances increase attraction.
- Repulsions between like charges also matter, but a stable lattice arranges the ions so that the net interactions favor the crystal.

This model explains why ionic solids often require substantial energy to melt. Melting disrupts the ordered lattice and allows ions to move more freely.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="3. Compare lattice energy" description="Compare ion charges first, then ion size">

**Lattice energy** describes the energy change associated with forming an ionic solid from separated gaseous ions. Some sources define the sign for formation; others report the energy required to separate the solid into gaseous ions. Because conventions differ, focus on the **magnitude** when comparing lattice strength unless the question defines the sign.

To compare lattice-energy magnitudes:

1. Compare the charge magnitudes of the ions.
2. If charges are similar, compare ion sizes: smaller ions can approach more closely.
3. State the predicted trend and explain the reason.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example 1: compare NaCl and MgO">
NaCl contains Na⁺ and Cl⁻. MgO contains Mg²⁺ and O²⁻.

The product of the charge magnitudes is 1 × 1 for NaCl and 2 × 2 for MgO. The larger charge magnitudes in MgO produce much stronger electrostatic attraction. Ion size also matters, but the charge difference strongly supports a larger lattice-energy magnitude for MgO.

This is why MgO has a much higher melting point than NaCl: more energy is required to disrupt its strongly attracted ionic lattice.
</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample title="Worked example 2: same charges, different sizes">
Consider two hypothetical ionic solids with the same +1 and −1 charges. In solid A, the ions are smaller and their centers are closer together; in solid B, the ions are larger and farther apart.

With the charge product held constant, the shorter separation in A produces stronger electrostatic attraction. A is therefore expected to have the larger lattice-energy magnitude, all else being comparable.
</WorkedExample>

<LessonSection title="4. Explain ionic-solid properties from the lattice" description="Use the structure to explain melting, brittleness, and conductivity">

## High melting points

A substantial amount of energy is needed to disrupt the many electrostatic attractions throughout the lattice. Stronger lattice attractions generally correspond to higher melting points, although actual melting behavior depends on the full crystal structure.

## Electrical conductivity

In a solid ionic crystal, ions vibrate around fixed lattice positions but cannot travel through the material. Because the charge carriers are not mobile, the solid does not conduct electricity well. When molten or dissolved, ions can move and carry charge, so the liquid or solution can conduct.

## Brittleness

If an external force shifts the crystal layers, ions of the same charge can become adjacent. Their strong repulsion destabilizes the arrangement and the crystal fractures. This explains why an ionic solid tends to break rather than bend smoothly.

<QuickCheck question="Why can molten NaCl conduct electricity while solid NaCl does not?" options={["The ions become neutral when melted","The ions are mobile in the liquid but fixed in the solid lattice","Melting creates new electrons from the sodium nuclei","The solid contains molecules but the liquid contains atoms"]} correctIndex={1} explanation="Charge carriers must be mobile. In solid NaCl the ions are fixed in the lattice; when molten they can move and carry charge." />

<WorkedExample title="Worked example 3: explain brittleness">
**Observation:** An ionic crystal shatters when struck.

**Structure:** The solid contains alternating positive and negative ions in a repeating lattice.

**Mechanism:** When layers shift, like-charged ions can be forced next to one another. Their repulsion destabilizes the crystal, causing it to fracture.

A complete answer explains the role of the shift and like-charge repulsion; saying only “ionic bonds are strong” does not explain why the crystal breaks.
</WorkedExample>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="5. Use data to identify an ionic-solid model" description="Combine more than one observation">

A solid has a high melting point, is brittle, does not conduct electricity as a solid, and conducts when molten. These observations together strongly support an ionic-lattice model:
- High melting point suggests substantial attractions throughout the structure.
- Brittleness is consistent with repulsion when like charges become adjacent after layers shift.
- Lack of solid-state conductivity indicates that the charged particles are not mobile.
- Conductivity when molten indicates that ions can move and carry charge.

Do not infer an entire structure from one property alone. Metals also conduct; some molecular substances have unexpectedly high melting points; and covalent-network solids can be hard. The full evidence matters.

<Mistake title="Treating lattice energy as a property of one ion pair">
Lattice energy reflects the collective interactions in an ionic solid. Coulomb's law for a pair of charges helps predict trends, but the crystal contains many attractive and repulsive interactions.
</Mistake>

<Mistake title="Confusing lattice energy and melting point">
Stronger lattice attractions generally mean a higher melting point because more energy is needed to disrupt the lattice. But lattice energy and melting point are not the same quantity, and the relationship can depend on the full crystal structure.
</Mistake>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="6. AP-style practice" description="Compare structures and justify a prediction">

Two ionic solids are compared.

| Property | Solid A | Solid B |
|---|---|---|
| Ion charges | +1 and −1 | +2 and −2 |
| Relative ion sizes | Larger | Similar to A |
| Melting point | Lower | Higher |

**(a)** Which solid is expected to have the larger lattice-energy magnitude?

**(b)** Explain the trend using Coulombic attraction.

**(c)** Explain why Solid B would not conduct electricity well in the solid state but could conduct when molten.

<LessonSection title="Reveal the reasoning" description="Link charge, separation, and mobility">

**(a)** Solid B is expected to have the larger lattice-energy magnitude.

**(b)** Its ions have larger charge magnitudes, which increase the product of the charges and strengthen electrostatic attraction. Since the ion sizes are similar, charge is the main difference identified by the data.

**(c)** In the solid, the ions are fixed in lattice positions and cannot carry charge through the material. When molten, the ions become mobile and can move in response to an electric field.
</LessonSection>
