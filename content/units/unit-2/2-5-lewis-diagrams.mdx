A Lewis diagram represents valence electrons as bonding pairs and lone pairs. It is a model, not a photograph: it does not show the full three-dimensional electron distribution, but it gives the information needed to reason about connectivity, formal charge, resonance, and VSEPR geometry.

<LessonSection title="1. Use the same drawing algorithm every time" description="Electron bookkeeping prevents most Lewis-structure errors" defaultOpen>

<ChemVisual kind="lewis" />

![Step-by-step Lewis structure construction for CO₂, showing the valence-electron count and conversion of C–O single bonds to double bonds. Source: LivePhysics, Drawing Lewis Structures Step by Step.](https://cdn.livephysics.com/images/infographics/og-chemistry-drawing-lewis-structures-step-by-step.png)

Use this sequence for a typical covalent molecule or polyatomic ion:

1. **Count total valence electrons.** Add the valence electrons of each atom. For a negative charge, add electrons; for a positive charge, subtract electrons.
2. **Choose the skeleton.** Hydrogen is terminal. The least electronegative atom (never H) is often central, but use known connectivity when provided.
3. **Connect atoms with single bonds.** Each single bond uses two electrons.
4. **Complete terminal octets.** Place lone pairs on outer atoms first; hydrogen needs only two electrons.
5. **Place remaining electrons on the central atom.**
6. **If the central atom lacks an octet, consider multiple bonds** by moving a lone pair from a neighboring atom into a shared bond.
7. **Check the electron count and formal charges.** For ions, put brackets around the structure and show the overall charge.

Every line bond represents two electrons; every lone-pair dot represents one. The final structure must use exactly the total number of electrons you counted at the start.

<LessonSection title="2. Worked example: CO₂" description="Count first, then use multiple bonds to complete the octets">

**Step 1 — Count.** Carbon contributes 4 valence electrons and each oxygen contributes 6, for a total of 16.

**Step 2 — Skeleton.** O–C–O uses two single bonds, consuming 4 electrons. There are 12 electrons left.

**Step 3 — Complete oxygen octets.** Put three lone pairs on each oxygen, using the remaining 12 electrons. Carbon has only four electrons around it from its two single bonds, so it does not have an octet.

**Step 4 — Make double bonds.** Move one lone pair from each oxygen into a bonding pair. The structure becomes O=C=O, with two lone pairs on each oxygen.

**Step 5 — Check.** Total electrons remain 16. Each atom has an octet, and the formal charges are zero in this structure.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="3. Work through ions and charges" description="Adjust the count before drawing the bonds">

The overall charge changes the valence-electron count before you start drawing:
- For a 1− ion, add one electron.
- For a 2− ion, add two electrons.
- For a 1+ ion, subtract one electron.
- For a 2+ ion, subtract two electrons.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example 1: ammonium, NH₄⁺">
Nitrogen contributes 5 valence electrons. Four hydrogens contribute 4. The +1 charge means subtract one electron.

Total = 5 + 4 − 1 = **8 valence electrons**.

The structure has four N–H single bonds, using all eight electrons. Nitrogen has four bonds and no lone pair, and the ion is written in brackets with an overall + charge: [NH₄]⁺.
</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample title="Worked example 2: hydroxide, OH⁻">
Oxygen contributes 6 valence electrons and hydrogen contributes 1. The −1 charge means add one electron: total = 6 + 1 + 1 = **8 valence electrons**.

One O–H single bond uses 2 electrons. The remaining 6 electrons are three lone pairs on oxygen. Put brackets around the structure and show the − charge.
</WorkedExample>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="4. Octet exceptions and reasonable structures" description="The octet rule is a guide, not an exception-free law">

- **Hydrogen** follows a duet rule: two electrons fill its first shell.
- **Electron-deficient centers:** boron compounds such as BF₃ are often represented with only six electrons around boron.
- **Odd-electron species:** radicals have an odd total number of valence electrons, so not every atom can have a paired octet.
- **Expanded-valence representations:** some period 3 and heavier central atoms are drawn with more than eight electrons in introductory Lewis structures. Treat these as the course's representation model, not proof that d orbitals must be used to form every bond.

When several structures are possible, use electron counting, formal charge, and the species' known chemistry. Do not choose whichever drawing simply looks symmetrical.

<Mistake title="Adding electrons to a positive ion">
A positive ion has fewer electrons than the neutral atoms. For NH₄⁺, add the neutral-atom valence counts and then subtract one for the + charge. For a negative ion, add electrons instead.
</Mistake>

<Mistake title="Stopping as soon as every terminal atom has an octet">
After completing the terminal atoms, the central atom may still lack an octet. If electrons remain available, consider forming multiple bonds and then recalculate the electron count and formal charges.
</Mistake>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="5. Check the drawing before moving on" description="A final checklist catches errors early">

Before trusting a Lewis structure, ask:

- Does the drawing use the exact total valence-electron count?
- Does every line count as two electrons?
- Does H have one bond and a duet?
- Do typical second-period atoms have reasonable octets?
- Are the formal charges plausible and consistent with the overall charge?
- Does the structure agree with any information in the prompt, such as connectivity or known bond lengths?

Lewis structures are the input to later topics: resonance and formal charge help compare valid electron arrangements, while VSEPR uses the bonds and lone pairs to predict 3D shape.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="6. AP-style practice" description="Build a Lewis structure and justify the electron count">

A student is asked to draw the Lewis structure of the cyanide ion, CN⁻.

**(a)** Calculate the total number of valence electrons. Carbon and nitrogen each contribute 4 and 5, respectively.

**(b)** Draw or describe a structure that gives both atoms an octet.

**(c)** Explain why the negative charge must be included in the electron count.

<LessonSection title="Reveal the reasoning" description="Use the total electron count and octet check">

**(a)** Total valence electrons = 4 + 5 + 1 = **10**.

**(b)** A valid Lewis representation has a C≡N triple bond and one lone pair on each atom, enclosed in brackets with an overall − charge. The triple bond uses six electrons and the two lone pairs use four, for ten total. Both atoms have an octet.

**(c)** The negative charge means the ion has one extra electron compared with neutral C and N atoms. Leaving it out would give only nine electrons and an inconsistent structure.
</LessonSection>
