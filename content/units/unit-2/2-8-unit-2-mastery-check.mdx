# Unit 2 Mastery Check

This is a mixed diagnostic, not a promise of a particular classroom score. Work without notes first. For each explanation, identify the evidence and connect it to the particle model. Questions are original practice, not released College Board questions.

<LessonSection title="1. Bonding models and bulk properties" description="Use observable evidence to choose a model" defaultOpen>

A solid conducts electricity, can be hammered into a thin sheet, and conducts heat well. These observations are consistent with a metallic solid, in which delocalized valence electrons move through a lattice of positive metal-ion cores.

<QuickCheck question="Why does solid NaCl not conduct electricity well, while molten NaCl does?" options={["Solid NaCl has no charged particles","Ions are fixed in the solid lattice but can move in the molten liquid","Electrons become covalent only when heated","Melting destroys the charges on the ions"]} correctIndex={1} explanation="The solid contains ions, but they cannot move freely through the lattice. In the melt, mobile ions carry charge."/>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="2. Potential energy and bond strength" description="Interpret the shape of a curve">

A potential-energy curve reaches a minimum at the equilibrium bond distance. A deeper well indicates that more energy is required to separate the atoms from that equilibrium state. Do not confuse bond length (the x-position of the minimum) with bond energy (the depth of the well).

<QuickCheck question="Two comparable bonds have equilibrium distances of 100 pm and 150 pm. The 100 pm bond also has the deeper potential-energy well. Which conclusion is supported?" options={["The 150 pm bond is shorter and stronger","The 100 pm bond is shorter and requires more energy to dissociate","The bond with the longer distance always has greater bond energy","The curve minimum represents zero nuclear attraction"]} correctIndex={1} explanation="The shorter equilibrium distance and deeper well indicate a shorter, stronger bond in this comparison."/>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="3. Lewis structures and resonance" description="Conserve electrons and compare formal charges">

Formal charge = valence electrons − nonbonding electrons − one-half of bonding electrons. Count the total valence electrons before drawing; add electrons for negative charge and subtract for positive charge. Resonance structures preserve atom connectivity and differ only in electron placement. The real structure is a delocalized hybrid.

<QuickCheck question="Which change creates a resonance contributor rather than a different molecule?" options={["Move an atom to a different position in the skeleton","Move a lone pair or π electrons while keeping atom connectivity fixed","Change the total number of valence electrons","Remove a terminal atom"]} correctIndex={1} explanation="Resonance moves electrons, not nuclei or atom connectivity. All contributors must preserve the same total electron count."/>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="4. Shape, bond polarity, and molecular polarity" description="Separate three different decisions">

First count electron groups around the central atom; a single, double, or triple bond each counts as one group. Then name electron-group geometry, molecular geometry (positions of atoms only), and finally molecular polarity by considering whether bond dipoles cancel in the actual shape.

<QuickCheck question="CO₂ contains polar C=O bonds but is nonpolar overall. Why?" options={["Its bonds are actually nonpolar","It is linear and the two equal bond dipoles point in opposite directions","It contains no electrons in its bonds","Its oxygen atoms have no electronegativity"]} correctIndex={1} explanation="Molecular polarity is the vector sum of bond dipoles. The linear, symmetric molecule has opposing dipoles that cancel."/>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="5. AP-style explanation checklist" description="What a complete response needs">

When asked to compare two substances, avoid one-word claims such as “ionic,” “polar,” or “strong.” State the structural evidence and connect it to the property:

- Identify the bonding or molecular structure.
- Name the relevant particle-level interaction or electron behavior.
- Explain how it produces the observed property.
- Distinguish intramolecular bonds from intermolecular attractions.

For a VSEPR question, show the Lewis structure or electron-group count. For a formal-charge question, show the electron count. For a property comparison, state the specific structural difference. These steps make reasoning visible and reduce avoidable lost points.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="6. Compare data and representations" description="Support a claim with structural evidence">

Two substances have the following properties:

| Property | Substance A | Substance B |
|---|---|---|
| Electrical conductivity as a solid | High | Very low |
| Malleability | High | Brittle |
| Conductivity when molten | High | High |

<QuickCheck question="Which explanation best accounts for Substance A's properties?" options={["It is a metallic solid with mobile delocalized electrons","It is a molecular solid with only dispersion forces","It is a covalent network whose electrons are all localized","It is an ionic solid with fixed ions in the solid"]} correctIndex={0} explanation="High conductivity as a solid and malleability are characteristic of metallic bonding and mobile electrons."/>

**Short free-response prompt:** Draw or describe a Lewis structure for a course-appropriate molecule, identify its electron-group geometry and molecular geometry, and determine whether its bond dipoles cancel. Earn full credit by showing the valence-electron count, lone pairs, electron groups, and a shape-based polarity explanation.

A second prompt may provide two potential-energy curves. Identify which bond is shorter from the location of each minimum, and which requires more energy to dissociate from the depth of the well. State the evidence from the graph rather than relying on the word “stronger.”

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="7. Readiness gate" description="Check transfer, not memorization">

You should be able to draw unfamiliar but course-appropriate Lewis structures, explain a property from a particle model, interpret a potential-energy curve, and reason from a 3D shape to polarity without a worked example in front of you. If not, return to the specific topic and then retry with a fresh question. This page is a sample diagnostic, not a guarantee of a 100% on any teacher's test.

</LessonSection>
