A metal is not best described as a collection of neutral atoms connected by fixed two-atom bonds. Its valence electrons are delocalized across the solid. The positive metal-ion cores are attracted to this shared electron density, producing metallic cohesion.

<ChemVisual kind="metallic-bonding" />

For a second diagram-based explanation of why different-sized atoms disrupt the layers in an alloy, see [Maths Genie: Properties of Metals and Alloys](https://mathsgenie.co.uk/gcse/chemistry/aqa/properties-of-metals-and-alloys/revision-guides). This is GCSE-level supplementary material; use the particle model and AP-style reasoning in this lesson for exam preparation.

In the simplest model, metal atoms contribute valence electrons to a shared, mobile electron cloud. The positive metal-ion cores remain arranged in a lattice, while the delocalized electrons are not confined to one particular bond.

This model explains:
- **Electrical conductivity:** mobile electrons respond to an applied electric field and carry charge through the solid.
- **Thermal conductivity:** mobile electrons help transfer energy through the material.
- **Metallic cohesion:** attraction between positive ion cores and delocalized electrons holds the structure together.

The electron-sea model is a simplified picture; real metals have band structures and quantum-mechanical behavior. For AP Chemistry, the key idea is that the valence electrons are delocalized and mobile.

<LessonSection title="2. Why pure metals bend instead of shatter" description="Malleability and ductility follow from nonlocalized bonding">

**Malleability** is the ability to be hammered or rolled into sheets. **Ductility** is the ability to be drawn into wire.

In a pure metal, atoms are similar in size and form regular layers. When a force is applied, layers can shift relative to one another while the delocalized electrons continue to attract the positive ion cores. The structure can deform without the metallic attraction being lost.

Compare this with an ionic crystal. Shifting ionic layers can align like charges, creating strong repulsion and causing the crystal to fracture. This difference in how the structure responds to shifting explains why many metals bend while ionic crystals are brittle.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example 1: explain why copper can be hammered into a sheet">
Copper is a metallic solid with positive ion cores and delocalized electrons. When force shifts a layer of ion cores, the electron cloud remains mobile and continues to attract the positive cores. The structure can change shape while remaining cohesive, so copper is malleable.

A complete explanation identifies the delocalized electrons and explains why the attraction continues during layer movement.
</WorkedExample>

<LessonSection title="3. Alloys: disrupt the regular layers" description="Why adding another element often makes a metal harder">

An **alloy** is a metallic material containing more than one element, at least one of which is a metal. Two common structural models are:

- **Substitutional alloy:** some host metal atoms are replaced by atoms of another element with a similar size.
- **Interstitial alloy:** smaller atoms fit into spaces between host metal atoms.

When atoms of different sizes occupy the structure, the regular layers become distorted. The layers cannot slide past one another as easily, so a greater force is often required to deform the material. This commonly makes an alloy harder or stronger than the pure metal.

Steel is based primarily on iron with carbon and sometimes other elements. Brass contains copper and zinc. The exact properties depend on composition and processing; an alloy is not automatically harder in every possible case.

<QuickCheck question="Why can adding a second element make a metal harder?" options={["The added atoms can distort the lattice and hinder layers from sliding","The added elements remove every valence electron","Alloys contain no metallic bonding","The second element always converts the metal into an ionic crystal"]} correctIndex={0} explanation="Atoms of different sizes can disrupt the regular lattice and impede the movement that allows a metal to deform." />

<WorkedExample title="Worked example 2: compare pure copper and brass">
Pure copper contains one type of metal atom, so its layers can slide relatively easily. Brass contains copper and zinc atoms of different sizes. Their presence distorts the regular arrangement and makes layer movement more difficult. Brass is therefore generally harder than pure copper, although exact hardness depends on the alloy composition and processing.
</WorkedExample>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="4. Predict material behavior from structure" description="Connect observations to the model">

| Observation | Structural explanation |
|---|---|
| A metal conducts electricity | Delocalized electrons move through the solid and carry charge |
| A metal transfers heat efficiently | Mobile electrons transfer energy through the material |
| A pure metal can be rolled into sheets | Similar-sized layers can shift while the electron cloud maintains attraction |
| An alloy is harder than the pure metal | Differently sized atoms distort the lattice and impede layer movement |
| An ionic crystal fractures when layers shift | Like charges can become adjacent and repel |

Use the full evidence rather than treating a single property as a perfect identifier. Different metals and alloys vary in melting point, hardness, and strength, but the electron-sea model explains the characteristic behaviors.

<Mistake title="Saying metal ions carry the current">
The positive metal-ion cores remain in the solid lattice. The delocalized electrons are the mobile charge carriers. In a molten ionic compound, by contrast, the mobile ions carry charge.
</Mistake>

<Mistake title="Assuming every alloy is harder than every pure metal">
Different-sized atoms often hinder layer movement, but alloy properties depend on composition and processing. Use a specific comparison or data when the question provides them.
</Mistake>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="5. AP-style practice" description="Explain conductivity and strength from the model">

A student compares pure copper with a copper-zinc alloy. Both conduct electricity, but the alloy is harder to deform.

**(a)** Explain why both materials conduct electricity.

**(b)** Explain why the alloy is harder to deform than pure copper.

**(c)** A student claims that the alloy is harder because its atoms are connected by stronger covalent bonds. Explain why this is not the best model.

<LessonSection title="Reveal the reasoning" description="Use delocalized electrons and lattice distortion">

**(a)** Both materials have metallic bonding with delocalized electrons that can move through the solid and carry charge.

**(b)** Zinc atoms differ in size from copper atoms and distort the regular arrangement. This makes layers more difficult to slide past one another, so more force is needed to deform the alloy.

**(c)** The metallic model describes positive ion cores held together by delocalized electrons, not fixed covalent bonds between specific atom pairs. The alloy's greater hardness is explained by lattice distortion that hinders layer movement.
</LessonSection>

</LessonSection>
