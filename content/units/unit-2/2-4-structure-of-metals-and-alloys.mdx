# Why metals bend instead of shatter

A metal is not best described as a collection of neutral atoms connected by fixed two-atom bonds. Its valence electrons are delocalized across the solid. The positive metal-ion cores are attracted to this shared electron density, producing metallic cohesion.


<ChemVisual kind="metallic-bonding" />

<p><strong>Why the model predicts these properties:</strong> because metallic bonding is not restricted to one fixed pair of atoms, layers of metal-ion cores can shift while remaining attracted to the mobile electron cloud. This helps explain malleability and ductility. Alloy atoms of different sizes disrupt the regular layer arrangement, making it harder for layers to slide and often increasing hardness.</p>

<LessonSection title="1. The metallic bonding model" description="Picture positive ion cores and mobile electron density." defaultOpen>

In the simplest model, metal atoms contribute valence electrons to a shared, mobile electron cloud. The positive ion cores remain arranged in a lattice, while the delocalized electrons are not confined to one particular bond.

This model explains two important properties:

- **Electrical conductivity:** mobile electrons respond to an applied electric field and carry charge through the solid.
- **Thermal conductivity:** mobile electrons and lattice vibrations help transfer energy through the material.

The model is simplified; real metals have band structures and quantum-mechanical behavior. For AP Chemistry, the key idea is the mobility of delocalized valence electrons.

<LessonSection title="2. Malleability and ductility" description="Explain why layers can shift without the solid falling apart.">

**Malleability** is the ability to be hammered or rolled into sheets. **Ductility** is the ability to be drawn into wire.

In a metal, layers of ion cores can shift relative to one another while the delocalized electrons continue to attract the positive cores. The metallic structure can therefore deform without requiring every bond to be broken and remade in the way a rigid network might.

Compare this with an ionic crystal. Shifting its layers can align like charges, causing strong repulsion and fracture. This is why many ionic solids are brittle while pure metals are malleable.

<LessonSection title="3. Alloys: tune the structure" description="Why combining elements can make a material harder or more useful.">

An **alloy** is a metallic material containing more than one element, at least one of which is a metal. Common models include:

- **Substitutional alloy:** some host metal atoms are replaced by atoms of another element with similar size.
- **Interstitial alloy:** smaller atoms fit into spaces between host metal atoms.

Different-sized atoms distort the regular arrangement of the metal lattice. This can make it harder for layers or defects to move, often increasing hardness or strength while reducing malleability compared with the pure metal.

Steel, an alloy based primarily on iron with carbon and sometimes other elements, is a familiar example. The exact properties depend on composition and processing; "alloy" does not automatically mean harder in every possible case.

## Exam-ready summary

- Metallic bonding involves attraction between positive metal-ion cores and delocalized electrons.
- Mobile electrons explain electrical and thermal conductivity.
- Continued metallic cohesion during layer movement helps explain malleability and ductility.
- Alloys alter the regular lattice; size differences can make layer movement more difficult.
- Use the structure to explain the property rather than merely listing the property.
