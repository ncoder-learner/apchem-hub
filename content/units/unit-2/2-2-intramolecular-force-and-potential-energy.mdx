A bond is not a rigid line connecting two motionless atoms. Two atoms interact through attractions between nuclei and electrons and repulsions between like charges. Their potential energy changes as the distance between their nuclei changes. The stable bond length corresponds to the separation where the combined interactions give a minimum in potential energy.

<LessonSection title="1. Read a potential-energy curve" description="Locate the equilibrium distance and the energy well" defaultOpen>

<ChemVisual kind="bond-energy" />

A typical potential-energy curve plots **potential energy** on the vertical axis against **internuclear distance** on the horizontal axis.

- **Far apart:** the interaction approaches zero by convention because the atoms barely interact.
- **Moving closer from far away:** attractive interactions lower the potential energy.
- **At the minimum:** the atoms are at the equilibrium bond distance. The slope is zero, so the net force is zero.
- **Too close:** repulsions increase sharply, so potential energy rises steeply.

The minimum is not “zero energy.” It is the lowest potential energy on the curve relative to the chosen reference. The **equilibrium bond length** is the distance at that minimum.

<QuickCheck question="On a potential-energy-versus-distance graph, where is the equilibrium bond distance?" options={["Where the curve is highest","Where the curve crosses zero energy","At the minimum of the potential-energy curve","At an arbitrarily large distance"]} correctIndex={2} explanation="At the minimum, potential energy is lowest and the slope is zero, so the net force is zero. That distance is the equilibrium bond length." />

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="2. The depth of the energy well measures bond strength" description="How much energy is required to separate bonded atoms?">

The vertical difference between the minimum and the energy of the separated atoms is related to the **bond dissociation energy** for that specified bond and process. A deeper well means more energy is required to separate the atoms, so the bond is generally stronger.

Bond dissociation energy is not the same as bond length. The horizontal location of the minimum tells you the equilibrium distance; the vertical depth tells you the energy scale required to separate the atoms.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example 1: compare two potential-energy curves">
Curve A has a minimum 400 kJ/mol below the separated-atom reference level. Curve B has a minimum 250 kJ/mol below that reference.

Curve A has the deeper energy well, so more energy is required to separate its bonded atoms. The bond represented by A is stronger by this measure. This conclusion follows from the vertical depth, not from which curve's minimum is farther left or right.
</WorkedExample>

<LessonSection title="3. Bond order, length, and strength" description="Use trends for the same pair of atoms">

For bonds between the same pair of elements, greater bond order generally means more shared electron density between the nuclei. This usually leads to a shorter, stronger bond.

| Bond between the same atoms | General trend |
|---|---|
| Single | Longest and weakest |
| Double | Shorter and stronger than a single bond |
| Triple | Shortest and strongest |

This is a trend, not a universal shortcut for comparing unrelated bonds. Atom size and the bonding environment also affect length and strength. If a question gives measured bond energies or lengths, use those data.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example 2: compare C–C, C=C, and C≡C">
For carbon–carbon bonds, the triple bond has greater bond order than the double bond, which has greater bond order than the single bond. Predict the triple bond to be shortest and strongest, the single bond longest and weakest, and the double bond intermediate.

The mechanism is that greater shared electron density strengthens the attraction between the nuclei and the bonding electrons, generally pulling the nuclei closer together.
</WorkedExample>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="4. Bond breaking and bond formation" description="Track the energy of the atoms before and after the process">

**Breaking a bond requires energy.** The bonded atoms begin in a lower-potential-energy state. Separating them requires energy input to move them out of the attractive energy well.

**Forming a bond releases energy.** As separated atoms form a stable bond, the system moves toward lower potential energy. The energy difference is released to the surroundings.

This distinction matters for chemical reactions:
- Energy is absorbed to break reactant bonds.
- Energy is released when product bonds form.
- The overall reaction energy depends on the balance of all bonds broken and formed.

A reaction can release energy overall even though bond breaking itself requires energy, if forming the product bonds releases more energy than was absorbed in breaking the reactant bonds.

<QuickCheck question="What happens energetically when an isolated covalent bond is broken?" options={["Energy is released because atoms become separate","Energy must be supplied to separate the bonded atoms","Potential energy must decrease","Nothing changes because atoms are conserved"]} correctIndex={1} explanation="Moving bonded atoms out of the attractive potential-energy well requires energy input." />

<WorkedExample title="Worked example 3: explain why bond formation releases energy">
Two separated atoms approach and form a stable covalent bond. As they move toward the equilibrium distance, attractive interactions lower the system's potential energy. The energy difference must be transferred away from the system, so bond formation releases energy.

The reverse process—breaking the bond—requires energy to move the atoms from the potential-energy minimum to the separated-atom reference level.
</WorkedExample>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="5. Don't confuse intramolecular bonds with intermolecular attractions" description="The distinction matters during phase changes">

An intramolecular bond holds atoms together within a molecule. An intermolecular force attracts separate molecules to one another. A molecular substance can melt or boil when intermolecular attractions are disrupted while its covalent bonds remain intact.

For example, boiling water separates H₂O molecules from one another; it does not normally break the O–H covalent bonds. This is why a phase change is not automatically a chemical reaction.

<Mistake title="“Breaking bonds releases energy”">
Bond breaking requires energy. Bond formation releases energy. For a chemical reaction, calculate the overall energy change by accounting for both the bonds broken and the bonds formed.
</Mistake>

<Mistake title="Using the curve's horizontal position to compare strength">
The horizontal position of the minimum gives equilibrium bond distance. The vertical depth of the energy well relates to bond dissociation energy. A shorter bond is often stronger for the same atom pair, but on a graph the well depth is the direct energy evidence.
</Mistake>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="6. AP-style practice" description="Interpret a graph and explain a bond-energy trend">

A potential-energy graph for bond A has a minimum at 100 pm and a well depth of 500 kJ/mol. Bond B has a minimum at 140 pm and a well depth of 300 kJ/mol.

**(a)** Which bond has the shorter equilibrium bond length?

**(b)** Which bond requires more energy to separate the atoms?

**(c)** Explain why the two answers use different parts of the graph.

<LessonSection title="Reveal the reasoning" description="Read the horizontal and vertical axes separately">

**(a)** Bond A has the shorter equilibrium bond length because its minimum occurs at 100 pm, compared with 140 pm for B.

**(b)** Bond A requires more energy to separate the atoms because its potential-energy well is deeper: 500 kJ/mol compared with 300 kJ/mol.

**(c)** Bond length is read from the horizontal location of the minimum. Bond strength, in this model, is related to the vertical depth of the well. They are different pieces of information.
</LessonSection>

</LessonSection>
