# A bond is an energy landscape

Two bonded atoms are not motionless dots connected by a line. Their nuclei repel, their electrons repel, and each nucleus attracts the other atom's electrons. The observed bond length is the distance where the combined interactions produce a minimum in potential energy.

<LessonSection title="1. Read the potential-energy curve" description="Identify the minimum, the equilibrium distance, and the dissociation energy." defaultOpen>

A typical potential-energy curve plots potential energy against the distance between two atoms.

- **Far apart:** interaction approaches zero by convention; the atoms are barely interacting.
- **Moving closer from far away:** attractive interactions lower the potential energy.
- **At the minimum:** the atoms are at the equilibrium bond distance, where the net force is zero.
- **Too close:** repulsions rise steeply, so potential energy increases sharply.

The minimum is not the point where the atoms have zero energy. It is the lowest potential energy on the curve, relative to the chosen reference.

The equilibrium distance is called the **bond length** in the diatomic model. The energy required to separate the atoms is the **bond dissociation energy** for that specified bond and process. A deeper potential-energy well generally corresponds to a stronger bond.

<LessonSection title="2. Bond length and bond strength" description="Use bond order to predict trends, then respect the data.">

For bonds between the same pair of atoms, higher bond order generally means more shared electron density between the nuclei. That tends to pull the nuclei closer and makes the bond harder to break.

| Bond | Typical trend |
|---|---|
| Single | Longest and weakest |
| Double | Shorter and stronger |
| Triple | Shortest and strongest |

These are trends, not a substitute for data when comparing different atom pairs. Atom size and the bonding environment also matter.

<LessonSection title="3. Energy in bond breaking and formation" description="Separate the energy of a bond from the energy of a whole reaction.">

Breaking a bond requires energy because the bonded atoms must be moved out of the attractive energy well. Forming a bond releases energy as the system moves toward a lower potential energy.

For a reaction, the overall energy change depends on **all** bonds broken and formed, not on a single bond:

- Energy is absorbed to break reactant bonds.
- Energy is released when product bonds form.
- The net change is the energy absorbed minus the energy released, using the appropriate bond-energy values.

This is why "breaking bonds releases energy" is incorrect. Bond breaking requires energy; bond formation releases energy.

<Lightbulb title="Keep the system boundary clear">
The energy needed to break one bond is not automatically the reaction's total energy change. A reaction may break strong bonds and form even stronger bonds, releasing energy overall; or it may form bonds that release less energy than was needed to break the starting bonds.

</Lightbulb>

## Exam-ready summary

- Equilibrium bond distance occurs at the potential-energy minimum.
- A deeper energy well generally indicates a stronger bond.
- For the same atom pair, higher bond order generally means shorter, stronger bonds.
- Breaking bonds requires energy; forming bonds releases energy.
- Reaction energy depends on the complete set of bonds broken and formed.
