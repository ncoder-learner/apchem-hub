A material's properties follow from how its particles are held together and how its electrons are distributed. The first question is not “Which bond name do I memorize?” It is **“What are the valence electrons doing, and what structure results?”**

<LessonSection title="1. Ionic bonding: ions attract throughout a lattice" description="Electron transfer creates charged particles; attraction stabilizes the solid" defaultOpen>

In a simple ionic model, one atom transfers electrons to another. A metal such as sodium loses an electron to become Na⁺; a nonmetal such as chlorine gains an electron to become Cl⁻. Oppositely charged ions attract through electrostatic forces.

The important distinction is that **electron transfer forms the ions, but electrostatic attraction holds the ionic solid together**. Solid NaCl is not a collection of separate NaCl molecules. It is an extended three-dimensional lattice in which each ion interacts with many neighboring ions.

<ChemVisual kind="ionic-lattice" />

This model predicts several properties:
- Many ionic solids have high melting points because substantial energy is needed to disrupt the lattice.
- They are often brittle because shifting layers can place like charges next to one another, causing repulsion and fracture.
- They generally do not conduct electricity as solids because the ions are fixed in position. When molten or dissolved, mobile ions can carry charge.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="2. Covalent bonding: shared electron density" description="The shared electrons attract both nuclei">

In a covalent bond, atoms share electron density. The shared electrons are attracted to both positively charged nuclei, helping hold the atoms together. Covalent bonding can produce **discrete molecules**, such as H₂O and CO₂, or an **extended covalent network**, such as diamond or silicon dioxide.

These structures must not be confused:
- In a molecular substance, covalent bonds hold atoms together inside each molecule, while intermolecular forces attract separate molecules.
- In a covalent-network solid, covalent bonds extend through the structure. There is no separate set of small molecules held together only by intermolecular forces.

This difference helps explain why many molecular substances melt or boil at lower temperatures than network solids. Melting a molecular solid usually changes how intact molecules pack together; melting a network solid requires disrupting bonds throughout the network.

<QuickCheck question="Which statement correctly compares molecular and network covalent substances?" options={["Both are always made of separate molecules","Molecular substances contain discrete molecules; network solids have covalent bonds extending throughout the structure","Network solids contain only ions","Molecular substances contain no covalent bonds"]} correctIndex={1} explanation="Molecules contain covalent bonds within each particle. In a network solid, covalent bonds connect atoms through an extended structure." />

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="3. Metallic bonding: delocalized electrons" description="Why metals conduct and can change shape">

In a metal, valence electrons are delocalized across many atoms rather than assigned to one specific pair of atoms. The positive metal-ion cores are attracted to this mobile electron density.

<ChemVisual kind="metallic-bonding" />

The model explains characteristic metal properties:

- **Electrical conductivity:** delocalized electrons can move through the solid and carry charge.
- **Thermal conductivity:** mobile electrons transfer energy through the material.
- **Malleability and ductility:** layers of metal-ion cores can shift while the electron cloud continues to hold the structure together. The solid can change shape without immediately fracturing in the way a typical ionic crystal does.

Metals vary widely in hardness and melting point, so “metallic” does not imply one exact numerical property. The model explains why many metals share these broad behaviors.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="4. Bond polarity: electron sharing can be uneven" description="Use electronegativity to explain partial charges">

**Electronegativity** describes how strongly an atom attracts shared electron density in a bond. When two bonded atoms have different electronegativities, the shared electron density is pulled toward the more electronegative atom.

In a polar covalent bond:
- The atom that attracts electron density more strongly becomes partially negative (δ−).
- The other atom becomes partially positive (δ+).
- The electrons remain shared; these are partial charges, not necessarily full ionic charges.

For example, in H–Cl, chlorine is more electronegative than hydrogen. The shared electron density is pulled toward chlorine, so Cl is δ− and H is δ+.

Bonding models are not three isolated boxes. Ionic and covalent character can be viewed as part of a continuum of electron distribution. Electronegativity difference helps predict bond polarity, but the structure and properties of the whole substance still matter.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example 1: explain why H–Cl is polar covalent">
**Evidence:** Chlorine is more electronegative than hydrogen.

**Model:** The shared electron density is pulled toward chlorine.

**Conclusion:** Chlorine has a partial negative charge and hydrogen a partial positive charge. The electrons are still shared, so the bond is described as polar covalent rather than as fully transferred electrons in the simple ionic model.
</WorkedExample>

<LessonSection title="5. Choose a bonding model from evidence" description="Use structure and mobile charge carriers">

| Observation | Likely model | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Solid does not conduct; molten material conducts | Ionic | Ions are fixed in the solid and mobile in the melt |
| Solid conducts and can be hammered into shape | Metallic | Delocalized electrons carry charge; layers can shift |
| Low-melting substance contains discrete molecules | Molecular covalent substance | Melting usually disrupts intermolecular attractions |
| Very hard solid with a high melting point and an extended atomic network | Covalent network | Covalent bonds extend through the structure |

These are evidence-based predictions, not infallible rules. A good answer connects the observation to a particle model and then to a mechanism. Do not identify a bond type from one clue if other observations contradict it.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example 2: classify an unknown solid">
An unknown solid is brittle, has a high melting point, does not conduct electricity as a solid, and conducts when molten.

The evidence is consistent with an ionic solid. Its lattice contains oppositely charged ions and requires substantial energy to disrupt. The ions are fixed in the solid, so they cannot carry charge; in the molten state, they become mobile and conduct. Brittleness follows because shifting layers can align like charges and create strong repulsion.
</WorkedExample>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="6. AP-style practice" description="Explain a property using the bonding model">

Three solids are tested:

- **Solid A:** conducts electricity as a solid and can be bent.
- **Solid B:** does not conduct as a solid but conducts when molten.
- **Solid C:** consists of discrete molecules and melts at a much lower temperature than A or B.

**(a)** Identify the most likely bonding/structure model for each solid.

**(b)** Explain why Solid B conducts only when molten.

**(c)** Explain why melting Solid C usually does not break the covalent bonds within its molecules.

<LessonSection title="Reveal the reasoning" description="Check whether the mechanism matches each observation">

**(a)** A is most consistent with metallic bonding; B with an ionic lattice; C with a molecular covalent substance.

**(b)** Solid B contains ions fixed in lattice positions. When molten, the ions become mobile and can carry charge.

**(c)** In a molecular substance, covalent bonds hold atoms together inside each molecule, while intermolecular attractions hold separate molecules near one another. Melting usually disrupts the intermolecular arrangement; the molecules remain intact.
</LessonSection>

</LessonSection>
