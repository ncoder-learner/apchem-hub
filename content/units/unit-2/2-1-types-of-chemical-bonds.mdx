# The big idea

A material's properties are not random. They follow from how its particles are held together and how easily electrons or particles can move. The first question is always: **what are the valence electrons doing?**


<ChemVisual kind="metallic-bonding" />

<p><strong>Translate structure into a property:</strong> mobile electrons explain electrical conductivity in metals; fixed ions explain why solid ionic compounds do not conduct; shared electron density explains directional covalent bonds. These models describe different patterns of electron distribution, not three unrelated lists to memorize.</p>

<LessonSection title="1. Three bonding models" description="Start by identifying what happens to the valence electrons." defaultOpen>

## Ionic bonding: attraction between ions

In an ionic solid such as sodium chloride, electrons are transferred in the process of forming ions. Sodium loses an electron to become Na⁺; chlorine gains one to become Cl⁻. The solid is not made of isolated NaCl molecules. It is an extended arrangement of alternating positive and negative ions held together by electrostatic attraction.

**Important distinction:** electron transfer explains how the ions form; the solid is held together by attractions among many ions throughout the lattice.

## Covalent bonding: shared electron density

In a covalent bond, atoms share a pair of electrons. The shared electron density is attracted to both nuclei, holding the atoms together. Covalent bonds form molecules such as H₂O and CO₂, but covalent bonding can also create extended networks, as in diamond and silicon dioxide.

## Metallic bonding: mobile, delocalized electrons

In a metal, valence electrons are delocalized across many atoms rather than assigned to one particular pair of atoms. The mobile electron density attracts the positive metal-ion cores and helps hold the solid together. Because electrons can move through the material, metals conduct electricity and heat well.

| Model | Electron behavior | Typical structure |
|---|---|---|
| Ionic | Electrons transfer to form ions; ions attract | Extended ion lattice |
| Covalent | Electron pairs are shared between atoms | Molecules or covalent network |
| Metallic | Valence electrons are delocalized | Metal-ion cores surrounded by mobile electrons |

<LessonSection title="2. Use properties as evidence" description="Connect the particle model to what you can observe.">

A bond label is useful only if you can use it to explain evidence. Ask which particles are present and what is free to move.

- **Ionic solids** are often hard and brittle, with high melting points. Their ions are locked into a lattice. If layers shift, like charges can line up and strongly repel, causing the crystal to fracture.
- **Molecular covalent substances** often have lower melting and boiling points than ionic solids because melting or boiling a molecular substance usually separates molecules by overcoming intermolecular attractions, not by breaking every covalent bond inside each molecule.
- **Covalent-network solids** can be extremely hard and have very high melting points because atoms are connected through an extended network of covalent bonds.
- **Metals** are malleable and ductile because layers of metal-ion cores can shift while the delocalized electron cloud continues to hold the structure together.

<Lightbulb title="Do not confuse bonds inside a molecule with attractions between molecules">
When water boils, the molecules separate from one another, but the O–H covalent bonds inside each H₂O molecule are not normally broken. This distinction becomes central in Unit 3, where intermolecular forces explain phase changes.

</Lightbulb>

<LessonSection title="3. Electronegativity and bond character" description="Use electron-pulling ability without treating the categories as absolute.">

**Electronegativity** describes how strongly an atom attracts shared electron density in a bond. When bonded atoms have different electronegativities, the shared electron density is pulled more toward one atom, producing a polar covalent bond.

- Similar electronegativities usually mean more evenly shared electron density.
- A larger electronegativity difference usually means a more polar bond.
- Ionic and covalent bonding are useful models along a continuum; the electronegativity difference alone does not tell you every property of a real substance.

For a polar covalent bond, the more electronegative atom is partially negative (δ−), while the other is partially positive (δ+). These are **partial charges**, not full ionic charges.

<WorkedExample title="Make a claim from bond evidence">
**Claim:** The H–Cl bond is polar covalent.

**Evidence:** Chlorine is more electronegative than hydrogen, so the shared electron density is pulled toward chlorine.

**Reasoning:** The uneven distribution creates δ− on chlorine and δ+ on hydrogen, but the electrons remain shared rather than being described as fully transferred in the simple ionic model.
</WorkedExample>

## Exam-ready summary

- Identify the electron model: transferred into ions, shared between atoms, or delocalized across a metal.
- Explain properties using particle arrangement and mobility, not just a memorized label.
- Distinguish partial charges in polar covalent bonds from full ionic charges.
- A claim about bonding should be supported by evidence about electrons, structure, or measured properties.
