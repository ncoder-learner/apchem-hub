A Lewis structure is electron bookkeeping, not a photograph of the electrons. Sometimes several valid Lewis structures can be drawn with the same arrangement of atoms but different placements of electrons. **Formal charge** helps evaluate those structures, and **resonance** explains why the real electron distribution cannot be represented by just one of them.

\n\n<InteractiveLab kind="lewis" title="Compare Lewis contributors and track valence electrons in ozone" />\n\n<LessonSection title="1. Calculate formal charge" description="Assign the valence electrons consistently" defaultOpen>

<ChemVisual kind="resonance" />

Use this equation for each atom:

**Formal charge = V − N − B/2**

- V = valence electrons in the neutral atom
- N = nonbonding electrons assigned to that atom
- B = total bonding electrons around that atom

A quick equivalent form is:

**Formal charge = valence electrons − lone-pair electrons − number of bonds**

In the shortcut, a single bond counts as one bond, a double bond as two, and a triple bond as three. The formal charges on all atoms must add up to the overall charge of the molecule or ion.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example 1: formal charge in NH₄⁺">
Nitrogen has 5 valence electrons, no lone-pair electrons, and four N–H bonds.

Formal charge on N = 5 − 0 − 4 = **+1**.

Each hydrogen has 1 − 0 − 1 = 0. The total formal charge is +1, matching the ammonium ion's overall charge.
</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample title="Worked example 2: formal charge on oxygen">
An oxygen atom has three lone pairs and one single bond.

Oxygen has 6 valence electrons, 6 nonbonding electrons, and one bond.

Formal charge = 6 − 6 − 1 = **−1**.

This is why a singly bonded oxygen with three lone pairs commonly carries a formal charge of −1 in Lewis structures.
</WorkedExample>

A good candidate Lewis structure generally minimizes unnecessary formal-charge magnitude and places negative formal charge on more electronegative atoms when other factors are comparable. These are guidelines for comparing plausible structures, not rules that override the required electron count or known chemistry.

<LessonSection title="2. Resonance: move electrons, not atoms" description="The sigma-bond skeleton stays the same">

Resonance structures have the same arrangement of nuclei but differ in where electrons are drawn. When converting one resonance form to another, move lone pairs and π bonds; do not move atoms or change the sigma-bond framework.

The real species is not rapidly switching between separate structures. It is better described as a **resonance hybrid** with electron density delocalized across the relevant atoms.

Nitrate, NO₃⁻, is a standard example. In one conventional Lewis structure, one N–O bond is drawn as a double bond and two as single bonds. But there are three equivalent resonance forms, placing the double bond at each oxygen in turn. The actual N–O bonds are equivalent in the resonance hybrid rather than one fixed double bond and two fixed single bonds.

For another nitrate-ion representation, see [Study.com: Resonance Structures of the Nitrate Ion](https://homework.study.com/explanation/explain-the-resonance-structures-for-the-nitrate-ion-no3.html). Focus on what changes between drawings (electron placement) and what stays fixed (the nuclei and sigma-bond framework).

<LessonSection title="3. Why resonance changes the predicted bonds" description="Connect the model to experimental evidence">

In a single nitrate Lewis structure, the double bond is drawn to one oxygen. If that drawing described the full physical structure, one N–O bond would be shorter and stronger than the other two. Experiments show that all three N–O bonds in nitrate are equivalent. The resonance-hybrid model explains this evidence: the bonding is delocalized across all three N–O connections.

For three equivalent nitrate resonance contributors, each N–O connection is single in two contributors and double in one contributor. The average bond order is therefore:

**Average bond order = (1 + 1 + 2) / 3 = 4/3 ≈ 1.33**

This does not mean each bond literally switches between a single and a double bond. It means the actual electron distribution is spread across the three equivalent connections.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example 3: why the two N–O bonds in nitrite are equivalent">
Nitrite, NO₂⁻, has two equivalent resonance forms. One has a double bond to the first oxygen and a single bond to the second; the other swaps those positions. Each N–O bond is single in one contributor and double in the other, giving an average bond order of (1 + 2)/2 = **1.5**. The real N–O bonds are equivalent and intermediate between a simple single and double bond.
</WorkedExample>

<LessonSection title="4. Compare candidate structures" description="Use a sequence, not a vague preference">

When several Lewis structures are possible:

1. **Check the electron count.** Every candidate must use the correct total number of valence electrons.
2. **Check octets and known exceptions.** Typical second-period atoms should usually satisfy the octet rule.
3. **Calculate formal charges.** The sum must equal the overall charge.
4. **Prefer smaller formal-charge magnitudes** when other factors are comparable.
5. **Place negative formal charge on more electronegative atoms** when that is consistent with the rest of the structure.
6. **Check resonance.** If equivalent atom arrangements allow the electrons to be drawn in multiple valid positions, include the relevant resonance contributors.

Formal charge is a bookkeeping model. It is not a direct measurement of the actual partial charge on an atom. In a resonance hybrid, electron density can be spread over several atoms.

<Mistake title="Thinking resonance forms are separate molecules in equilibrium">
A resonance hybrid is one species with delocalized electrons, not a molecule that rapidly switches between drawings. The double-headed resonance arrow does not mean an equilibrium between different substances.
</Mistake>

<Mistake title="Forgetting the overall charge">
For a polyatomic ion, the sum of the formal charges must match the charge shown outside the brackets. If the individual formal charges add to zero for NO₃⁻, the structure has been counted incorrectly.
</Mistake>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="5. AP-style practice" description="Calculate formal charge and interpret resonance evidence">

A student draws one Lewis structure of nitrate, NO₃⁻, with one N=O double bond and two N–O single bonds.

**(a)** Calculate the formal charge on nitrogen.

**(b)** Calculate the formal charge on the double-bonded oxygen and on each single-bonded oxygen.

**(c)** Explain why experiments show three equivalent N–O bond lengths even though the drawing has one double bond and two single bonds.

<LessonSection title="Reveal the reasoning" description="Check the formal charges and the resonance model">

**(a)** Nitrogen has 5 valence electrons, no lone pairs, and four bonds in this Lewis structure. Formal charge = 5 − 0 − 4 = **+1**.

**(b)** The double-bonded oxygen has two lone pairs and two bonds: 6 − 4 − 2 = **0**. Each single-bonded oxygen has three lone pairs and one bond: 6 − 6 − 1 = **−1**. The total is +1 + 0 − 1 − 1 = −1, matching NO₃⁻.

**(c)** Three equivalent resonance contributors place the double bond at each oxygen in turn. The real species is a resonance hybrid with electron density delocalized across all three N–O connections, so the three bonds are equivalent.
</LessonSection>
