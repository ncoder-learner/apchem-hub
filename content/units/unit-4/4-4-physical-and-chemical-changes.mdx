A change in appearance is not automatically a chemical reaction. Ice melting looks dramatic at the macroscopic scale, but the particles remain H₂O molecules. When hydrogen and oxygen form water, the atoms become connected in new combinations. The key distinction is whether the identity and bonding of the chemical species change, not whether the change is easy to see.

<LessonSection title="1. Physical change: the species remain the same" description="Phase and arrangement can change without changing chemical identity" defaultOpen>

In a physical change, the chemical identity of the particles remains the same even though their spacing, arrangement, motion, or phase may change. Melting ice, boiling water, crushing salt crystals, and dissolving many molecular substances are examples of physical processes.

During melting, water molecules in ice become more mobile and lose the ordered crystal arrangement. The O–H covalent bonds within each H₂O molecule do not break as part of melting. Intermolecular attractions change as the solid becomes liquid, but the molecules remain water.

Dissolving an ionic solid can involve separating the ions from a crystal lattice and surrounding them with water molecules. For a soluble salt such as NaCl, the dissolved species are hydrated Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions. The process changes their arrangement and environment; it does not convert sodium into a new element or chloride into a different ion.

<LessonSection title="2. Chemical change: chemical species are transformed" description="Look for new bonding and new species">

A chemical reaction transforms reactant species into product species. Bonds are broken and formed, and the atoms are rearranged. Examples include combustion, oxidation of iron, acid–base neutralization, and precipitation reactions.

For methane combustion, **CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O**. Carbon atoms that were in methane end up in carbon dioxide; oxygen atoms that were in O₂ end up in carbon dioxide and water. The product species differ from the reactants, even though each type of atom is conserved.

Observations that can support a chemical-change claim include:
- a precipitate forming when solutions are mixed;
- a gas forming in a process not explained by boiling or release of dissolved gas;
- a persistent color change associated with new species;
- measurable energy transfer accompanying a reaction.

No single observation is universally decisive. A temperature change can result from physical mixing or phase change, and a color change may occur without a chemical reaction. Strong reasoning connects the observation to evidence that new chemical species formed.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: classify the process">
A student heats a sample of solid iodine. Purple vapor forms, and when the vapor cools it deposits as solid iodine.

This is a physical change: I₂(s) → I₂(g) → I₂(s). The phase changes, but the particles remain iodine molecules. The observation of a color change does not by itself establish a chemical reaction.
</WorkedExample>

<LessonSection title="3. Energy changes do not define the category by themselves" description="Endothermic and exothermic processes can be physical or chemical">

Both physical changes and chemical reactions can involve energy transfer. Melting ice absorbs energy, and vaporizing water absorbs energy. Combustion releases energy, and some dissolutions release or absorb energy. Therefore, “temperature changed” does not by itself distinguish physical from chemical change.

At the particle level, energy is transferred when the system's potential-energy arrangement or particle motion changes. In a reaction, energy is required to disrupt existing interactions and can be released when new interactions form. The overall energy change depends on the difference between the energy absorbed and released.

**Exothermic** means the system transfers energy to its surroundings; **endothermic** means the system absorbs energy from its surroundings. These labels describe energy transfer, not whether the process is chemical or physical.

<LessonSection title="4. AP-style evidence and explanation" description="Make a claim at the level the evidence supports">

A student observes that a clear solution becomes warm after a salt dissolves. The strongest conclusion from the observation alone is that the process transferred energy to the solution and surroundings. To determine whether the process is a chemical reaction, the student must consider the species before and after dissolving and the particle-level model.

For a strong free-response explanation:
1. State whether the chemical species remain the same or are transformed.
2. Identify the particle-level change (phase/spacing versus new bonding/species).
3. Use the observation as evidence, not as a substitute for the explanation.
4. Avoid claiming that atoms are created or destroyed.

For example, “The ice melted” is a description. “This is a physical change because H₂O molecules remain H₂O; the particles gain mobility and the ordered solid structure disappears” is an explanation grounded in the particle model.

For a review of phase changes and chemical transformations, see [OpenStax Chemistry 2e](https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/10-1-intermolecular-forces).

</LessonSection>
