Reaction classifications are useful because they help you recognize patterns in how substances transform. But a pattern is a starting point, not a substitute for chemical evidence: the actual products depend on the identities and conditions of the reactants.

\n\n<InteractiveLab kind="reaction-types" title="Compare synthesis, decomposition, replacement, exchange, and combustion patterns" />\n\n<LessonSection title="1. Recognize common reaction patterns" description="Use the pattern to organize, then balance and verify" defaultOpen>

**Synthesis:** two or more reactants form a product. A simple pattern is A + B → AB. Example: 2Mg(s) + O₂(g) → 2MgO(s).

**Decomposition:** one compound breaks into simpler substances. A pattern is AB → A + B. Example: CaCO₃(s) → CaO(s) + CO₂(g) when heated.

**Combustion:** a substance reacts with O₂ and releases energy. Hydrocarbon combustion in sufficient oxygen commonly produces CO₂ and H₂O, but incomplete combustion can also produce CO or carbon. Example: CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O.

**Single-replacement (single-displacement):** an element reacts with a compound and may replace one of its elements. Whether the reaction occurs depends on relative reactivity and chemical context; do not assume every proposed replacement is spontaneous.

**Double-replacement:** ions from two compounds exchange partners. In aqueous solution, the reaction is driven when a product forms a precipitate, a gas, or a weak electrolyte such as water. If all species remain soluble strong electrolytes, there may be no net ionic reaction.

<LessonSection title="2. Predict double-replacement outcomes using ions" description="A partner exchange only matters if something changes">

For aqueous ionic compounds, write the ions first and consider possible pairings. Then use solubility rules and acid–base/gas-forming patterns to decide whether there is a driving force.

Example: AgNO₃(aq) mixed with NaCl(aq) can form AgCl(s) and NaNO₃(aq). Silver chloride is insoluble, so a precipitate forms. The net ionic equation is **Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s)**.

By contrast, mixing NaNO₃(aq) and KCl(aq) gives Na⁺, NO₃⁻, K⁺, and Cl⁻ in solution. The possible products, NaCl and KNO₃, are both soluble, so all ions remain in solution. Under the usual aqueous conditions, there is no net ionic equation because no species undergoes a net change.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: predict whether a precipitate forms">
Mix BaCl₂(aq) and Na₂SO₄(aq).

Exchange ions to identify possible products: BaSO₄ and NaCl. Solubility rules indicate BaSO₄ is insoluble while NaCl is soluble. The balanced molecular equation is BaCl₂(aq) + Na₂SO₄(aq) → BaSO₄(s) + 2NaCl(aq).

The net ionic equation is **Ba²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → BaSO₄(s)**. Sodium and chloride remain dissolved and are spectators.
</WorkedExample>

<LessonSection title="3. Single replacement and activity reasoning" description="A pattern does not guarantee a reaction">

In a single-replacement reaction, a free element may displace another element from a compound if the free element is sufficiently reactive under the conditions. For example, magnesium can displace copper from copper(II) ions: **Mg(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Mg²⁺(aq) + Cu(s)**. Magnesium atoms lose electrons and enter solution as Mg²⁺; Cu²⁺ ions gain electrons and become copper metal.

A less reactive metal generally cannot displace a more reactive metal ion from solution. Thus, the proposed reaction Cu(s) + Mg²⁺(aq) → Cu²⁺(aq) + Mg(s) does not occur spontaneously under ordinary aqueous conditions. Product prediction requires the activity series or redox reasoning, not just rearranging symbols.

<LessonSection title="4. Classify carefully and balance last" description="Use evidence, not labels alone">

A reaction can fit more than one useful classification. Combustion of a hydrocarbon is also a redox process. Metal reacting with an acid can be described as single replacement and redox. A reaction of an acid and base is neutralization and may also be treated as a double-replacement pattern.

When asked to predict or classify:
1. Identify reactants and their states.
2. Determine whether the prompt provides conditions (heat, aqueous solution, excess oxygen).
3. Predict plausible products from the pattern and chemistry.
4. Check whether a net reaction is supported by a precipitate, gas, weak electrolyte, or electron transfer.
5. Balance atoms and charge.

Do not classify solely by counting how many reactants and products appear. The chemical change and particle behavior are the underlying evidence.

For a reference on reaction classes, see [OpenStax Chemistry 2e: Classifying Chemical Reactions](https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/4-2-classifying-chemical-reactions).

## Readiness check

You should be able to identify common reaction patterns, determine when an aqueous partner exchange has a driving force, use activity reasoning for single replacement, and avoid claiming a reaction occurs based only on a memorized pattern.
