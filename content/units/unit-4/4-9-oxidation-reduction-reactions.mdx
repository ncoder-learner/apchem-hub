Oxidation–reduction (redox) reactions involve electron transfer or changes in oxidation number. They connect many reaction types: metals reacting with ions, combustion, and several processes used in electrochemistry. The central task is to determine which species loses electrons and which gains them.



<InteractiveLab kind="redox" title="Track which species loses electrons and which species gains them" />

<LessonSection title="1. Oxidation and reduction happen together" description="Use electron bookkeeping" defaultOpen>

**Oxidation** is loss of electrons; **reduction** is gain of electrons. A memory aid is OIL RIG: Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain. If one species loses electrons, another species must accept them, so oxidation and reduction occur together.

For example:
- Mg(s) → Mg²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ is oxidation; magnesium loses two electrons.
- Cu²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ → Cu(s) is reduction; copper(II) gains two electrons.

Adding the half-reactions gives **Mg(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Mg²⁺(aq) + Cu(s)**. The two electrons cancel because electrons lost by Mg equal electrons gained by Cu²⁺.

Copper's oxidation number decreases from +2 to 0." />

<LessonSection title="2. Assign oxidation numbers" description="A bookkeeping system for electron ownership">

Oxidation numbers are formal bookkeeping values, not always the actual charges on atoms in covalent molecules. Use these common rules:

- An element in its standard elemental form has oxidation number 0 (Mg, O₂, Cl₂).
- A monatomic ion has oxidation number equal to its charge (Na⁺ is +1; S²⁻ is −2).
- Oxygen is usually −2 in compounds; hydrogen is usually +1 with nonmetals and −1 in metal hydrides.
- The sum of oxidation numbers equals the overall charge of the species.
- Group 1 metals are +1 in compounds; Group 2 metals are +2.

For water, H is usually +1. The molecule is neutral, so 2(+1) + oxidation number of O = 0, giving O = −2.

For sulfate, SO₄²⁻, oxygen contributes 4(−2) = −8. The total must equal −2, so sulfur is +6: S + (−8) = −2, therefore S = +6.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: identify the element oxidized">
In Mg(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Mg²⁺(aq) + Cu(s), Mg changes from 0 to +2, so magnesium is oxidized. Copper changes from +2 to 0, so copper is reduced. The oxidation-number changes match the electron transfer: Mg loses two electrons and Cu²⁺ gains two.
</WorkedExample>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="3. Identify oxidizing and reducing agents" description="The agent's name describes what it does to the other species">

The **reducing agent** donates electrons and is itself oxidized. The **oxidizing agent** accepts electrons and is itself reduced. This wording often feels backward: the oxidizing agent causes oxidation of the other species by accepting its electrons.

For Mg + Cu²⁺ → Mg²⁺ + Cu:
- Mg is the reducing agent because it gives electrons to Cu²⁺ and becomes Mg²⁺.
- Cu²⁺ is the oxidizing agent because it accepts electrons and becomes Cu.

<QuickCheck question="In the reaction 2Al + 3Cu²⁺ → 2Al³⁺ + 3Cu, which species is the oxidizing agent?" options={["Al(s)","Al³⁺(aq)","Cu²⁺(aq)","Cu(s)"]} correctIndex={2} explanation="Cu²⁺ accepts electrons and is reduced to Cu(s). The species that is reduced is the oxidizing agent." />

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="4. Balance simple redox equations by tracking electrons" description="Atoms and charge must both balance">

For a simple redox equation, write oxidation and reduction half-reactions, balance atoms other than H and O first, balance O with H₂O when appropriate, balance H with H⁺ in acidic solution, and balance charge with electrons. Then multiply half-reactions so electrons cancel, add them, and simplify. In basic solution, additional steps are needed to remove H⁺ using OH⁻ and simplify water.

At the AP Chemistry level, focus on recognizing oxidation-number changes and balancing the redox equations required by the prompt. Never add electrons to a final overall equation if they cancel as internal electron transfer.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: balance iron and silver ions">
Fe(s) + Ag⁺(aq) → Fe²⁺(aq) + Ag(s).

Oxidation: Fe → Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻.

Reduction: Ag⁺ + e⁻ → Ag. Multiply this reduction half-reaction by 2 so two electrons are accepted.

Add the half-reactions: **Fe(s) + 2Ag⁺(aq) → Fe²⁺(aq) + 2Ag(s)**.

Check atoms and charge: one Fe and two Ag on each side; the left has charge +2 and the right has charge +2. Electrons do not appear in the final equation.
</WorkedExample>
