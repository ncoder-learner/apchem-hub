When two aqueous solutions react, the most useful chemistry is often not the full list of dissolved ions. It is the small set of particles that actually changes. A net ionic equation removes ions that remain unchanged and shows the transformation directly.



<InteractiveLab kind="net-ionic" title="Mix aqueous solutions and identify spectator ions" />

<LessonSection title="1. What is actually present in aqueous solution?" description="Separate strong electrolytes from intact molecules" defaultOpen>

Soluble ionic compounds that are strong electrolytes are represented as separated ions in aqueous solution. For example, NaCl(aq) is represented as Na⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq). Soluble strong acids and strong bases are also represented as ions. By contrast, a molecular substance such as glucose remains primarily as intact molecules, and a weak acid such as CH₃COOH is represented mostly in its molecular form because it ionizes only partially.

State symbols matter:
- **(aq)** means dissolved in water; it does not automatically mean “write as ions.”
- **(s)** means a solid phase, including a precipitate.
- **(l)** means liquid; pure liquid water is usually left intact.
- **(g)** means gas.

Use solubility rules to decide whether an ionic product remains dissolved or forms a solid. A precipitate is not split into aqueous ions in the net ionic equation.

<LessonSection title="2. The three-equation method" description="Molecular → complete ionic → net ionic">

Use the same sequence each time:

1. Write and balance the **molecular equation** with correct formulas and state symbols.
2. Split soluble strong electrolytes into their ions to write the **complete ionic equation**. Keep solids, gases, weak electrolytes, and pure liquids intact.
3. Cancel identical ions that appear on both sides. These are **spectator ions**.
4. Check that atoms and total charge balance in the final net ionic equation.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: precipitation of silver chloride">
Mix aqueous silver nitrate and sodium chloride.

**Molecular equation:** AgNO₃(aq) + NaCl(aq) → AgCl(s) + NaNO₃(aq)

Silver chloride is insoluble, so it forms a precipitate. The aqueous reactants and sodium nitrate product are strong electrolytes.

**Complete ionic equation:** Ag⁺(aq) + NO₃⁻(aq) + Na⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s) + Na⁺(aq) + NO₃⁻(aq)

Na⁺ and NO₃⁻ appear unchanged on both sides. Cancel them.

**Net ionic equation:** **Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s)**

Check charge: the left side has total charge +1 − 1 = 0; the neutral solid on the right has charge 0. Atoms are also balanced.
</WorkedExample>

<LessonSection title="3. Acid–base neutralization and gas formation" description="Not every reaction produces a precipitate">

For a strong acid–strong base neutralization, hydronium and hydroxide form water. For example, the molecular equation HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l) becomes the net ionic equation **H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l)** in the simplified notation commonly used in AP Chemistry. More explicitly in water, the reacting acid species is H₃O⁺: H₃O⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → 2H₂O(l).

A weak acid should not be split completely. For acetic acid reacting with hydroxide, the net ionic equation is **CH₃COOH(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → CH₃COO⁻(aq) + H₂O(l)**. Writing CH₃COOH as separated H⁺ and acetate would incorrectly treat the weak acid as fully ionized before reaction.

Gas-forming reactions also have a characteristic net ionic equation. For example, carbonate ions reacting with acid can form carbon dioxide and water: **CO₃²⁻(aq) + 2H⁺(aq) → CO₂(g) + H₂O(l)**.

<LessonSection title="4. Common traps and an AP-style check" description="State symbols, charge, and spectator ions">

**Trap 1: splitting every aqueous formula.** Weak acids and weak bases are not strong electrolytes; aqueous does not mean fully ionized.

**Trap 2: splitting a precipitate.** If a product is AgCl(s), write it as a solid, not Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq).

**Trap 3: cancelling species that are not identical.** You can cancel the same ion with the same charge and state on both sides. Do not cancel an ion that changes charge or phase.

**Trap 4: skipping the charge check.** A net ionic equation must conserve both atoms and net charge.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: barium sulfate precipitation">
Barium chloride and sodium sulfate react in water.

Molecular equation: BaCl₂(aq) + Na₂SO₄(aq) → BaSO₄(s) + 2NaCl(aq).

Complete ionic equation: Ba²⁺ + 2Cl⁻ + 2Na⁺ + SO₄²⁻ → BaSO₄(s) + 2Na⁺ + 2Cl⁻.

Cancel Na⁺ and Cl⁻. The net ionic equation is **Ba²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → BaSO₄(s)**. Both sides have net charge zero, and the Ba, S, and O atom counts match.
</WorkedExample>
