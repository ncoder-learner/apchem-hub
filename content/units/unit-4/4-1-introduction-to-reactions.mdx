A chemical equation is a model of a rearrangement of matter. It tells you which substances are consumed and produced, and in what relative amounts. It does not say that atoms disappear or that coefficients are arbitrary numbers: the equation must obey conservation laws.

<LessonSection title="1. What changes during a chemical reaction?" description="Atoms are rearranged; nuclei are not changed" defaultOpen>

During an ordinary chemical reaction, existing atoms become connected in new ways. Chemical bonds break and form, electrons redistribute, and the products have different properties from the reactants. The nuclei of the atoms remain the same, so the number of atoms of each element is conserved.

For example, hydrogen gas reacts with oxygen gas to form water:

**H₂(g) + O₂(g) → H₂O(l)**

This is a useful *skeleton equation*, but it is not yet balanced. The left side has two oxygen atoms while the right side has one. It also has two hydrogen atoms on the left and two on the right. The oxygen mismatch means this equation cannot correctly represent the reaction in its current form.

A balanced equation is a particle-counting statement. The coefficients tell you the relative numbers of molecules, formula units, or moles involved; they do not tell you the mass ratio directly.

<LessonSection title="2. Balance equations by conserving each element" description="Change coefficients, never subscripts">

Use a systematic method:

1. Write correct formulas for the reactants and products.
2. Count each type of atom on both sides.
3. Adjust **coefficients** to make the counts match.
4. Recount every element and reduce coefficients to the smallest whole-number ratio.
5. If ions appear, check total charge as well as atoms.

For hydrogen reacting with oxygen, begin with H₂ + O₂ → H₂O. Put a 2 before H₂O to balance the two oxygen atoms: H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O. The right side now has four hydrogen atoms, so put a 2 before H₂: **2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O**. Both sides now have four H atoms and two O atoms.

The coefficient applies to the entire formula. Two H₂O molecules contain four H atoms and two O atoms. Do not change H₂O into H₂O₂ to make the counts match: that changes water into a different substance, hydrogen peroxide.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: combustion of propane">
Start with C₃H₈ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O.

Balance carbon first: three carbon atoms require 3CO₂. Balance hydrogen next: eight hydrogen atoms require 4H₂O. The products now contain 3(2) + 4(1) = 10 oxygen atoms, so the reactants need 5O₂.

Balanced equation: **C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O**.

Check: C = 3 on each side; H = 8 on each side; O = 10 on each side. The coefficients 1:5:3:4 are the simplest whole-number ratio.
</WorkedExample>

<LessonSection title="3. Coefficients encode mole ratios" description="The bridge from equations to quantitative chemistry">

For **2H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2H₂O(l)**, the coefficients give the ratio 2 mol H₂ : 1 mol O₂ : 2 mol H₂O. This does not mean that a reaction always uses exactly two molecules of hydrogen; any proportional amount follows the same ratio.

The equation also does not say that equal masses react. Hydrogen molecules are much lighter than oxygen molecules. Mole ratios come from coefficients; converting those mole amounts to grams requires molar masses.

A balanced equation is necessary for stoichiometry, but it is not a complete description of reaction rate, mechanism, or equilibrium. It records the net transformation, not every molecular collision along the way.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: interpreting a balanced equation">
For N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃, the coefficients mean 1 mol N₂ reacts with 3 mol H₂ to form 2 mol NH₃, assuming the reaction proceeds according to the written stoichiometric ratio.

If 0.40 mol N₂ reacts completely with sufficient H₂, the equation predicts 0.80 mol NH₃: 0.40 mol N₂ × (2 mol NH₃ / 1 mol N₂) = 0.80 mol NH₃.
</WorkedExample>

**Common mistake:** treating coefficients as mass ratios. A 1:3 mole ratio is not generally a 1:3 gram ratio. Use molar mass after establishing the mole ratio.

For further reference, consult [OpenStax Chemistry 2e: Chemical Reactions and Equations](https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/4-1-writing-and-balancing-chemical-equations).

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="4. AP-style application" description="Explain why a proposed equation fails">

A student writes Mg + O₂ → MgO and claims it is balanced because there is one magnesium-containing formula on each side. The claim is incorrect: the left side contains two oxygen atoms and the right side contains one. A balanced equation must conserve **each element individually**, not just the number of written formulas.

The correct equation is **2Mg(s) + O₂(g) → 2MgO(s)**. If asked to justify the coefficients, state the atom counts: two Mg atoms and two O atoms appear on each side.

## Readiness check

You should be able to balance equations without changing subscripts, verify atom and charge counts, explain what coefficients mean at the molecular and molar scales, and distinguish a balanced equation from a mechanism or rate law.
