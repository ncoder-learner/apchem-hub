Chemistry uses several representations of the same event. A balanced equation summarizes the relative amounts of substances; a particle diagram shows which particles are present and how they rearrange; an observation describes what can be measured, such as a color change, precipitate, or gas formation. AP questions often ask you to move from one representation to another without confusing the model with the real system.

\n\n<InteractiveLab kind="net-ionic" title="Connect dissolved ions, a precipitate, and the net ionic equation" />\n\n<LessonSection title="1. Three levels of representation" description="Macroscopic, particulate, and symbolic" defaultOpen>

**Macroscopic evidence** is what you can observe or measure: a solid appears, a solution changes color, gas bubbles form, or temperature changes. Evidence can support a reaction claim, but one observation by itself may not prove which chemical reaction occurred.

**Particulate representations** show atoms, molecules, or ions as symbols or spheres. They help explain the observations by showing particles before and after reaction. A particle diagram must conserve the number of each type of atom; in ionic reactions, it must also represent charges correctly.

**Symbolic representations** include chemical formulas, state symbols, coefficients, and net ionic equations. Coefficients encode relative amounts, while subscripts define composition. State symbols identify the physical phase.

These representations are complementary. A particle diagram may reveal that two ions combine into a solid, while the net ionic equation gives the concise symbolic statement of that transformation.

<LessonSection title="2. Read particle diagrams quantitatively" description="Count particles and preserve identity">

When interpreting a particle diagram, use a fixed sequence:

1. Identify the key for each symbol or color. Do not assume a color means a particular element unless the key says so.
2. Count each type of atom in the reactant particles.
3. Identify which bonds or groupings change.
4. Count each type of atom in the product particles.
5. Compare the diagram with the balanced equation and the stated conditions.

For example, in **N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃**, one nitrogen molecule and three hydrogen molecules form two ammonia molecules. Before reaction there are two N atoms and six H atoms. After reaction, two NH₃ molecules also contain two N atoms and six H atoms. The number of molecules changes from four to two, but the number of atoms of each element is conserved.

A correct particle drawing must reflect the stoichiometric ratio. If it shows one N₂ molecule reacting with only one H₂ molecule and forming ammonia with no leftover particles, it is inconsistent with the balanced equation because there is not enough hydrogen to make two NH₃ molecules.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: identify the limiting particle count">
A particle diagram starts with 2 N₂ molecules and 3 H₂ molecules. The reaction is N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃.

Each N₂ needs three H₂ molecules. The three H₂ molecules can react with only one N₂ molecule, forming two NH₃ molecules. One N₂ molecule remains unreacted. The maximum product count is two NH₃ molecules, not four, because hydrogen is the limiting reactant.
</WorkedExample>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="3. Translate observations into a chemical model" description="Evidence is not the same as the explanation">

Suppose two clear solutions are mixed and a cloudy solid appears. The observation is that a solid phase formed. A plausible chemical model is that dissolved ions combined to form a compound with low solubility. To identify the net ionic equation, you still need the identities of the ions and solubility information.

If a gas is produced, bubbles may be evidence of a reaction, but bubbles can also arise from boiling or dissolved gas escaping. Temperature change can indicate energy transfer, but it does not identify the products. Strong answers connect a specific observation to a particle-level explanation and avoid claiming more than the evidence supports.

**Claim–evidence–reasoning pattern:**
- **Claim:** state what happened, such as formation of a precipitate.
- **Evidence:** report the observation or data.
- **Reasoning:** connect it to particle behavior, such as formation of an insoluble ionic solid.

<LessonSection title="4. Match the representation to the question" description="Do not read more into a model than it contains">

| If the prompt asks… | Use the representation to… |
|---|---|
| How much product forms? | Use balanced-equation coefficients and stoichiometry. |
| Which ions react? | Write the complete and net ionic equations. |
| Why does a precipitate form? | Explain the particle-level formation of a low-solubility solid. |
| What evidence supports a reaction? | Cite observations or measured data, then explain them. |
| Is the model conserved? | Count each element and check charge where relevant. |

An equation does not show molecular shape or reaction speed unless those details are specifically included. A particle diagram is often schematic rather than to scale. An observation is evidence, not a full mechanism.
