Acid–base reactions are often easiest to understand as proton transfer. In the Brønsted–Lowry model, an acid donates H⁺ and a base accepts H⁺. This definition explains why acids and bases can react even when hydroxide ions are not written explicitly in the reactants.

<LessonSection title="1. Identify the proton donor and acceptor" description="Track H⁺, not just the formula labels" defaultOpen>

A **Brønsted–Lowry acid** donates a proton, H⁺. A **Brønsted–Lowry base** accepts a proton. When an acid donates a proton, it becomes its **conjugate base**; when a base accepts a proton, it becomes its **conjugate acid**.

For example: **HCl + H₂O → H₃O⁺ + Cl⁻**. HCl donates H⁺, so HCl is the acid and Cl⁻ is its conjugate base. H₂O accepts H⁺, so water is the base and H₃O⁺ is its conjugate acid.

In aqueous solution, a proton is not present as a free isolated H⁺ ion; it is associated with water, commonly represented as H₃O⁺. AP equations sometimes use H⁺(aq) as a compact notation for hydronium-related chemistry, but the physical model is proton transfer to water.

<LessonSection title="2. Strong and weak acids are not the same as concentrated and dilute" description="Strength is about ionization; concentration is amount per volume">

A strong acid ionizes essentially completely in water under ordinary dilute conditions. A weak acid ionizes only partially, establishing an equilibrium between molecules and ions. Strength describes the extent of ionization, not how many moles of acid are present per liter.

A concentrated solution contains a relatively large amount of solute per unit volume; a dilute solution contains less. A dilute strong acid can have a lower concentration than a concentrated weak acid. Do not use “strong” and “concentrated” interchangeably.

For a strong monoprotic acid such as HCl, the solution is modeled as essentially complete ionization: HCl + H₂O → H₃O⁺ + Cl⁻. For a weak acid such as acetic acid, use an equilibrium arrow: CH₃COOH + H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + CH₃COO⁻.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="3. Neutralization and net ionic equations" description="Strong acid–strong base reactions form water">

A typical strong acid–strong base neutralization combines acidic and basic species to form water. For HCl and NaOH:

Molecular equation: HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l)

Complete ionic equation: H⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) + Na⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → Na⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) + H₂O(l)

Cancel Na⁺ and Cl⁻. The net ionic equation is **H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l)**. A more explicit aqueous representation is H₃O⁺ + OH⁻ → 2H₂O.

The molecular equation shows all substances and state symbols; the net ionic equation shows the actual reacting species. The spectator ions remain dissolved before and after reaction.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: neutralization with a weak acid">
Acetic acid reacts with sodium hydroxide: CH₃COOH(aq) + NaOH(aq) → CH₃COONa(aq) + H₂O(l).

Sodium hydroxide and sodium acetate are strong electrolytes, but acetic acid is weak and remains mostly molecular in the ionic equation. Cancel Na⁺ to obtain **CH₃COOH(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → CH₃COO⁻(aq) + H₂O(l)**.

The hydroxide accepts a proton from acetic acid. Do not write CH₃COOH as fully separated H⁺ and acetate before the reaction.
</WorkedExample>

<LessonSection title="4. Connect acid–base chemistry to mole ratios" description="Neutralization does not always occur in a 1:1 ratio">

The balanced equation determines how much acid reacts with how much base. A monoprotic acid such as HCl reacts 1:1 with NaOH. Sulfuric acid, H₂SO₄, can donate two protons overall in a complete neutralization and reacts with two moles of NaOH per mole of H₂SO₄:

**H₂SO₄ + 2NaOH → Na₂SO₄ + 2H₂O**

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: amount of base required">
How many moles of NaOH are required to neutralize 0.0150 mol H₂SO₄ completely?

The equation gives 2 mol NaOH per 1 mol H₂SO₄.

0.0150 mol H₂SO₄ × (2 mol NaOH / 1 mol H₂SO₄) = **0.0300 mol NaOH**.
</WorkedExample>

A strong response explains why the coefficient ratio applies, rather than relying on a memorized shortcut. When a prompt gives volumes and molarities, calculate moles first, use the balanced equation, and then solve for the unknown.
