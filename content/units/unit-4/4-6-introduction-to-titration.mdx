A titration determines an unknown concentration by reacting a measured sample with a solution whose concentration is known. The key is not to assume that equal volumes or equal molarities react in a 1:1 ratio. The balanced equation determines the mole relationship at the equivalence point.

<InteractiveLab kind="titration" title="Change titrant volume and concentration and follow the titration curve" />

<LessonSection title="1. What the titration apparatus measures" description="Known titrant added to a measured analyte sample" defaultOpen>

The **analyte** is the sample whose concentration is unknown. The **titrant** is the solution of known concentration delivered from a buret. The volume delivered is the final buret reading minus the initial reading. The analyte volume is usually measured with a pipet and placed in a flask.

As titrant is added, it reacts with the analyte. The **equivalence point** is reached when the reactants have been added in the stoichiometric proportions specified by the balanced equation. An indicator or a pH meter helps identify the endpoint experimentally; the endpoint is the observed signal used to estimate the equivalence point and may not be exactly identical.

At the equivalence point, the equation's mole ratio is satisfied. It is only for a 1:1 reaction that the moles of titrant equal the moles of analyte.

<LessonSection title="2. Derive the concentration calculation" description="Molarity × volume gives moles">

Molarity is concentration in moles per liter:

**M = n / V**, so **n = M × V**

Use volume in liters when calculating moles. For a general reaction **aA + bB → products**, the equivalence condition is:

**n(A) / a = n(B) / b**

Equivalently, the moles of B needed are n(A) × (b/a). If the unknown analyte is A and the known titrant is B, solve for the unknown concentration after finding the required moles.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: 1:1 acid–base titration">
A 25.00 mL sample of HCl is titrated with 0.1000 M NaOH. The equivalence point occurs after 18.60 mL NaOH is delivered. Find the HCl concentration.

Balanced reaction: HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O. The mole ratio is 1:1.

Moles NaOH = 0.1000 mol/L × 0.01860 L = 0.001860 mol.

Therefore, moles HCl = 0.001860 mol. The analyte volume is 0.02500 L.

[HCl] = 0.001860 mol / 0.02500 L = **0.07440 M**.

The HCl concentration is lower than the NaOH concentration because the 25.00 mL acid sample contains fewer moles than 25.00 mL of 0.1000 M base would; the measured equivalence volume confirms the calculated concentration.
</WorkedExample>

<LessonSection title="3. When the stoichiometric ratio is not 1:1" description="Never use M₁V₁ = M₂V₂ blindly">

For sulfuric acid neutralized by sodium hydroxide:

**H₂SO₄ + 2NaOH → Na₂SO₄ + 2H₂O**

One mole of H₂SO₄ reacts with two moles of NaOH. At equivalence, n(NaOH) = 2n(H₂SO₄). The common shortcut M₁V₁ = M₂V₂ works only when the balanced mole ratio is 1:1; otherwise, include the coefficients explicitly.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: diprotic acid">
A 10.00 mL H₂SO₄ sample is neutralized by 24.00 mL of 0.1500 M NaOH.

Moles NaOH = 0.02400 L × 0.1500 mol/L = 0.003600 mol.

From the 2:1 ratio, moles H₂SO₄ = 0.003600 / 2 = 0.001800 mol.

[H₂SO₄] = 0.001800 mol / 0.01000 L = **0.1800 M**.
</WorkedExample>

<LessonSection title="4. Experimental quality and common errors" description="Volume readings, indicators, and assumptions">

Read a buret at eye level and record both initial and final readings with the instrument's precision. Delivered volume is their difference. Convert milliliters to liters before using M × V, and keep track of which volume belongs to the analyte and which to the titrant.

An indicator changes color over a pH range. Choosing an appropriate indicator means its transition range should be near the steep pH change around the equivalence point. In a quantitative problem, use the stated equivalence volume or titration data rather than guessing from an indicator color alone.

Frequent errors:
- using total buret reading instead of delivered volume;
- treating mL as L in M × V;
- assuming a 1:1 mole ratio without balancing the reaction;
- using the titrant volume as the analyte volume;
- confusing the endpoint signal with the theoretical equivalence condition.

For a laboratory reference, see [Chemistry LibreTexts: Acid–Base Titrations](https://chem.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/General_Chemistry).

</LessonSection>
