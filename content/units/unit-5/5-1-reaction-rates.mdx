Chemical kinetics asks how fast reactions occur and what controls that speed. Thermodynamics can indicate whether a process is energetically favorable, but it does not by itself tell you whether the process happens in a millisecond or over years. Rate data connect observable concentration changes to the particle-level events that produce them.

<TopicLab unitId={5} topicNumber="5.1" title="Reaction Rates" />

<LessonSection title="1. Define rate from concentration change" description="Track how quickly reactants disappear or products form" defaultOpen>

A reaction rate describes concentration change per unit time. For a product P, an average formation rate over an interval is:

**rate of formation of P = Δ[P] / Δt**

For a reactant A, concentration decreases, so Δ[A] is negative. The rate of disappearance is usually written as **−Δ[A] / Δt** so the reported rate is positive. Concentration is commonly measured in mol·L⁻¹ (M), and time may be seconds, giving rate units of M·s⁻¹.

An average rate applies over a finite interval. An instantaneous rate is the slope of the tangent to the concentration-versus-time curve at one moment. As a reaction proceeds, reactant concentrations often decrease, collision frequency changes, and the rate changes.

<LessonSection title="2. Relate reactant and product rates with coefficients" description="Different species can change at different numerical rates">

For **2N₂O₅ → 4NO₂ + O₂**, four moles of NO₂ form for every one mole of O₂ formed. Their concentration formation rates therefore differ by a factor of four if measured over the same interval.

A stoichiometrically normalized reaction rate accounts for the coefficients. For aA → bB, the reaction rate is **−(1/a) Δ[A]/Δt = +(1/b) Δ[B]/Δt**. This means the normalized rate is consistent regardless of which species is monitored. When a question asks only for the disappearance rate of a particular reactant or the formation rate of a particular product, do not divide by the coefficient unless it asks for the normalized reaction rate.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: compare species rates">
For 2NO₂(g) → 2NO(g) + O₂(g), suppose O₂ forms at 0.015 M·s⁻¹ over an interval.

The equation produces 2 mol NO for each 1 mol O₂, so NO forms at twice the rate: 0.030 M·s⁻¹. Two mol NO₂ disappear for each mol O₂ formed, so NO₂ disappears at 0.030 M·s⁻¹.

The normalized reaction rate is −(1/2)Δ[NO₂]/Δt = +(1/2)Δ[NO]/Δt = Δ[O₂]/Δt = 0.015 M·s⁻¹.
</WorkedExample>

<LessonSection title="3. Read concentration–time graphs" description="Slope represents rate">

On a concentration-versus-time graph, the slope is Δconcentration/Δtime. A steep slope means a large rate magnitude; a flatter slope means a smaller rate magnitude. A reactant curve usually slopes downward, while a product curve usually slopes upward. If the slope approaches zero, the concentration is changing very slowly.

Do not confuse the height of a curve with its slope. A high product concentration does not necessarily mean the product is forming quickly at that moment. The rate depends on the slope, not the concentration value alone.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: interpret a graph in words">
Imagine a reactant concentration curve that falls steeply at first and then levels off. The reaction is fastest early in the interval and slows as time passes. In a typical closed system, the reactant is being consumed and its concentration is falling, so there are fewer reactant particles available for productive collisions.
</WorkedExample>

<LessonSection title="4. Connect macroscopic rate to particles" description="Collisions are necessary, but not every collision reacts">

Reactant particles must encounter one another for many reactions to occur. A collision can lead to products only if the particles have sufficient energy and an appropriate orientation. Increasing concentration often increases the frequency of collisions, but the rate also depends on the activation-energy barrier and the molecular details of the reaction.

Rate data alone do not reveal every elementary step. They describe how quickly concentrations change under measured conditions. To determine the rate law or mechanism, chemists combine rate measurements with models and additional evidence.

For a foundation in rate measurements, see [OpenStax Chemistry 2e: Factors Affecting Reaction Rates](https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/12-2-factors-affecting-reaction-rates).

<LessonSection title="5. Distinguish average rate from instantaneous rate" description="A curved graph does not have one rate at every time">

The **average rate** over an interval is the slope of the secant line joining two points. The **instantaneous rate** at a particular time is the slope of the tangent line at that point. On a curved reactant-concentration graph, the tangent is usually steeper early in the reaction and flatter later, so the instantaneous rate changes continuously.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: estimate a tangent rate">

A reactant concentration is 0.62 M at 4.0 s and 0.54 M at 6.0 s. A short-interval estimate of the disappearance rate near 5.0 s is `−Δ[A]/Δt = −(0.54 − 0.62)/(6.0 − 4.0) = 0.040 M·s⁻¹`.

Across the wider interval from 0.0 to 10.0 s, the concentration falls from 0.80 M to 0.50 M, so the average disappearance rate is 0.030 M·s⁻¹. These values are not contradictory: the rate near 5 s can exceed the average over an interval that also includes slower later times.

</WorkedExample>

<LessonSection title="6. Convert a measured concentration change into a stoichiometric rate" description="The reaction rate is normalized by the coefficient in the balanced equation">

For a reaction `aA → bB`, the normalized reaction rate is `rate = −(1/a)Δ[A]/Δt = (1/b)Δ[B]/Δt`. The minus sign makes the disappearance rate of a reactant positive. The raw concentration slopes for A and B need not have the same magnitude; their relationship is fixed by stoichiometry.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: use a balanced equation">

For `2N₂O₅(g) → 4NO₂(g) + O₂(g)`, suppose [N₂O₅] decreases by 0.020 M over 10.0 s. Its disappearance rate is 0.0020 M·s⁻¹, but the normalized reaction rate is half that value because the coefficient of N₂O₅ is 2: `rate = −(1/2)Δ[N₂O₅]/Δt = 0.0010 M·s⁻¹`. The corresponding O₂ formation rate is one times the reaction rate, 0.0010 M·s⁻¹, while NO₂ forms four times as fast, 0.0040 M·s⁻¹.

</WorkedExample>
