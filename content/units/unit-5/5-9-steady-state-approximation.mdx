A short-lived intermediate can be produced and consumed continuously while remaining at a low, nearly constant concentration during much of a reaction. The **steady-state approximation** models this situation by assuming that the rate of formation of the intermediate is approximately equal to its rate of consumption after an initial transient.

<TopicLab unitId={5} topicNumber="5.9" title="Steady-State Approximation" />

<LessonSection title="1. What steady state means" description="Low concentration does not mean no reaction" defaultOpen>

For an intermediate X, the steady-state approximation sets **d[X]/dt ≈ 0**. In words, the concentration of X changes very little over the interval being modeled because X is being formed and consumed at nearly the same rate.

This does not mean that X is absent, that its concentration is exactly zero, or that the reaction has reached chemical equilibrium. At steady state, there can be a continuous flow of material through X even though [X] is nearly constant. Equilibrium is a different condition involving forward and reverse processes balancing for a reversible reaction.

X can still be produced and consumed continuously." />

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="2. Set up the intermediate balance" description="Write formation minus consumption">

Suppose a mechanism contains:
1. A → X with rate k₁[A]
2. X → P with rate k₂[X]

If X is produced only by step 1 and consumed only by step 2, then:

**d[X]/dt = k₁[A] − k₂[X]**

Under the steady-state approximation, set this expression approximately equal to zero:

0 ≈ k₁[A] − k₂[X]

Rearrange to obtain **[X] ≈ k₁[A]/k₂**. The product formation rate is rate = k₂[X], so substituting gives rate ≈ k₂(k₁[A]/k₂) = **k₁[A]**.

This simplified example illustrates the method. In a more complex mechanism, multiple steps may produce or consume X, so every relevant contribution must appear in the balance.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: write the steady-state equation">
Suppose X is formed by A → X at rate k₁[A] and consumed by X + B → products at rate k₂[X][B].

Formation rate = k₁[A]. Consumption rate = k₂[X][B].

Steady state: 0 ≈ k₁[A] − k₂[X][B], so **[X] ≈ k₁[A]/(k₂[B])**. The approximation depends on the mechanism and the assumption that the concentration of X is nearly constant.
</WorkedExample>

<LessonSection title="3. Know the limitation" description="An approximation that must be justified">

The steady-state approximation is useful when an intermediate reaches a low, slowly changing concentration relative to the timescale of the overall reaction. It is not automatically valid for every mechanism or at every instant. In particular, the earliest moments after mixing may include a transient while intermediate concentrations adjust.

AP-level questions may provide the steady-state assumption or ask what it means rather than requiring a complicated derivation. If the prompt does not justify a steady-state or pre-equilibrium assumption, do not introduce it as if it were guaranteed.

<LessonSection title="4. Apply the steady-state idea carefully" description="An intermediate can remain at low, nearly constant concentration while still reacting">

The steady-state approximation says that after a brief initial period, the rate of formation of an intermediate approximately equals its total rate of consumption, so \(d[I]/dt\approx0\). It does **not** mean [I] is zero, and it does not mean the intermediate is at equilibrium. Steady state and pre-equilibrium are different assumptions.

If I forms at rate \(k_1[A]\) and disappears through pathways at rates \(k_{-1}[I][B]\) and \(k_2[I][C]\), the steady-state condition is \(k_1[A]-k_{-1}[I][B]-k_2[I][C]\approx0\). Rearranging gives \([I]\approx\frac{k_1[A]}{k_{-1}[B]+k_2[C]}\). Substitute this into the product-formation rate to remove I from the final law.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: balance formation and consumption">
If I forms at 0.12 M·s⁻¹ and is consumed through two pathways at 0.07 and 0.05 M·s⁻¹, its net rate of change is approximately 0.12 − 0.07 − 0.05 = 0.00 M·s⁻¹. I is still being formed and consumed; its concentration is simply not building up.

</WorkedExample>

Use the approximation only when the mechanism and data justify it. If the intermediate accumulates substantially or the initial transient matters, assuming \(d[I]/dt\approx0\) may fail.
