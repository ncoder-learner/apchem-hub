A balanced overall equation tells you the net chemical transformation, but many reactions occur through a sequence of molecular events. An **elementary reaction** is one individual step in a proposed mechanism. Because it describes a single molecular event, its molecularity can be used to write its rate law directly.

<TopicLab unitId={5} topicNumber="5.4" title="Elementary Reactions" />

<LessonSection title="1. What makes a step elementary?" description="A single molecular event, not an overall summary" defaultOpen>

A mechanism is a sequence of elementary steps that, when added, gives the overall reaction. Each elementary step represents a proposed event at the particle level. An elementary step may be unimolecular (one reactant particle), bimolecular (two reactant particles collide), or, rarely, termolecular (three particles participate in one event).

For an elementary step **A → products**, the step is unimolecular and its rate law is first order in A: rate = k[A]. For **A + B → products**, a bimolecular elementary step has rate = k[A][B]. For **2A → products**, the step's rate law is rate = k[A]².

This direct connection applies to an elementary step, not automatically to a net equation formed by adding several steps. Mechanisms involving intermediates can produce overall rate laws with exponents that differ from the overall stoichiometric coefficients.

<QuickCheck question="For the elementary step A + B → products, what rate law follows from its molecularity?" options={["rate = k[A]","rate = k[B]²","rate = k[A][B]","rate = k[A + B]² in all cases"]} correctIndex={2} explanation="A and B participate together in one bimolecular elementary step, so the rate is proportional to the concentration of each reactant: k[A][B]." />

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="2. Molecularity is not the same as reaction order" description="Molecularity applies to a step; order comes from a rate law">

Molecularity is the number of reactant particles participating in one elementary step. It is always a positive integer for the stated step. Reaction order is the sum of exponents in a rate law and is established from the rate expression. For an elementary step, order follows from its molecularity; for an overall reaction, order is determined experimentally.

A reaction with overall equation A + B → products could proceed through multiple steps, so you cannot assume its rate law is k[A][B] just because A and B have coefficient 1. The mechanism could include a slow unimolecular rearrangement after a rapid equilibrium, or a different rate-determining event.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: compare an overall equation and an elementary step">
The overall equation is 2NO₂ + F₂ → 2NO₂F. If the prompt explicitly states this transformation occurs in one elementary step, the rate law for that step is rate = k[NO₂]²[F₂].

If the equation is only the overall reaction and no mechanism is provided, the exponents cannot be inferred from the overall coefficients alone. Initial-rate data or a justified mechanism is needed.
</WorkedExample>

<LessonSection title="3. Elementary steps and intermediates" description="Mechanisms must add to the observed overall reaction">

An **intermediate** is produced in one step and consumed in a later step, so it does not appear in the net overall equation. To obtain the overall reaction, add the elementary steps and cancel intermediates that appear on both sides.

For example:
1. NO₂ + NO₂ → NO₃ + NO (slow)
2. NO₃ + CO → NO₂ + CO₂ (fast)

Add the steps. NO₃ appears as a product and reactant, so it cancels. One NO₂ also cancels from both sides. The net equation is **NO₂ + CO → NO + CO₂**. NO₃ is an intermediate. The labels “slow” and “fast” are part of the proposed mechanism and must be supported by kinetics, not guessed from the balanced overall equation.

It cancels algebraically from the sum of the steps, while its atoms are still conserved in each step." />

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="4. Read an elementary step at the particle level" description="Molecularity belongs to a single step, not the overall equation">

An elementary reaction represents one modeled molecular event. Its molecularity is the number of reacting particles in that event: unimolecular (one), bimolecular (two), or, rarely, termolecular (three). For an elementary step, the reactant coefficients give the rate-law exponents because they describe the collision event itself. For example, A + B→products has rate k[A][B], while 2A→products has rate k[A]^2.

This shortcut does not apply to an overall balanced equation assembled from multiple steps. Overall coefficients express net stoichiometry, not necessarily the molecular event that controls the rate.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: overall versus elementary">
For 2NO₂ + F₂→2NO₂F, the single-step hypothesis predicts k[NO₂]^2[F₂]. If experiments instead show k[NO₂][F₂], the net equation alone cannot be used to infer the law; a multistep mechanism or another kinetic model is needed.

</WorkedExample>

A termolecular event is statistically less likely than a bimolecular event because three particles must meet with suitable orientation and sufficient energy at the same time. This makes a proposed mechanism worth scrutinizing, but low plausibility alone does not prove it impossible.

<QuickCheck question="For the elementary step A + 2B → C, what rate law follows for that step?" options={["k[A][B]","k[A][B]²","k[A]²[B]","k[C]"]} correctIndex={1} explanation="Because this is explicitly an elementary step, its two B reactants and one A reactant give rate = k[A][B]²." />

</LessonSection>


<LessonSection title="5. Use elementary steps to test a proposed mechanism" description="Molecularity belongs to a single step, and intermediates cancel when steps are added">

An elementary step describes one molecular event in the proposed pathway. Its molecularity is the number of reacting particles in that step, and the elementary-step rate law follows those reactant coefficients. A mechanism is plausible only if its steps add to the correct net equation and its predicted rate law agrees with experiment.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: cancel an intermediate">

Consider these proposed steps:

1. `2NO₂(g) → NO₃(g) + NO(g)` (slow)
2. `NO₃(g) + CO(g) → NO₂(g) + CO₂(g)` (fast)

Adding the steps and canceling NO₃, which is formed in step 1 and consumed in step 2, also cancels one NO₂ appearing on both sides. The net equation is `NO₂(g) + CO(g) → NO(g) + CO₂(g)`. Because the slow step is elementary and contains two NO₂ molecules, it gives the candidate rate law `rate = k[NO₂]²`. That prediction must still be checked against measured kinetics.

</WorkedExample>

Do not treat every species in a mechanism as a reactant or product in the net equation. An intermediate is formed in one step and consumed in another; it cancels. A catalyst is consumed in an early step and regenerated later, so it also cancels from the net equation but is present at the beginning of the pathway.

</LessonSection>
