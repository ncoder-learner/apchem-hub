A balanced overall equation tells you the net chemical transformation, but many reactions occur through a sequence of molecular events. An **elementary reaction** is one individual step in a proposed mechanism. Because it describes a single molecular event, its molecularity can be used to write its rate law directly.

<TopicLab unitId={5} topicNumber="5.4" title="Elementary Reactions" />

<LessonSection title="1. What makes a step elementary?" description="A single molecular event, not an overall summary" defaultOpen>

A mechanism is a sequence of elementary steps that, when added, gives the overall reaction. Each elementary step represents a proposed event at the particle level. An elementary step may be unimolecular (one reactant particle), bimolecular (two reactant particles collide), or, rarely, termolecular (three particles participate in one event).

For an elementary step **A → products**, the step is unimolecular and its rate law is first order in A: rate = k[A]. For **A + B → products**, a bimolecular elementary step has rate = k[A][B]. For **2A → products**, the step's rate law is rate = k[A]².

This direct connection applies to an elementary step, not automatically to a net equation formed by adding several steps. Mechanisms involving intermediates can produce overall rate laws with exponents that differ from the overall stoichiometric coefficients.

<LessonSection title="2. Molecularity is not the same as reaction order" description="Molecularity applies to a step; order comes from a rate law">

Molecularity is the number of reactant particles participating in one elementary step. It is always a positive integer for the stated step. Reaction order is the sum of exponents in a rate law and is established from the rate expression. For an elementary step, order follows from its molecularity; for an overall reaction, order is determined experimentally.

A reaction with overall equation A + B → products could proceed through multiple steps, so you cannot assume its rate law is k[A][B] just because A and B have coefficient 1. The mechanism could include a slow unimolecular rearrangement after a rapid equilibrium, or a different rate-determining event.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: compare an overall equation and an elementary step">
The overall equation is 2NO₂ + F₂ → 2NO₂F. If the prompt explicitly states this transformation occurs in one elementary step, the rate law for that step is rate = k[NO₂]²[F₂].

If the equation is only the overall reaction and no mechanism is provided, the exponents cannot be inferred from the overall coefficients alone. Initial-rate data or a justified mechanism is needed.
</WorkedExample>

<LessonSection title="3. Elementary steps and intermediates" description="Mechanisms must add to the observed overall reaction">

An **intermediate** is produced in one step and consumed in a later step, so it does not appear in the net overall equation. To obtain the overall reaction, add the elementary steps and cancel intermediates that appear on both sides.

For example:
1. NO₂ + NO₂ → NO₃ + NO (slow)
2. NO₃ + CO → NO₂ + CO₂ (fast)

Add the steps. NO₃ appears as a product and reactant, so it cancels. One NO₂ also cancels from both sides. The net equation is **NO₂ + CO → NO + CO₂**. NO₃ is an intermediate. The labels “slow” and “fast” are part of the proposed mechanism and must be supported by kinetics, not guessed from the balanced overall equation.
