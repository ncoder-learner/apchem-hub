Collision theory explains why particles can be present together without reacting every time they meet. For a collision to produce reaction, the particles must collide with sufficient energy to overcome the activation-energy barrier and with an orientation that allows the necessary bonds to break and form.

<TopicLab unitId={5} topicNumber="5.5" title="Collision Model" />

<LessonSection title="1. Effective collisions" description="Not every collision leads to products" defaultOpen>

A gas contains particles moving in many directions and with a range of kinetic energies. Collisions occur frequently, but only a fraction are effective. In the collision model, an effective collision has enough energy and a suitable orientation to reach a configuration from which products can form.

**Activation energy, Eₐ**, is the energy barrier that must be overcome along the reaction pathway. It is not the total energy released by the reaction and not the energy stored in the products. A reaction with a high activation barrier may be slow even if its products are thermodynamically favorable.

<LessonSection title="2. Explain common rate factors" description="Each factor changes a different part of the collision picture">

**Higher concentration** usually increases collision frequency because more reactant particles occupy the same volume. For gases, increasing pressure by compressing the gas can have a similar effect if temperature and composition are otherwise held constant.

**Higher temperature** increases average kinetic energy and changes the distribution of particle energies. Particles move faster and, crucially, a larger fraction have energy at or above the activation barrier. The rate often increases substantially even when the average kinetic energy rises by a more modest fraction.

**Greater surface area** for a solid reactant exposes more particles to contact with other reactants. Powdered calcium carbonate typically reacts faster with acid than the same mass in one large chunk, because more solid surface is available for collisions.

**A catalyst** provides an alternative pathway with a lower activation-energy barrier. It increases the fraction of collisions that can be effective without being consumed overall. A catalyst does not change the reaction's overall ΔG or the equilibrium constant at a fixed temperature.

<LessonSection title="3. Use evidence to distinguish rate factors" description="Predict what changes and what stays the same">

If concentration increases while temperature stays fixed, the frequency of collisions generally increases, but the energy distribution is not changed by temperature. If temperature increases, both collision frequency and the fraction of high-energy particles can increase. If a catalyst is added, the pathway and activation barrier change; the catalyst is not simply “adding energy” to every particle.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: powdered vs. chunky solid">
Equal masses of CaCO₃ are placed in excess hydrochloric acid, one as a powder and one as a single chunk. If all else is controlled, the powder reacts faster because it exposes more surface area to the acid. The amount of CaCO₃ is the same, so the total theoretical amount of CO₂ is the same if both samples react completely; the rate differs, not the stoichiometric yield.
</WorkedExample>

<LessonSection title="4. Explain rate changes using successful collisions" description="Separate collision frequency, orientation, and energy">

Collision theory predicts that reaction rate depends on how often particles collide and what fraction of collisions are successful. A collision must have sufficient energy to overcome the activation barrier and an orientation that allows the relevant bonds to break and form. More collisions do not guarantee a proportional rate increase if the fraction of successful collisions changes as well.

- **Higher concentration or gas pressure at fixed temperature:** more particles per volume usually increases collision frequency.
- **Higher temperature:** particles move faster and, more importantly, a larger fraction have energy at or above the activation energy.
- **Greater surface area for a solid reactant:** more particles are exposed for collisions.
- **Catalyst:** changes the pathway and lowers the relevant activation barrier; it does not simply make particles move faster.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: why temperature has a strong effect">
A student says heating a reaction by 10°C must increase the rate by a fixed percentage for every reaction. This is not a general rule. The change depends on the activation energy and temperature range because the fraction of particles with enough energy changes nonlinearly. Use measured data or an Arrhenius relationship when a numerical factor is requested.

</WorkedExample>

An energy-distribution model does not mean every molecule has the same kinetic energy. At a given temperature, particles have a distribution of energies. Increasing temperature shifts and broadens that distribution, increasing the fraction beyond the activation threshold.
