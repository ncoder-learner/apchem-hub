A proposed reaction mechanism makes predictions about rate. The challenge is that an elementary step may contain a species that is not present in the overall equation. A valid derivation must express the predicted rate in terms of quantities consistent with the experiment, not simply leave an unmeasured intermediate in the final rate law.

<TopicLab unitId={5} topicNumber="5.8" title="Reaction Mechanism and Rate Law" />

<LessonSection title="1. Begin with the elementary step that controls rate" description="Use the stated slow step, but check its assumptions" defaultOpen>

In introductory problems, the **rate-determining step** is the slow step that limits the overall rate. If that step is elementary and contains only reactants whose concentrations are known, its molecularity gives a rate law for that step.

For example:
- Slow elementary step: A + B → X. A simple model gives rate = k[A][B].
- Slow elementary step: 2A → X. A simple model gives rate = k[A]².

However, if the slow step includes an intermediate X, the rate expression may contain [X]. To compare the mechanism with experimental rate data, X must be related to the measurable reactant concentrations using justified information about preceding or following steps. This is why a mechanism cannot be judged solely by the slow-step label.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="2. Why intermediates complicate rate laws" description="The final rate law should match measurable species">

Consider a mechanism with a fast pre-equilibrium **A + B ⇌ X**, followed by a slow step **X + C → products**. The slow-step expression is rate = k₂[X][C]. But X is an intermediate, so a useful overall rate law should eliminate [X].

If the first step is at equilibrium, its equilibrium expression can be used to relate [X] to [A] and [B]: K = [X]/([A][B]), so [X] = K[A][B]. Substitution gives rate = k₂K[A][B][C]. This conclusion depends on the stated fast-equilibrium assumption; it is not a universal shortcut for all mechanisms.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: remove an intermediate using a fast equilibrium">
Suppose A + B ⇌ X is fast and at equilibrium, and X + C → products is slow. The slow step gives rate = k₂[X][C]. From the first-step equilibrium, [X] = K[A][B]. Substitute: rate = k₂K[A][B][C]. Combining constants gives **rate = kobs[A][B][C]**, where kobs = k₂K under the stated conditions.
</WorkedExample>

<LessonSection title="3. Test consistency rather than guessing" description="A mechanism must satisfy multiple constraints">

Check a proposed mechanism in this order:
1. Add elementary steps and cancel intermediates.
2. Confirm the sum matches the balanced overall reaction.
3. Write rate expressions only for elementary steps as justified.
4. Eliminate intermediates if needed using the assumptions provided.
5. Compare the predicted rate law with experimental evidence.

If a mechanism predicts a different dependence on concentration than the measured rate law, the mechanism is inconsistent with the data even if the steps sum correctly. Conversely, matching one rate law does not prove a unique mechanism; multiple mechanisms can sometimes produce the same observed rate expression.

For further reference, see [OpenStax Chemistry 2e: Rate Laws](https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/12-3-rate-laws).

<LessonSection title="4. Connect a mechanism to the observed rate law" description="Do not leave an intermediate in the final prediction">

If the slow elementary step contains only stable reactants, its molecularity can often give a direct candidate rate law. If it contains an intermediate, the first expression may not be experimentally useful. Eliminate that intermediate using a justified relationship, such as a fast pre-equilibrium or the steady-state approximation when appropriate.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: use a fast pre-equilibrium">
Consider A + B⇌ I as a fast pre-equilibrium followed by slow I + C→ P. The slow step gives rate = k₂[I][C]. For the fast equilibrium, K = [I]/([A][B]), so [I] = K[A][B]. Substitution yields rate = k_2K[A][B][C]. This derivation depends on the pre-equilibrium assumption; it is not a universal rule for every mechanism.

</WorkedExample>

The net equation and rate law answer different questions. The net equation tracks what is consumed and formed; the rate law describes how measured speed responds to concentrations. A strong mechanism explanation accounts for both.

<LessonSection title="5. Reject mechanisms that fail the experimental rate law" description="Stoichiometric cancellation and kinetic agreement are separate tests">

A mechanism must pass two independent checks. First, adding the elementary steps must recover the observed net reaction. Second, the steps must predict a rate law consistent with experiments. A correct net equation does not rescue a mechanism whose predicted concentration dependence is wrong.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: a mechanism that balances but fails kinetics">

Suppose a proposed slow elementary step is `A + B → I`, followed by a fast step that converts I to products. The slow step predicts `rate = k[A][B]` if its reactants are stable species. But experiments show that doubling [A] quadruples the rate while holding [B] constant. That observation supports second-order dependence on A, not first-order dependence. Unless an additional justified pre-equilibrium or intermediate relationship changes the derived law, the proposed mechanism is inconsistent with the data.

</WorkedExample>

Do not force a mechanism to fit by inserting an arbitrary exponent. If a slow step contains an intermediate, derive or use a justified relationship for that intermediate, then compare the resulting law with experiment. A mechanism is a model constrained by evidence, not a story inferred from the balanced equation alone.

## Readiness check

You should be able to derive a rate law from an explicitly stated elementary slow step, eliminate intermediates using a supplied equilibrium relationship, and evaluate a mechanism against both net stoichiometry and experimental kinetics.
