A reaction mechanism is a proposed sequence of elementary steps that explains how reactants become products. A mechanism must be consistent with the overall balanced equation and the experimental rate law. It is a model that can be tested, not a story accepted simply because the steps add up.

<TopicLab unitId={5} topicNumber="5.7" title="Introduction to Reaction Mechanisms" />

<LessonSection title="1. Add elementary steps to recover the net equation" description="Cancel intermediates but conserve atoms at every step" defaultOpen>

When you add elementary steps, treat the equations like algebra. A species that appears as a product in one step and a reactant in another may cancel from the sum. Such a species is an **intermediate**: it is produced and then consumed, so it does not appear in the net reaction.

Example:
1. NO₂ + NO₂ → NO₃ + NO
2. NO₃ + CO → NO₂ + CO₂

Adding the equations and canceling NO₃ and one NO₂ gives **NO₂ + CO → NO + CO₂**. The atoms balance in every elementary step and in the overall reaction.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="2. Identify a catalyst in the mechanism" description="A catalyst is used in a step and regenerated later">

A **catalyst** participates in the mechanism but is regenerated, so it is not consumed overall. It may be consumed in an early step and regenerated in a later one. This differs from an intermediate, which is created in a step and then consumed.

Example:
1. X + A → XA
2. XA + B → X + products

X is consumed in step 1 and regenerated in step 2, so it is a catalyst. XA is formed in step 1 and consumed in step 2, so it is an intermediate.

The catalyst changes the pathway and can lower the activation barrier, while the intermediate is part of the pathway itself. Neither needs to appear in the overall equation after cancellation.

XA is an intermediate." />

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="3. Mechanisms need evidence" description="A plausible sum is necessary but not sufficient">

For a mechanism to be acceptable, its elementary steps must sum to the observed overall equation and its predicted rate behavior must agree with experiment. The slow step is often described as rate-determining in introductory models, but deriving a rate law for a multistep mechanism can require accounting for fast equilibria or intermediates. Do not infer a mechanism from the net equation alone.

If the proposed first step is slow and elementary, its rate law may determine the observed rate expression directly. If a slow step contains an intermediate, its concentration may need to be related to measurable reactant concentrations before the proposed law can be compared with data.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: test a mechanism's net equation">
Suppose the proposed steps are A + B → X and X + B → C. Add the steps and cancel X to obtain A + 2B → C. The proposed mechanism is consistent with that net equation only if the experimentally established overall equation is A + 2B → C. Even then, the mechanism is not proven; the rate law and other evidence must also agree.
</WorkedExample>

<LessonSection title="4. Test whether a proposed mechanism is chemically consistent" description="The steps must reproduce the net reaction and fit the kinetics">

A mechanism is a sequence of elementary steps proposed to explain the observed reaction. To test it, add the elementary equations and cancel species that appear on both sides. The remaining equation must match the overall balanced reaction. A species formed in one step and consumed in a later step is an **intermediate**; a species consumed early and regenerated later is a **catalyst**.

The slow step often limits the rate in an introductory mechanism analysis, but a mechanism must also be consistent with the experimentally determined rate law. If the slow elementary step includes an intermediate, its concentration may need to be related to reactant concentrations through a preceding fast equilibrium or another justified approximation.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: cancel the intermediate">
Step 1: \(A+B\rightleftharpoons I\). Step 2: \(I+C\rightarrow P\). Adding the steps cancels I and gives \(A+B+C\rightarrow P\). I is an intermediate because it is produced and consumed. This algebra alone does not prove the mechanism is correct; compare its predicted rate law with experimental data.

</WorkedExample>

Do not call a species a catalyst merely because it appears in a mechanism. It must be regenerated and not consumed overall. Likewise, a proposed sequence can sum correctly but still fail if it predicts the wrong rate dependence.

<LessonSection title="4. Test whether a proposed mechanism is consistent" description="The steps must reproduce the net reaction and fit the kinetics">

Add the elementary equations and cancel species that appear on both sides. The remaining equation must match the overall balanced reaction. A species formed in one step and consumed later is an **intermediate**; a species consumed and then regenerated is a **catalyst**. A mechanism that sums correctly is not automatically correct: it must also agree with the experimentally measured rate law.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: identify the intermediate">
Step 1: \(A+B\rightleftharpoons I\). Step 2: \(I+C\rightarrow P\). Adding the steps cancels I and gives \(A+B+C\rightarrow P\). I is an intermediate because it is produced and consumed. The equations alone do not prove the mechanism; its kinetic prediction must be tested against data.

</WorkedExample>

The slow step often controls the rate in simplified problems. If it contains an intermediate, the intermediate concentration must be related to stable reactant concentrations through a justified pre-equilibrium or steady-state treatment. Do not call a species a catalyst just because it appears in multiple steps.
