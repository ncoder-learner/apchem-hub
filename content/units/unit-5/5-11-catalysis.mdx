A catalyst increases reaction rate by providing a mechanism with a lower kinetic barrier. It participates in one or more elementary steps but is regenerated overall. Catalysts matter in industrial chemistry and biological systems because they can make useful reactions proceed at practical rates under manageable conditions.

<LessonSection title="1. What a catalyst changes" description="A different pathway, not a different net reaction" defaultOpen>

A catalyst provides an alternative pathway with a lower activation-energy barrier. At the same temperature, a larger fraction of collisions can overcome the relevant barrier, so the reaction proceeds faster.

The catalyst is consumed in some elementary steps and regenerated in others, so it does not appear in the net overall equation. It does not change the overall reaction enthalpy, the Gibbs free-energy change between the same states, or the equilibrium constant at a fixed temperature. It speeds both forward and reverse reactions and helps the system reach equilibrium faster.

<LessonSection title="2. Homogeneous and heterogeneous catalysis" description="The catalyst and reactants may occupy the same or different phases">

In **homogeneous catalysis**, the catalyst and reactants are in the same phase, often all dissolved in one liquid mixture. The catalyst interacts with reactants in the same phase and is regenerated through the mechanism.

In **heterogeneous catalysis**, the catalyst is in a different phase from at least one reactant. A common example is a gas reacting at the surface of a solid catalyst. Reactant particles adsorb onto active surface sites, react through a pathway facilitated by the surface, and products desorb. The available surface area and condition of the catalyst can affect rate.

A catalyst's presence is not enough to identify the mechanism. Experimental evidence is needed to determine the detailed steps and active species.

<LessonSection title="3. Catalysts and energy profiles" description="Compare barriers while keeping the endpoints fixed">

On an energy profile, the catalyzed pathway has a lower activation barrier than the uncatalyzed pathway. It may use several steps and intermediates rather than one step, so the catalyzed curve can have multiple peaks. The overall reactants and products remain the same, so the endpoint energy difference is unchanged.

The phrase “lowers activation energy” refers to the pathway being followed, not to the catalyst subtracting energy from every reactant molecule. The catalyst makes a lower-barrier route available; thermal energy still supplies the distribution of particle energies.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: interpret catalyzed and uncatalyzed profiles">
An uncatalyzed pathway has a forward activation energy of 85 kJ/mol. A catalyzed pathway for the same overall reaction has a maximum relevant barrier of 45 kJ/mol. The catalyzed route has a barrier lower by 40 kJ/mol. If the initial and final states are the same, the overall ΔH is unchanged; the reaction can reach equilibrium more quickly.
</WorkedExample>

<LessonSection title="4. Explain catalysis without changing thermodynamics" description="A catalyst changes the pathway, not the endpoints">

A catalyst provides an alternative pathway with a lower effective activation barrier. It is regenerated by the mechanism and is not consumed in the net reaction, even though it may participate in individual elementary steps. Lower barriers generally increase the fraction of successful events per unit time at a given temperature.

A catalyst does not change reactant or product energies, so it does not change the net ΔH. It also does not change the equilibrium constant at fixed temperature. It speeds both forward and reverse processes, allowing equilibrium to be reached sooner, but the equilibrium composition remains the same when temperature and the system are otherwise unchanged.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: distinguish rate from equilibrium">
A reversible reaction has K = 4.0 at a fixed temperature. Adding a catalyst may change how quickly the system reaches equilibrium, but it does not make K equal 10.0. The equilibrium ratio remains the value determined by the reaction and temperature.

</WorkedExample>

A catalyst can be selective: different catalysts may favor different pathways or products in complex systems. At AP level, the core claim is that catalysis offers a lower-barrier pathway for the same net reaction.

<LessonSection title="5. Explain why a catalyst changes rate but not equilibrium" description="The catalyst changes the pathway, not the initial and final thermodynamic states">

A catalyst offers an alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation barrier. On an energy profile, the catalyzed pathway has lower transition-state energy relative to the corresponding starting states, while the reactant and product energy levels remain unchanged. Thus ΔH is unchanged, and at fixed temperature the equilibrium constant is unchanged.

For a reversible reaction, a catalyst accelerates both the forward and reverse processes. If the mixture begins away from equilibrium, it reaches equilibrium faster; it does not make the equilibrium composition more product-favored. This distinction matters because “more product appears sooner” is a kinetic observation, not evidence that K increased.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: compare the catalyzed and uncatalyzed pathways">

An uncatalyzed pathway has a forward activation energy of 80 kJ·mol⁻¹ and a reverse activation energy of 50 kJ·mol⁻¹. A catalyst provides a pathway that lowers both barriers by 25 kJ·mol⁻¹, giving 55 and 25 kJ·mol⁻¹. The difference between the barriers remains 30 kJ·mol⁻¹, consistent with unchanged ΔH. Both directions are faster, while the equilibrium constant remains the same at that temperature.

</WorkedExample>

A catalyst is regenerated by the mechanism and is not consumed in the net equation. In heterogeneous catalysis, reactants may adsorb to a surface, react at active sites, and desorb as products; blocking those sites can reduce the rate even if reactant concentrations remain unchanged.
