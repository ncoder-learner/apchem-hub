A reaction energy profile shows how the system's potential energy changes along a reaction pathway. It is a model of the energy barrier, not a graph of energy versus time. The horizontal axis represents reaction progress or a reaction coordinate, and the vertical axis represents potential energy.

<TopicLab unitId={5} topicNumber="5.6" title="Reaction Energy Profile" />

<LessonSection title="1. Read the diagram" description="Reactants, transition state, products, and energy changes" defaultOpen>

A typical single-step profile begins at the reactant energy, rises to a maximum, then falls to the product energy. The maximum corresponds to the transition-state region, a high-energy arrangement along the pathway.

**Activation energy for the forward reaction** is the energy difference between the reactants and the transition state. **Reaction enthalpy, ΔH**, is the energy difference between products and reactants in a constant-pressure enthalpy diagram:

**ΔH = H(products) − H(reactants)**

If products are lower in energy than reactants, ΔH is negative and the reaction is exothermic. If products are higher, ΔH is positive and the reaction is endothermic. The reverse activation energy is measured from the products up to the same transition state, so it differs from the forward activation energy when ΔH is not zero.

<QuickCheck question="On a potential-energy profile, how is the forward activation energy measured?" options={["From products to reactants","From reactants to the transition-state maximum","From reactants to products only","From the x-axis to the transition state"]} correctIndex={1} explanation="Activation energy is the energy barrier between the reactants and the transition-state maximum along the pathway." />

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="2. Activation energy and ΔH answer different questions" description="Rate versus energy difference between endpoints">

Activation energy is a kinetic barrier that affects how quickly reaction occurs. ΔH describes the energy difference between the initial and final states. A large negative ΔH does not guarantee a fast reaction: if the activation barrier is high, the reaction can be slow.

For a one-step profile, the forward and reverse barriers are related by the reaction enthalpy: **Eₐ,reverse = Eₐ,forward − ΔH**. If ΔH is negative, the reverse barrier is larger than the forward barrier by the magnitude of ΔH.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: calculate a reverse activation energy">
A reaction has a forward activation energy of 50 kJ/mol and ΔH = −20 kJ/mol.

Eₐ,reverse = Eₐ,forward − ΔH = 50 − (−20) = **70 kJ/mol**.

The products are 20 kJ/mol lower than the reactants, so the reverse reaction must climb 20 kJ/mol farther to reach the same transition state.
</WorkedExample>

<LessonSection title="3. How a catalyst changes the profile" description="Lower barrier, same starting and ending states">

A catalyst provides an alternative mechanism with a lower activation barrier. On a profile, the catalyzed pathway has a lower maximum relative to the same reactant and product energies. The catalyst does not change the overall ΔH because the initial and final chemical states remain the same.

Catalysis affects forward and reverse reaction rates and allows equilibrium to be reached faster. It does not change the equilibrium constant or the equilibrium composition at a fixed temperature. A catalyst is regenerated overall, although it may participate in intermediate steps.

<QuickCheck question="Which feature changes when a catalyst provides an alternative pathway for the same overall reaction?" options={["The energies of the initial reactants and final products must change","The activation barrier decreases while overall ΔH remains unchanged","The atoms are no longer conserved","The equilibrium constant increases at fixed temperature"]} correctIndex={1} explanation="A catalyst changes the pathway and lowers the activation barrier. It does not change the overall energy difference between the same reactants and products." />

For additional background, see [OpenStax Chemistry 2e: Reaction Mechanisms and Activation Energy](https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/12-5-collision-theory).

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="4. Read the energy profile quantitatively" description="Activation energy and enthalpy use different reference points">

For a one-step profile, \(E_a=E_{TS}-E_R\) for the forward direction and \(\Delta H=H_P-H_R\). The reverse activation energy is \(E_{a,rev}=E_{TS}-E_P\). Therefore, \(E_{a,fwd}-E_{a,rev}=\Delta H\). This relation is a useful consistency check: for an exothermic reaction, the forward activation barrier is smaller than the reverse barrier when both are measured from their respective starting levels.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: recover a missing energy">
Reactants are at 20 kJ·mol⁻¹, the transition state is at 95 kJ·mol⁻¹, and products are at −10 kJ·mol⁻¹. Then \(E_{a,fwd}=95-20=75\) kJ·mol⁻¹, \(\Delta H=-10-20=-30\) kJ·mol⁻¹, and \(E_{a,rev}=95-(-10)=105\) kJ·mol⁻¹. Check: 75 − 105 = −30.

</WorkedExample>

The transition state is a high-energy arrangement along the pathway, not an isolable intermediate. An intermediate is a local minimum between two transition-state peaks in a multistep profile. Do not label every peak an intermediate or every valley a transition state.
